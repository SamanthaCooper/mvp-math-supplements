What does a system's solution tell us?

A system of equations is a collection of equations that must all hold for the same variable values. For two line equations, their intersection is an ordered pair satisfying both. A graph can suggest its coordinates; algebra can find exact values, including fractions.

Main 1 — Compare the window-cleaning prices

Let \(n\) be the number of windows and \(C\) the cost in dollars. Sunshine Express charges a $50 service fee plus $10 per window; “Pane”less charges a $25 fee plus $15 per window:

\[C=50+10n\quad \text{(Sunshine Express)}.\]\[C=25+15n\quad \text{(“Pane”less)}.\]

The service fee is the starting cost, shown by each y-intercept. The per-window charge is the rate of increase, shown by each slope.

Equate two expressions for the same output

At the intersection, both companies charge the same \(C\) for the same \(n\). Therefore their cost expressions are equal.

Original problem: Determine which company costs less, supporting the recommendation with table, graph, and algebra.

Worked solution:

\[50+10n=25+15n.\]\[25=5n\quad \Longrightarrow\quad n=5.\]

Substitute \(n=5\) into either rule: \(C=50+10(5)=100\). Check the other rule: \(25+15(5)=100\). The exact intersection is \((5,100)\).

Recommendation: For 1–4 windows, choose “Pane”less; for 5 windows, the costs tie at $100; for 6 or more, choose Sunshine Express. The packet does not give the number of windows, so a recommendation must depend on that count.

Algebra also explains the comparison: Sunshine's cost minus “Pane”less's cost is \(25-5n\). It is positive below 5 windows, zero at 5, and negative above 5, matching the table and graph.

Equating expressions is substitution

If \(C=50+10n\), you may replace \(C\) in \(C=25+15n\) with \(50+10n\). That gives \(50+10n=25+15n\). Equal quantities can replace one another. Always recover the other variable and check both original equations.

Main 2 — Find the exact space/budget intersection

Let \(x\) count cats and \(y\) count dogs. Use equalities for the boundaries: using all the space gives \(6x+24y=360\), and spending all the budget gives \(32x+80y=1280\).

Original problem: Write the space and start-up-cost equations, then find their intersection algebraically.

Worked solution — equate the y-expressions:

\[y=-\frac14x+15.\]\[y=-\frac25x+16.\]\[-\frac14x+15=-\frac25x+16.\]

Multiply both sides by 20 to clear both fractions:

\[-5x+300=-8x+320.\]\[3x=20\quad \Longrightarrow\quad x=\frac{20}{3}.\]

Then \(y=-\frac14(\frac{20}{3})+15=\frac{40}{3}\). The intersection is

\[(x,y)=\left(\frac{20}{3},\frac{40}{3}\right).\]

Check in both original equations:
Space: \(6(\frac{20}{3})+24(\frac{40}{3})=360\).
Budget: \(32(\frac{20}{3})+80(\frac{40}{3})=1280\).

The same intersection by direct substitution

You do not have to isolate \(y\) in both equations. From the space equation, divide by 6 to get \(x+4y=60\), so \(x=60-4y\). Substitute that entire expression for \(x\) in the budget equation:

\[32(60-4y)+80y=1280.\]\[1920-48y=1280.\]\[-48y=-640\quad \Longrightarrow\quad y=\frac{40}{3}.\]

Now \(x=60-4(\frac{40}{3})=\frac{20}{3}\), the same exact answer. Parentheses ensure that 32 multiplies the entire expression replacing \(x\).

These fractional coordinates are a valid solution to the two line equations, but they are not actual pet counts. Do not round the intersection and claim the rounded point still satisfies both equalities. Whole-number pet choices must be checked against the original inequalities.

Main 3 — The two time boundaries

Original problem: Find the intersection of the feeding-time and pampering-time boundaries.

Worked solution: Their equations are \(12x+20y=480\) and \(16x+20y=480\). Solve each for \(y\) and equate:

\[-\frac35x+24=-\frac45x+24.\]

Multiplying both sides by 5 gives \(-3x+120=-4x+120\), so \(x=0\). Then \(y=24\), giving \((0,24)\).

Both checks give \(20(24)=480\). This is an intersection of the two time boundaries, but 24 dogs would require \(24(24)=576\) square feet and \(80(24)=1920\) dollars, exceeding the separate space and budget limits. An intersection of two boundaries is not automatically feasible for the entire business.

2. Ready — Check right-triangle side lengths

In your MVP packet

Section 5.7 · Ready · Problems 1–8 · p. 28

For a right triangle, the hypotenuse is the side opposite the right angle and is the longest side. The Pythagorean theorem says \(a^2+b^2=c^2\), where \(c\) is its length. Conversely, if three triangle side lengths satisfy this equation with the longest length as \(c\), the triangle is right.

Square the two shorter lengths, add, and compare with the square of the longest length. For example, the packet's \(5,12,13\) gives \(25+144=169\), which equals \(13^2\). Try each listed triple before checking the key.

3. Set — Substitute an expression into another equation

In your MVP packet

Section 5.7 · Set · Problems 9–15 · pp. 28–29

Substitution method
  1. Use an equation with a variable isolated, or rearrange one equation to isolate a variable.
  2. Replace that variable in the other equation with its equal expression. Use parentheses when a coefficient multiplies the expression.
  3. Solve the resulting one-variable equation.
  4. Substitute the value back to find the other variable.
  5. Write the ordered pair \((x,y)\) and check both original equations.

Set 9 — Replace y with x + 1

Original problem:

\[\begin{cases}y=x+1\\x+2y=8.\end{cases}\]

Worked solution: Since \(y=x+1\), substitute \(x+1\) for \(y\) in the second equation:

\[x+2(x+1)=8.\]\[3x+2=8\quad \Longrightarrow\quad x=2.\]

Then \(y=2+1=3\). Check: \(3=2+1\) and \(2+2(3)=8\). The answer is \((2,3)\).

Set 10 — Replace x instead

Original problem:

\[\begin{cases}y+2x=7\\x=3+y.\end{cases}\]

Worked solution: Substitute \(3+y\) for \(x\) in the first equation:

\[y+2(3+y)=7.\]\[3y+6=7\quad \Longrightarrow\quad y=\frac13.\]

Then \(x=3+\frac13=\frac{10}{3}\). Write x first in the ordered pair: \((\frac{10}{3},\frac13)\). Check: \(\frac13+2(\frac{10}{3})=7\) and \(\frac{10}{3}=3+\frac13\).

Try 11–15 using the same routine. For the word problem, choose variables for the two ticket counts and write one equation for count and one for money.

Check your work — Set 9–15

9–14 — Substitution and checks

  1. \( (2,3) \). Substitute \(x+1\): \(x+2(x+1)=8\). Checks: \(3=2+1\) and \(2+2(3)=8\).
  2. \((\frac{10}{3},\frac13)\). Substitute \(3+y\): \(y+2(3+y)=7\). Checks: \(\frac13+2(\frac{10}{3})=7\) and \(\frac{10}{3}=3+\frac13\).
  3. \((\frac{85}{11},-\frac7{11})\). \(3(9+2y)+5y=20\) gives \(27+11y=20\), so \(y=-\frac7{11}\) and \(x=\frac{85}{11}\). Checks: \(9+2(-\frac7{11})=\frac{85}{11}\) and \(3(\frac{85}{11})+5(-\frac7{11})=20\).
  4. \((1,-2)\). \(3(2x-4)+21x=15\) gives \(27x=27\). Checks: \(-2=2(1)-4\) and \(3(-2)+21(1)=15\).
  5. \((3,-2)\). \(3(-1-2y)+5y=-1\) gives \(-3-y=-1\). Checks: \(3=-1-2(-2)\) and \(3(3)+5(-2)=-1\).
  6. \((-\frac23,-\frac{13}{3})\). \(x+(2x-3)=-5\) gives \(3x=-2\). Checks: \(2(-\frac23)-3=-\frac{13}{3}\) and \(-\frac23-\frac{13}{3}=-5\).

15 — Concert tickets

Let \(a\) be advance tickets and \(d\) tickets at the door:

\[a+d=120.\]\[10a+15d=1390.\]

Substitute \(d=120-a\):

\[10a+15(120-a)=1390.\]\[-5a+1800=1390\quad \Longrightarrow\quad a=82.\]

82 tickets were purchased in advance; 38 were purchased at the door. Check: \(82+38=120\) and \(10(82)+15(38)=820+570=1390\).

4. Go — Inequalities and solution-set notation

In your MVP packet

Section 5.7 · Go · Problems 16–25 · p. 29

Use equivalent operations, reversing the inequality sign when multiplying or dividing by a negative number. For a refresher, see Math I 4.4. See Math I 4.2 for interval and set-builder notation.

Strict bounds use an open endpoint and a parenthesis in interval notation. Inclusive bounds use a closed endpoint and a bracket. Infinity always uses a parenthesis. A description such as \(\{x\in\mathbb R\mid x<2\}\) means all real x-values less than 2.

Go 18 — Clear the fraction early

Original problem: \(2(\frac{x}{4}+3)>6(x-1)\).

Worked solution: Distribute to get \(\frac{x}{2}+6>6x-6\). Multiply both sides by 2:

\[x+12>12x-12.\]\[24>11x\quad \Longrightarrow\quad x<\frac{24}{11}.\]

The interval is \((-\infty,\frac{24}{11})\).

Try the remaining exercises before checking the answers and number lines.

16–19 — Interval notation and number lines

16. \(x<2\); \((-\infty,2)\). Open endpoint at 2; shade left.

17. \(x>7\); \((7,\infty)\). Open endpoint at 7; shade right.

18. \(x<\frac{24}{11}\); \((-\infty,\frac{24}{11})\). Open endpoint at \(\frac{24}{11}\), approximately 2.18; shade left.

19. \(x\le-\frac5{11}\); \(\left(-\infty,-\frac5{11}\right] \). Closed endpoint at \(-\frac5{11}\), approximately −0.45; shade left. The original expression is \(9x+4\le-2(x+\frac12)\), which gives \(11x\le-5\).

20–25 — Set-builder notation

  1. \(\{x\in\mathbb R\mid x<\frac{10}{3}\}\). Multiplying \(-\frac{x}{3}>-\frac{10}{9}\) by \(-3\) reverses the sign.
  2. \(\{x\in\mathbb R\mid x<-9\}\).
  3. \(\{x\in\mathbb R\mid x>5\}\).
  4. \(\{x\in\mathbb R\}\) (all real numbers). \(3x-7\ge3(x-7)\) simplifies to \(-7\ge-21\), true for every real x.
  5. \(\{x\in\mathbb R\mid x>\frac{36}{5}\}\).
  6. \(\{x\in\mathbb R\mid x>-2\}\).
