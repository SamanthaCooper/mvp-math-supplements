Section 5.5 · Main task · Problems 1–10 · pp. 17–18

What does “all” mean?

A system of inequalities is a collection of inequalities that must hold simultaneously. One ordered pair is a solution only if it makes every inequality true. A combination that fits the budget but uses too much space cannot work for the business.

How to graph a system
  1. Draw each boundary line on the same axes. Use a solid line for \(\le\) or \(\ge\), and a dashed line for \(<\) or \(>\).
  2. Choose a point off the boundary and substitute it to decide which side satisfies each inequality.
  3. Keep only the common overlap of all those sides. Include a boundary point only when it also satisfies every other inequality.

Main 1 — Budget and space together

Let \(x\) count cats and \(y\) count dogs. The first two limits give

\[\begin{cases}32x+80y\le1280 & \text{budget}\\6x+24y\le360 & \text{space}.\end{cases}\]

The equivalent forms \(y\le-\frac25x+16\) and \(y\le-\frac14x+15\) show that both require shading below their own lines. Both boundaries are solid.

Main 4 — Keep the overlap

The budget boundary passes through \((0,16)\) and \((40,0)\). The space boundary passes through \((0,15)\) and \((60,0)\). The origin satisfies both, so keep the points on or below both lines. This is an overlap of regions, not just the point where the lines cross.

Main 5 — Add the two time limits

Eight hours is 480 minutes. Include feeding and pampering along with budget and space:

\[\begin{cases}32x+80y\le1280 & \text{budget}\\6x+24y\le360 & \text{space}\\12x+20y\le480 & \text{feeding}\\16x+20y\le480 & \text{pampering}.\end{cases}\]

The time boundaries are \(y=-\frac35x+24\) and \(y=-\frac45x+24\). Again, shade on and below each.

Main 8 — The unrestricted four-inequality solution set

Overlay the four lines and keep their common overlap. As algebraic inequalities alone, these allow some negative coordinates. For example, \((-5,0)\) satisfies all four, although a negative number of cats is not an actual boarding choice.

Main 9–10 — The feasible region for pet counts

A feasible combination satisfies every limit and makes sense in the situation. Add \(x\ge0\) and \(y\ge0\), so keep the part of the overlap in the first quadrant, including the axes. Actual cat and dog counts must also be whole numbers.

The corner labeled A is \((\frac{20}{3},\frac{40}{3})\), approximately \((6.67,13.33)\). It is where the budget and space boundaries cross. B is \((20,8)\), where budget and pampering meet. You can estimate these intersections on a graph and verify the displayed coordinates by substitution.

For example, check B: \(32(20)+80(8)=1280\), \(6(20)+24(8)=312\le360\), \(12(20)+20(8)=400\le480\), and \(16(20)+20(8)=480\). It satisfies all four limits.

The other corners are \((0,0)\), \((0,15)\), and \((30,0)\). Draw the solid edges connecting the corners around the region. A is useful for locating a boundary, but its fractional coordinates are not an actual pet combination.

Main 9 — Shading and actual animal counts

The packet calls the first-quadrant polygon the feasible region. Shading is a convenient picture of the limits, but only its whole-number coordinate points represent actual counts. The axes are included: boarding zero cats or zero dogs is allowed.

Main 10: The graph in 8 includes negative coordinates and extends indefinitely. The region in 9 restricts both coordinates to be nonnegative and is bounded. For the business, additionally select only its whole-number points.

Why does feeding not trim the feasible region further?

For nonnegative cat counts, \(12x+20y\le16x+20y\). Therefore any combination meeting the 480-minute pampering limit automatically meets the feeding limit. Feeding remains one of the stated checks, but it does not remove any additional feasible combinations.

Plots not shown. These checks specify the correct side and boundary; shade your packet graphs, correcting 3 as described above.

  1. \(y\le\frac15x-4\). At \((0,0)\), \(0\le-4\) is false. Shade below the solid line, away from the origin.
  2. \(y\ge-3x+5\). At \((0,0)\), \(0\ge5\) is false. Shade above the solid line, away from the origin.
  3. \(5x-2y\le10\), equivalently \(y\ge\frac52x-5\). At \((0,0)\), \(0\le10\) is true. Shade above the corrected solid line, toward the origin. Dividing \(-2y\le10-5x\) by \(-2\) reverses the inequality.
  4. \(3x+4y\ge24\), equivalently \(y\ge-\frac34x+6\). At \((0,0)\), \(0\ge24\) is false. Shade above the solid line, away from the origin.

3. Set — Write an inequality from its graph

In your MVP packet

Section 5.5 · Set · Problems 5–8 · p. 20

First read the boundary's slope and y-intercept to write \(y=mx+b\). Then use the shading: above the line means \(y>mx+b\), and below means \(y<mx+b\). Include equality for a solid boundary; use a strict inequality for a dashed boundary.

Set 6 — Read the boundary, then the shading

Original problem: Use graph 6 to write the inequality for its shaded region.

Worked solution: The dashed line passes through \((0,-4)\) and \((2,0)\). Its slope is \(\frac{0-(-4)}{2-0}=2\), so the boundary is \(y=2x-4\). The shaded side is above the line. Because the boundary is dashed, the answer is \(y>2x-4\). As a check, the shaded origin gives \(0>-4\), which is true.

Try reading the other three graphs before checking.

4. Go — Connect a rule, table, graph, and context

In your MVP packet

Section 5.5 · Go · Problems 9–12 · pp. 21–22

A proportional relationship has the form \(y=kx\), where \(k\) is the constant amount of output per unit of input. Its graph passes through \((0,0)\), and \(\frac{y}{x}=k\) for every nonzero input. For example, at $8 per day, four days cost \(8(4)=32\) dollars. The table, equation, and graph describe that same rate.

Packet error — Go 12 under “proportional relationships”

The given line does not pass through the origin: it has a positive starting amount of sand. It represents a linear relationship with a nonzero starting value, so it is not proportional. Describe the given graph rather than changing it to a line through the origin.

For a refresher on translating among tables, rules, graphs, and stories, see Math I 3.1. Try creating the missing representations before checking these sample answers.

Check your work — Go 9–12

9 — Days and cost

Equation: \(y=8x\), where \(x\) is days and \(y\) is cost in dollars.

Graph: Plot not shown. Use \((0,0)\), \((1,8)\), \((2,16)\), \((3,24)\), and \((4,32)\). Choose a vertical scale that reaches at least 32; label both axes.

Sample context: A rental costs $8 per day with no starting fee. If billed only in whole days, plot whole-day points.

Connection: Every cost is eight times its day count; the graph has slope 8 and passes through the origin.

10 — Claire's allowance

Equation: \(y=9x\), where \(x\) is the number of weeks and \(y\) is allowance earned in dollars.

Sample table: weeks \(0,1,2,3,4\) correspond to dollars \(0,9,18,27,36\).

Graph: Plot not shown. Plot those ordered pairs with a vertical scale reaching at least 36. Whole-week totals are discrete points.

Connection: Each additional week adds $9; the dollars-per-week ratio is 9 for nonzero weeks.

11 — \(y=3x\)

Sample table: inputs \(0,1,2,3,4\) give outputs \(0,3,6,9,12\).

Graph: Plot not shown. These points lie on the line through the origin with slope 3.

Sample context: Apples cost $3 per pound; \(x\) pounds cost \(y\) dollars. For this context, use nonnegative inputs, with fractional pounds allowed.

Connection: The table ratios, graph slope, and context's unit price all give 3.

12 — Sand decreasing over time

The graph's axes have no numerical labels. If each square is one unit on both axes, the intercepts are \((0,9)\) and \((9,0)\), giving \(y=9-x\). If a different scale is intended, the numerical rule changes; the graph alone does not specify those units.

Sample table under that scale: seconds \(0,1,3,6,9\) correspond to sand amounts \(9,8,6,3,0\).

Sample context: An hourglass starts with 9 grams of sand in its upper chamber and loses 1 gram per second until empty. The modeled time range is \(0\le x\le9\). “Grams” is a chosen unit for this sample story, not a unit printed in the packet.

Connection: The y-intercept gives the starting sand amount; the slope gives the decrease per second. The graph has a constant decrease, but a nonzero intercept, so it is not proportional. A replacement graph is not shown; use the given packet graph with your chosen scale labeled.
