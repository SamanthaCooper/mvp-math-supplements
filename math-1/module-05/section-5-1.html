In your MVP packetSection 5.1 · Main task · Pet-sitting planning question · p. 1

Represent the constraints

A constraint is a limit that a proposed choice must satisfy. Here the twins have limits on space and on the money available to buy pens and runs. Let x be the number of cats and y the number of dogs. The ordered pair \((x,y)\) records one possible boarding plan.

QuantityCatsDogsAvailable limit
Space per animal6 ft²24 ft²360 ft²
Pen/run purchase cost$32$80$1,280
Daily boarding income$8$20To be compared across plans

The two resource limits become:

\[6x+24y\le360\qquad\text{and}\qquad32x+80y\le1280\]

“Up to” includes the limit, so use \(\le\). The counts must also be nonnegative whole numbers. A system of inequalities is a collection of inequalities considered together; a solution must make all of them true.

Worked example 1 — Check the father’s suggestion

Original proposal: Board 12 cats and 12 dogs.

Worked solution: Substitute \(x=12\), \(y=12\) into both constraints:

CheckCalculationDoes it fit?
Space\(6(12)+24(12)=360\)Yes — exactly the available space.
Purchase cost\(32(12)+80(12)=1344\)No — $64 over the budget.

Meeting the space constraint alone is not enough. This plan cannot be funded with the $1,280 budget.

Worked example 2 — Compare plans that do fit

Sample alternatives: Compare an all-dog plan, an all-cat plan, and a mixed plan. These are examples of the kinds of choices the task asks you to investigate.

Plan (cats, dogs)SpacePurchase costDaily income
\((0,15)\)360 ft²$1,200$300
\((40,0)\)240 ft²$1,280$320
\((10,12)\)348 ft²$1,280$320

All three meet both resource limits. As in the packet, these income calculations assume all boarding spaces are filled. More dogs do not automatically mean more income: dogs also need more space and cost more to accommodate.

The boarding prices are exactly one quarter of the corresponding purchase costs. Therefore:

\[8x+20y=\frac14(32x+80y)\le\frac14(1280)=320\]

No affordable plan can bring in more than $320 per day. The mixed plan \((10,12)\) reaches that limit, so it is a valid best recommendation. It is not the only one: for example, \((20,8)\) also meets both constraints and earns $320 per day.

When giving your recommendation, state the counts, check both constraints, calculate the daily income, and explain why your choice is reasonable.

There are many possible solutions. The packing below is one example; other arrangements that fill the grid without gaps or overlaps are also valid.

One complete packing: 36e, with zero of a, b, c, and d. Shape e is a 2 × 2 square. Place six squares across each row of blocks and six rows of blocks down the grid.

This covers all \(12\times12=144\) unit squares, so no packing can cover more. Every supplied piece has area 4; \(36\times4=144\). The layout is not drawn here.
