Today's goal. Choose and explain domains for models of books, paper, and medicine; distinguish discrete inputs from counting in jumps; compare linear and exponential growth; and practice unit rates and one-step equations.
Important mathematical clarification — Discrete does not mean discontinuous

The MVP student packet and teacher's notes sometimes use discrete to describe separated input values, and at other times focus on quantities that change in jumps. Those are not the same property. This lesson states the input, output, and allowed inputs for each model, and uses red notes where a statement in the original materials could lead to a misleading conclusion.

A separate companion explanation examines the mathematical distinction and quotes the MVP student and teacher materials.

What makes a model discrete or continuous?

IN YOUR MVP PACKETSection 2.2 · Main task · pp. 6–10 · Problems 1–11
Every question in the main activity depends on identifying what the input is and which values are allowed.

A function matches each allowed input with exactly one output. Its domain is the set of allowed inputs. Before drawing a graph, decide what you are recording and at which inputs.

CLEAR DEFINITIONS — THREE DIFFERENT IDEAS

Discrete-domain function: a function defined at separated input values, such as . Its graph has individual dots at those input values.

Continuous function: a function defined throughout an interval whose output changes without jumps. Its graph is an unbroken curve over that interval.

Step function: a function whose value stays constant for stretches and jumps at particular inputs. A step function can be defined for every moment of time, but it is not a continuous function at its jumps.

Domain is not the same as range. A function may be defined at every instant (continuous-time domain) and still output only whole numbers.

In the next examples, a book count and the amount of data transferred will use different outputs. That choice matters more than the word “books” in the story.

Choose a model and explain it

When a problem asks whether a model is discrete or continuous, begin with the defined function, not whether the objects in the story can be counted.

Question to answerWhy it matters
What is the input?Number of boxes, completed cutting rounds, or elapsed time?

Which inputs are allowed?Only separated values, or every time in an interval?
Can the output jump?A full interval of inputs is not enough to guarantee a continuous function.

Worked comparison — Similarities can hide different domains

for box counts and for smooth shelving progress both have a linear form: a fixed rate times the input. But the first is defined only at whole-number box counts, while the second has a full interval of time inputs.

Likewise and both involve repeated multiplication, but the first uses discrete rounds and the second models continuous time.

For tables, list only allowed inputs. A continuous function may be shown in a table with a few samples, but a table containing only whole-number inputs does not prove the function's domain is discrete. For graphs, mark discrete inputs as separate dots. Draw smooth curves for continuous functions. Draw horizontal steps with correctly specified endpoints when representing a continuous-time integer count.

Plant A: inches per day. Plant B: inches per day. Plant A has the greater daily growth rate.

Worked example — Compare gallons per minute and miles per hour

A tank fills with 42 gallons in 6 minutes: gallons per minute. A second fills with 30 gallons in 5 minutes: gallons per minute. The first fills faster.

A cyclist riding 12 miles in 1.5 hours averages miles per hour. A runner covering 3 miles in 30 minutes takes 0.5 hour, giving miles per hour. The cyclist's rate is greater.

Watch the units: A rate of 3 miles in 24 minutes is not “3 miles per hour.” Convert 24 minutes into hours, or convert both comparisons into minutes.

MVP clarification — Set, Problems 5–10

Many descriptions give an average rate but do not state the input, output, or permitted inputs. They cannot always be classified uniquely as discrete or continuous. A rate stated “per day” or “per hour” does not force the input to be a whole number. State your proposed model and domain before assigning a label.

Likewise, one rate does not always establish that a quantity will follow that same linear or exponential rule indefinitely; the question may be inviting a simplifying constant-rate model. Make that assumption clear.

Teacher's review of the original packet

This review identifies substantive mathematical problems, not ordinary simplifying assumptions.

Main task, Problems 2–5 — What exactly is being downloaded or shelved?

The student packet asks students to model numbers of e-books downloaded and books shelved. The teacher's notes justify a connected graph of downloads on the grounds that fractions of a book are transferred during download, while acknowledging separately that completely shelved books cannot have fractional output values. Those descriptions switch between work/data progress and completed-item count without explicitly changing the output variable.

Why it matters: the definition of the output changes the graph: a smooth progress model and a running integer count are not the same function. Correction: specify whether each model counts completed books or measures transferred data/work, and state the domain.

Teacher notes, shelving-books discussion — Continuous time versus a continuous function

The notes correctly recognize that elapsed time can include fractional values while the number of shelved books remains whole. However, the same passage then treats this as a continuous function for simplicity. A whole-number step count defined at all times has a continuous interval of inputs but is not continuous at the jumps. If the intended model is the smooth rate , it should be labeled an approximation to book-equivalent work, not an exact completed-book count.

Correction: explain the modeling choice and do not use “continuous domain” and “continuous function” interchangeably.

Set, Problems 5–10 — Classifications that need missing modeling information

Descriptions such as a quantity “per day” often do not identify the independent and dependent variables or their domains. The form of change (fixed addition or fixed percentage) can sometimes be inferred, but the discrete/continuous classification is not determined uniquely. Problem 10 specifies a surface flow speed without defining a distance or position function.

Correction: state the intended input, output, domain, and assumption of steady rate. Alternatively, require students to propose and justify a model rather than grade one implicit answer.

Set, Problem 11 — Domains must accompany equations

A recurrence with index naturally defines a discrete sequence and a dot graph displays separated plotted inputs, but an explicit rule does not establish a domain by itself. A letter such as or is a convention, not a mathematical domain definition. Correction: give the intended domain for each representation or explicitly ask students to choose one.
