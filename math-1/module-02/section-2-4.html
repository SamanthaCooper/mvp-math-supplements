Packet clarification — Main task, Part c (investment choice)

The exercise asks which company you would choose to invest in. Projected net income alone is not enough to decide which investment is better. The exercise can be answered as a comparison of the two growth models; a real investment decision would also depend on stock price, risks, whether the forecasts come true, and many other factors.

Choose what the model tells you between years

IN YOUR MVP PACKETMain task · p. 23 · Part e
Explain when discrete yearly forecasts or a continuous-time approximation is useful.

Two models can use the same formula but have different domains (permitted inputs).

Model choiceDomain and meaningGraph
Yearly snapshotst=0,1,2,3,...; the model provides a value only at each annual checkpointIsolated points
Smooth projectionEvery real time t≥0; the model estimates values between annual checkpointsConnected line or curve

A business's actual yearly net income is normally determined over a reporting period, not smoothly earned at exactly the predicted rate. That doesn't prevent a smooth curve from being a useful approximation for planning. A discrete-year model avoids suggesting we know the precise income at every instant.

Clarifying MVP — Main task, Part e

Discrete does not mean “outputs are whole numbers.” Net income can take decimal values in millions of dollars, even if we calculate it only at whole-number years. The discrete or continuous domain depends on which input times our model permits. A smooth formula is not evidence that actual business income changes smoothly at every instant.

MVP clarification — Section 2.4, Ready, Problems 1–3

The worked example uses a positive common ratio, but Problems 1–3 do not restrict the sign of the ratio. Both positive and negative common ratios give valid geometric sequences for these problems.

Module 1, Section 1.10 (“Geometric Meanies”) explicitly teaches students to consider whether geometric sequences can have more than one solution. That applies here, too.

For example, in Problem 1, both 3, 6, 12, 24, 48 and 3, −6, 12, −24, 48 are valid geometric sequences. Their common ratios are 2 and −2, respectively.

Both sequences satisfy the given first and fifth terms. The packet does not say that only positive ratios are allowed.

Teacher's Review of the Original MVP Packet

The central business-comparison task has a sound mathematical purpose, and the teacher's manual explicitly invites either discrete-year or smooth modeling assumptions. The following are the issues that warrant clarification.

Set, Problem 9 — “Always” needs a domain convention

The prompt treats a sequence domain as though it must be the entire set of natural numbers. But finite sequences, zero-indexed sequences, and other integer-indexed sequences are legitimate. A more precise statement is that an arithmetic or geometric sequence has an integer-indexed domain (often consecutive nonnegative or positive integers), a constant difference or ratio, and an appropriate recursive description.

Go, Problems 16–21 — Reading graphs and extending the model

Students must infer grid scales where numerical labels are not shown, and the optional Problem 21 is a curve crossing the x-axis, unlike a basic unshifted exponential. The worksheet should state “one grid square represents one unit” where intended and clearly label Problem 21 as requiring a different, shifted exponential model. The bonus designation appropriately signals extra challenge, but it does not specify the new function form.
