What makes a polygon regular?

A regular polygon has all its sides the same length and all its corner angles the same size. Its vertices are evenly spaced around a circle. Its center is the center of that circle, where its mirror lines meet.

The letter n will stand for the number of sides. For example, a regular hexagon has \(n=6\). For a refresher on line and rotational symmetry, see Section 6.5.

Worked example 1 — Find the hexagon’s rotation angles

Original problem — Main 4: Describe the rotations and reflections that carry a regular hexagon onto itself.

Worked solution — rotations: Join the center O to each vertex. The six equal gaps around O make a full 360° turn, so each gap has angle \(360°\div6=60°\).

A 60° turn moves every vertex to the next vertex, making the whole figure match. Two such steps give 120°, three give 180°, and so on. The positive matching turns up to a full turn are 60°, 120°, 180°, 240°, 300°, 360°.

The rotation rule

For a regular n-sided polygon, the smallest positive matching turn is \(\frac{360°}{n}\). Every whole-number multiple of that angle also works. Rotate about the polygon’s center.

How to find all the mirror lines

If n is odd, each mirror line passes through one vertex and the midpoint of the opposite side. There are n vertices, giving n different mirror lines.

If n is even, there are two kinds: \(\frac n2\) lines through opposite vertices and \(\frac n2\) lines through opposite-side midpoints. Their total is n.

Reflecting across any of these lines pairs up the vertices on opposite sides, preserving the equally spaced pattern. The entire polygon matches itself.

3. Set — Count mirrors and diagonals; identify rotation angles

In your MVP packetSection 6.6 · Set · Problems 4–10 · pp. 33–34

Count diagonals without counting any twice

For Set 4, use the symmetry rule above. Set 5 introduces a different count: a diagonal joins nonadjacent vertices.

From one vertex, you can connect to n − 3 vertices: exclude the vertex itself and its two neighbors. Doing this from all n vertices gives n(n − 3) connections, but every diagonal was counted from both ends. Divide by 2:

\[\text{Number of diagonals}=\frac{n(n-3)}2\]

For the pentagon, the count is \(\frac{5(5-3)}2=5\). Unlike mirror lines, most diagonals are not lines of symmetry.

For 9–10, watch the wording: An “angle of rotation” does not necessarily mean the smallest matching angle. More than one regular polygon can satisfy each question.

9 — A regular polygon with a 180° matching turn

Any even number of sides: 4, 6, 8, 10, … . A half-turn sends each vertex to the vertex halfway around the polygon. For that opposite vertex to exist, the number of vertices must be even.

10 — A regular polygon with a 200° matching turn

One valid answer is 9 sides and 9 mirror lines. A nonagon’s smallest turn is 40°, and \(200°=5\times40°\).

The answer is not unique: 18 sides also works, since \(200°=10\times20°\). In general, 9, 18, 27, … sides work, with that same number of mirror lines.

Teacher’s review of the source packet

Set 9–10, p. 34: A matching angle does not determine a unique side count. Set 9 permits any even number of sides; Set 10 permits any positive multiple of 9. If one answer is intended, ask for the fewest sides.

Teacher edition, Discuss for 6.6 (PDF p. 60): Locate the center using the intersecting mirror lines or the circle through the vertices, rather than saying it is “the point of intersection of the diagonals.” A triangle has no diagonals, and a polygon’s diagonals need not all meet at its center.
