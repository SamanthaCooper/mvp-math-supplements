Definitions that describe exactly what moves

The pre-image is the original figure; the image is the result of a transformation. Here are sample completions for the definitions in Part 1.

1 — Translation

A translation moves every point of the pre-image the same distance in the same direction. The paths from points to their images lie along parallel lines. For example, translating 3 units right and 2 units down sends \((x,y)\) to \((x+3,y-2)\).

2 — Rotation

A rotation turns every point through the same angle, in the same direction, about a fixed center of rotation. Each point and its image are equidistant from that center: they are the same distance away.

A circle consists of points at a fixed distance from its center. Each moving point follows a circular arc. These circles are concentric, meaning they share the same center, even when their radii differ. The center itself stays fixed. A complete turn is 360°; 90° is a quarter-turn and 180° is a half-turn.

3 — Reflection

A reflection across a line sends each point to the opposite side so that the line is the perpendicular bisector of the segment joining the point and its image. That means the line crosses the segment at a right angle, halfway between its endpoints. Points on the mirror line stay fixed.

4 — Why these are rigid motions

Translations, rotations, and reflections are rigid-motion transformations because they preserve distances and angle measures within a figure. The image therefore has the same size and shape as the pre-image.

How two reflections can make a rotation

Reflect across the first line, then reflect the resulting image across the second line. When the mirror lines intersect, this composition is a rotation about their intersection. The rotation angle is twice the smaller angle between the mirror lines. Follow the original point to its final image to determine the direction of the turn.

Worked example — Check two reflections using the packet’s mirror lines

Original problem — Part 2: Explain why the February 29 image is a reflection of the February 28 image, why the March 1 image is a reflection of February 29, and why the two reflections together make a rotation.

Worked solution: The first mirror line is \(y=-2x+48\); the second is \(y=2x-24\). They meet at \(C=(18,12)\). The packet’s house artwork is approximate. The constructed outline below shows exact reflections across those same mirror lines.

First reflection: check perpendicularity and equal distances

The marked point moves from \(P=(10,18)\) to \(P'=(18,22)\). Its midpoint is \((14,20)\), which lies on the first mirror line because \(20=-2(14)+48\). The connecting segment has slope \(\frac{22-18}{18-10}=\frac12\), perpendicular to the mirror line’s slope −2.

The mirror therefore crosses PP′ at a right angle and at its midpoint. That is precisely the definition of reflection. The outline’s other vertices obey the same rule.

Second reflection: apply the same check

Now \(P'=(18,22)\) moves to \(P''=(26,18)\). Their midpoint is \((22,20)\), on the second mirror line. The segment has slope \(-\frac12\), perpendicular to the second mirror line’s slope 2. Again, the line is a perpendicular bisector.

Combined motion: locate the center and the turn

The first and final marked points are both 10 units from \(C=(18,12)\): their horizontal/vertical changes from C are \((-8,6)\) and \((8,6)\). The point turns clockwise around C.

A protractor gives about 53° for the smaller angle between the mirror lines, so the combined rotation is about 106° clockwise. Every vertex undergoes that same turn about C. In the packet, look for these relationships between corresponding corners of the house; use the constructed outline above for exact numerical checks.

3. Set — Rotate about a center and compose reflections

In your MVP packetSection 6.4 · Set · Problems 7–11 · pp. 22–23

Worked example — Set 7: rotate about C, not the origin

Original problem: Rotate P 90° clockwise about C to get P′. Also rotate the original P 180° about C to get P″.

Worked solution: Read \(C=(-2,1)\) and \(P=(-6,2)\). Relative to C, P is 4 units left and 1 unit up: displacement \((-4,1)\).

A clockwise quarter-turn changes a displacement \((u,v)\) to \((v,-u)\). Here that gives \((1,4)\): 1 right and 4 up from C, so \(P'=(-1,5)\).

A half-turn reverses both displacement components, giving \((4,-1)\): 4 right and 1 down from C, so \(P''=(2,0)\).

For 9–11: find each mirror line, then the rotation

The first mirror line is the perpendicular bisector of PP′; the second is the perpendicular bisector of P′P″. Find each midpoint, then use a perpendicular slope to write the line through it. For a refresher, see Section 6.3’s mirror-line examples.

The two lines’ intersection is the rotation center. Measure the smaller angle between the lines and double it; follow P to P″ to determine clockwise or counterclockwise. In 10 and 11, the angles are approximate measurements, not exact whole numbers of degrees.
