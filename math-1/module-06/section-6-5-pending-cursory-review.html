In your MVP packetSection 6.5 · Main task · Quadrilateral chart and trapezoid investigation · pp. 25–26

What does it mean for a figure to have symmetry?

A figure has a line of symmetry when reflecting it across that line places the whole figure exactly on top of its original position. Imagine folding along the line: the two parts must match.

A figure has rotational symmetry when a turn smaller than 360° places it exactly on top of itself. The vertices may exchange places; what matters is that the whole figure matches. The fixed point used for the turn is its center of rotation.

A full 360° turn works for every figure. The turns listed below are the positive turns smaller than 360° that reveal rotational symmetry.

A diagonal connects two nonadjacent vertices (corners). Some diagonals are mirror lines, but many are not.

Worked example 1 — The rectangle in the packet’s chart

Original problem: Find its lines of symmetry and its center and angles of rotation.

Worked solution: The rectangle shown is wider than it is tall. A line through the midpoints of the top and bottom sides swaps its left and right sides. A line through the midpoints of the left and right sides swaps its top and bottom sides. Both reflections make the figure match itself.

A 180° turn about O sends each corner to the opposite corner, so the rectangle matches. A 90° turn puts its longer sides where the shorter sides were, so it does not match. Its diagonals are not mirror lines: a reflection across a diagonal would need to swap adjacent sides of different lengths.

Compare the parallelogram, rhombus, and square

A parallelogram has both pairs of opposite sides parallel. A half-turn about the intersection of its diagonals exchanges opposite corners and sides, so it matches itself. The slanted parallelogram in the packet has no mirror lines.

In a rhombus, all four sides have the same length. Each diagonal divides it into matching halves and acts as a mirror line. The rhombus shown also matches after a half-turn.

A square has four equal sides and four right angles. It has the rectangle’s two midpoint mirror lines and the rhombus’s two diagonal mirror lines. Its equal sides allow a quarter-turn as well as a half-turn.

The extra properties matter: a square is also a rectangle and a rhombus, and both rectangles and rhombuses are parallelograms. Use the symmetry of the actual figure, rather than assuming every parallelogram has only the symmetries of the slanted example.

Worked example 2 — Find a trapezoid that does have a mirror line

Original problem — p. 26: Draw a trapezoid, test its symmetries, and try another if the first has none.

Worked solution: Here a trapezoid has exactly one pair of opposite sides parallel. A lopsided trapezoid may have no mirror line. If you draw an isosceles trapezoid, with equal-length nonparallel sides, the line through the midpoints of its two bases is a mirror line.

Neither of these trapezoids matches after a half-turn: that would put the longer base where the shorter base was. The isosceles example has line symmetry, but no rotational symmetry from a turn smaller than 360°.

3. Set — Compare mirror lines and diagonals; explore kites

In your MVP packetSection 6.5 · Set · Problems 3–8 · pp. 27–28

What is a kite?

A kite is a quadrilateral with two pairs of adjacent congruent sides. In the ordinary kite below, the two pairs have different lengths: AB = AD and BC = CD. We use this non-rhombus kite for the examples.

Reflection across AC fixes A and C and exchanges B and D, so the figure matches. Reflection across BD would send A to a point below C, so it fails. A half-turn also fails: the top and bottom portions do not match.

3 — Definition and sketch

A quadrilateral with two pairs of adjacent congruent sides. In the example above, AB = AD and BC = CD; draw a similar kite.

4 — Mirror lines and diagonals

The ordinary kite has one mirror line, along AC. Its two diagonals are AC and BD; only AC is a mirror line.

5 — Rotational symmetry

For the ordinary kite shown: 360° only; no positive turn smaller than 360° matches it. If your definition of kite allows a rhombus and you drew one, its 180° turn also works; a square also allows 90° and 270°.

6 — First occurrence

Are lines of symmetry also diagonals in a polygon? Explain.

Not always. A rectangle’s mirror lines pass through side midpoints, rather than opposite vertices. A square’s diagonal mirror lines show that some lines of symmetry do lie along diagonals.

6 — Second occurrence

Are all diagonals also lines of symmetry in a polygon? Explain.

No. A non-square rectangle has two diagonals, but neither is a mirror line: reflecting across one would need to exchange adjacent sides of different lengths.

7 — Examples to name and draw

A non-square rectangle and a slanted parallelogram that is not a rhombus both have diagonals that are not mirror lines. Draw examples like those above and connect their opposite vertices.

8 — Parallelograms

Some do, but not all. A rhombus is a parallelogram whose two diagonals are mirror lines; this includes a square. The general slanted parallelogram shown above has neither diagonal as a mirror line. A diagonal reflection would swap adjacent sides, so it cannot work when those sides have unequal lengths.
