1. In class: A Solidify Understanding Task

Revisit the ideas from class

This section explains some of the ideas you explored in class through the guided exercises, in a more formal, step-by-step way. Use it alongside the “A Solidify Understanding Task” portion of Section 3.2 in your MVP packet.

Inputs, outputs, and features of a function

In your MVP packetSection 3.2 · Main task · pp. 6–7 · Problems 1–3

Identify the quantities and use domain, range, direction, extremes, and intercepts to describe them.

A function assigns exactly one output to each allowed input. In a river trip, time is the input. At each time, the depth of the water at the floaters’ location is one possible output; total distance traveled is another. These are two different functions, even though both use time.

Read an ordered pair in input–output order. For a depth function, \((20,8)\) means that at 20 minutes the water was 8 feet deep. For a distance function, \((20,2000)\) would mean 2,000 feet traveled after 20 minutes. The second pair is an invented example, not a reading from Maria’s graph.

Domain and range answer different questions

The domain is the set of allowed input values. The range is the set of output values the function actually produces. On a graph, look horizontally for the domain and vertically for the range.

Worked example 1 — A restricted trip

A walker travels steadily at 200 feet per minute for 15 minutes, beginning at distance zero. Here time can be any real number from 0 to 15 minutes, including fractional times.

The domain is \([0,15]\). Distance begins at 0 feet and ends at \(200(15)=3000\) feet. Because the walker moves continuously through all intermediate distances, the range is \([0,3000]\).

The square brackets include both endpoints. The interval \([0,15]\) means \(0\le t\le15\), not just the two values 0 and 15. The function’s formula could produce a number when \(t=20\), but 20 minutes is outside this particular trip’s domain.

Changing output and attained extremes

A function is increasing on an interval when later inputs give larger outputs, decreasing when later inputs give smaller outputs, and constant when the output stays the same. Read these changes from left to right. An increasing function can have negative outputs: rising from −8 to −3 is an increase.

A maximum value is the greatest output actually reached anywhere in the domain; a minimum value is the least output actually reached. Distinguish the value from its location: “the maximum is 12 feet” gives an output; “it occurs at 90 minutes” gives an input. More than one input may attain the same maximum or minimum.

An x-intercept is a point on the graph where the output is zero; a y-intercept is a point where the input is zero. Write intercepts as ordered pairs. A graph need not have either kind of intercept, and the origin \((0,0)\) can be both.

Read the river-depth table

In your MVP packetSection 3.2 · Main task · pp. 6–7 · Problem 1; Problem 3(a), (f), (h), (j), (k), (m)

Use Alonzo’s table to describe depth, direction, domain, range, and intercepts.

Alonzo’s table on page 6 shows the water depth every 10 minutes. To identify an increase or decrease, compare the depths at successive times.

Example. From 30 to 40 minutes, depth falls from 10 to 6 feet—a decrease of 4 feet: \(6-10=-4\).

The depth increases from 0 to 30 minutes, decreases from 30 to 60, increases from 60 to 90, and decreases from 90 to 120. The greatest depth is 12 feet at 90 minutes, and the least is 4 feet at both 0 and 60 minutes.

The trip lasts from 0 to 120 minutes, so the domain is \([0,120]\). Depth ranges from 4 to 12 feet, so the range is \([4,12]\). These intervals include the times and depths between the listed measurements: a table can show selected points from a continuous relationship.

At time zero, depth is 4 feet, giving the y-intercept \((0,4)\). The depth stays above zero, so there is no x-intercept. Remember that domain and direction intervals use times, while the range and maximum or minimum values use depths.

Worked example 2 — A value between measurements

The water is 7 feet deep at 80 minutes and 12 feet deep at 90 minutes. Did it reach 11 feet even though 11 does not appear in the table?

Yes. As the depth changes continuously from 7 to 12 feet, it passes through 11 feet. It also passes through 11 again while dropping from 12 feet at 90 minutes to 9 feet at 100 minutes. The table does not give the exact times.

A range is not just the starting and ending outputs. The trip begins at a depth of 4 feet and ends at 5 feet, but its range includes the deeper water encountered along the way.

Total distance is different from speed

In your MVP packetSection 3.2 · Main task · pp. 6–7 · Problem 2; Problem 3(b)–(e), (g), (i), (l)

Interpret Maria’s distance graph and compare distance gained over equal time intervals.

Total distance traveled accumulates as a person moves. It increases while the person is moving and stays constant during a stop. It does not decrease merely because the person slows down. Maria’s distance graph rises throughout the displayed trip, although its steepness changes.

Her horizontal axis is time in minutes and her vertical axis is accumulated distance in feet. For the displayed two-hour trip, use the time domain \([0,120]\). The origin represents zero distance traveled at the start, so it is both an x-intercept and a y-intercept. The least distance is 0, attained at the start, and the greatest is attained at the end of this restricted trip.

The graph starts at zero distance and ends at about 16,000 feet, giving a range of approximately \([0,16000]\) feet. Read the range from the graph’s lowest and highest outputs.

Compare changes over equal time intervals

The steepness of a distance graph shows how quickly distance is accumulating. Over an interval, average speed is distance gained divided by time elapsed:

\[\text{average speed}=\frac{\text{distance gained}}{\text{time elapsed}}.\]

Worked example 3 — Higher on the graph does not mean faster

In an invented trip, distance rises from 800 to 2,000 feet between minutes 10 and 20. Later it rises from 6,000 to 6,900 feet between minutes 50 and 60.

\[\begin{aligned}\text{First interval: }&\frac{2000-800}{20-10}=120\text{ ft/min},\\\text{Later interval: }&\frac{6900-6000}{60-50}=90\text{ ft/min}.\end{aligned}\]

The later portion lies higher because more distance has accumulated, but the person travels more slowly during that interval: 900 feet rather than 1,200 feet in the same 10 minutes. A shallower upward curve still means increasing total distance.

Packet clarification — Main task 3(d): “during the last ten minutes”

Interpret this as the additional distance covered within that interval, not the accumulated distance at its end. Compare the vertical increases across 10-minute spans. The final endpoint being highest does not establish that the last 10 minutes covered the most distance; an earlier part of Maria’s curve is steeper.

Both depth and total distance are functions of time: each time has one value of each quantity. Different times can produce the same depth without violating the function rule. The two outputs have different behavior: depth can increase and decrease, while accumulated distance cannot decrease.

2. Ready — Solve systems by graphing

In your MVP packetSection 3.2 · Ready · p. 8 · Problems 1–6

Graph each pair of lines and identify the point that satisfies both equations.

A system of equations asks for values that make two equations true at once. When both graphs are drawn on the same coordinate plane, a common point belongs to both. Its ordered pair is a solution of the system.

For a line in the form \(y=mx+b\), plot the y-intercept \((0,b)\), then use the slope \(m=\frac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}}\) to find another point. Draw each straight line through its points. Keep the same axis scales for both graphs; otherwise their apparent intersection is meaningless.

Worked example 4 — Locate and check an intersection

Solve by graphing \(f(x)=x+1\) and \(g(x)=-2x+7\).

  1. For the first line, start at \((0,1)\). Slope 1 means right 1 and up 1, giving \((1,2)\) and \((2,3)\).
  2. For the second line, start at \((0,7)\). Slope −2 means right 1 and down 2, giving \((1,5)\) and \((2,3)\).
  3. Draw both lines. Their intersection is \((2,3)\), so the common input is 2 and the common output is 3.
  4. Check the point in both formulas: \(f(2)=2+1=3\) and \(g(2)=-2(2)+7=3\). Both equal the proposed y-coordinate.

Report the whole ordered pair \(\boxed{(2,3)}\). Writing only “2” omits the output coordinate. Substitution here checks the solution read from the graph.

Worked example 5 — A fractional slope

Solve by graphing \(f(x)=\frac12x-1\) and \(g(x)=-x+5\).

The first line begins at \((0,-1)\). Its slope \(\frac12\) means right 2, up 1, so it also contains \((2,0)\) and \((4,1)\). The second begins at \((0,5)\); right 1, down 1 gives points such as \((2,3)\) and \((4,1)\). Drawing the two lines shows the intersection \((4,1)\).

\[\begin{aligned}f(4)&=\tfrac12(4)-1=1,\\g(4)&=-4+5=1.\end{aligned}\]

Thus \(\boxed{(4,1)}\) works for both lines. For packet slopes such as \(\frac23\) and \(-\frac13\), use a horizontal step of 3 units and the corresponding vertical rise of 2 or −1 units.

Some packet intersections have negative coordinates. Extend your grid below or left of the axes as needed; choose a window that contains both y-intercepts and the crossing. A crossing that falls between grid marks may require an estimate, which you should label and check. All six Ready pairs have different slopes, so their complete lines have exactly one intersection each.

3. Set — Describe intervals, endpoints, and extremes

In your MVP packetSection 3.2 · Set · p. 9 · Problems 7–9

Use interval notation to describe direction, domain, and range, and identify attained maximum and minimum values.

How interval notation works

An interval includes every real number between its boundaries. A square bracket includes a boundary value; a parenthesis excludes it. A filled endpoint belongs to a graph, while an open circle does not.

NotationMeaning
\([-3,5]\)\(-3\le x\le5\): both endpoints included
\((-3,5]\)\(-3<x\le5\): exclude −3, include 5
\((2,\infty)\)\(x>2\): all real numbers greater than 2
\([-4,-1]\cup[2,6]\)Two intervals, with a gap between them; \(\cup\) means “union,” or combine the sets

Infinity is not an endpoint you can reach, so always place a parenthesis beside \(\infty\) or \(-\infty\). Also distinguish an interval \((2,5)\) from an ordered pair \((2,5)\): in a domain or range statement it describes all values strictly between 2 and 5; in a point statement it gives one input and one output.

Worked example 6 — A graph with completely specified endpoints

The following original graph has an open circle at \((-3,1)\), then straight segments through \((0,4)\), \((2,4)\), and a filled endpoint at \((5,-2)\). There is no continuation beyond its two ends.

  1. Domain: every input after −3 through 5 appears, so \(\boxed{(-3,5]}\). The open circle excludes the input −3.
  2. Range: the graph reaches every output from −2 through 4, including both, so \(\boxed{[-2,4]}\). Excluding one point at height 1 does not exclude height 1 from the range: the descending segment reaches it again.
  3. Direction: increasing on \((-3,0)\), constant on \((0,2)\), and decreasing on \((2,5)\). These describe input intervals.
  4. Extremes: the maximum output is 4, attained for every \(x\) in \([0,2]\). The minimum output is −2, attained at \(x=5\).
  5. Intercepts: \((0,4)\) is the y-intercept. On the descending segment, y decreases by 6 while x increases by 3, so the slope is −2. Moving 2 units right from \((2,4)\) decreases y by 4 to zero; the x-intercept is \((4,0)\).

Disconnected domains and outputs that overlap

If a graph contains separate pieces with gaps between their input intervals, list the domain as a union. For instance, a graph defined only from −4 to −1 and from 2 to 6, with filled endpoints, has domain \([-4,-1]\cup[2,6]\). It has no input values in the gap \((-1,2)\).

The range may nevertheless be one continuous interval if the pieces’ output values overlap. To find it, project all the pieces onto the vertical axis. Do not copy the domain’s gaps into the range. In Set 7, the separate pieces have filled endpoints: read each x-interval separately, then check which y-values the combined pieces actually reach.

A boundary may be approached without being attained

Worked example 7 — No smallest output

Consider the continuous graph of \(q(x)=2+6(\frac12)^x\), restricted to \(x\ge0\). At \(x=0\), the output is \(2+6=8\); at \(x=1\), it is 5; at \(x=2\), it is 3.5. As x increases, the positive term \(6(\frac12)^x\) becomes smaller, so q decreases toward 2 while staying above it.

The domain is \([0,\infty)\), and the range is \((2,8]\). The maximum is 8, attained at \(x=0\). There is no minimum: 2 is never reached, and every attained output above 2 is followed by a smaller one. Writing “minimum = 2” would confuse an approached boundary with an attained value.

In Set 8, read the curve as continuing beyond the picture and approaching height 3 without reaching it. It decreases throughout, has domain \((-\infty,\infty)\) and range \((3,\infty)\), and has neither a maximum nor a minimum.

In Set 9, read the outer curves as continuing beyond the picture. The function rises to a flat top, stays constant from \(x=-2\) to \(x=5\), then decreases. Its maximum is 2, reached throughout \([-2,5]\). The graph has domain \((-\infty,\infty)\) and range \((-\infty,2]\), with no minimum.

For each Set graph, separate four answers: direction on input intervals; greatest and least attained outputs; domain; and range. For a flat highest piece, give the maximum value and the whole interval where it occurs. Do not choose just one point on the plateau.

4. Go — Explicit and recursive sequence rules

In your MVP packetSection 3.2 · Go · p. 10 · Problems 10–15

Write arithmetic and geometric sequence rules in explicit and recursive form, including tables with skipped indices.

A sequence is an ordered list of terms. Its input n is a term index, and \(f(n)\) is the term’s value. The index may begin at 0 or at 1; check the table rather than assuming. An arithmetic or geometric sequence has integer indices, even when its terms are negative or fractional. Its domain is a set such as \(\{0,1,2,\ldots\}\), not the continuous interval \([0,\infty)\).

An explicit rule calculates a term directly from n. A recursive rule calculates it from the preceding term, so it must give a starting value as well as the step rule. The notation \(f(n-1)\) means the term at the previous index; it does not mean \(f(n)-1\).

Arithmetic sequences: add the same amount per step

An arithmetic sequence has a constant common difference d: each increase of 1 in the index adds d to the output. For values 7, 10, 13 at indices 1, 2, 3, both differences are 3. Starting from the first term, the terms are:

\[\begin{aligned}f(1)&=7,\\ f(2)&=7+3,\\ f(3)&=7+2(3).\end{aligned}\]

Index n is \(n-1\) steps after index 1. Therefore the explicit rule is \(f(n)=7+3(n-1)=3n+4\), for integer \(n\ge1\). The recursive rule gives the first term and adds 3 at every later step:

\[\begin{aligned}f(1)&=7,\\ f(n)&=f(n-1)+3.\end{aligned}\]

The step rule applies for integers \(n\ge2\).

After the example: the general arithmetic rule

If the sequence begins at index 1, \(f(n)=f(1)+d(n-1)\). If it begins at index 0, \(f(n)=f(0)+dn\). The difference is the number of steps from the chosen starting index, not a different mathematical method.

Negative d makes successive terms smaller. When inputs skip indices, divide the output change by the number of index steps. A jump from index 2 to index 5 covers three steps, not one.

Geometric sequences: multiply by the same factor per step

A geometric sequence multiplies by a fixed common ratio r when the index increases by 1. For nonzero neighboring terms, divide the later term by the earlier one. Values 4, 12, 36 at indices 1, 2, 3 have ratios \(12\div4=3\) and \(36\div12=3\).

Starting at index 1, the terms are \(4\), \(4(3)\), \(4(3)(3)\), and so on. There are \(n-1\) multiplying steps to reach index n, giving:

\[f(n)=4\cdot3^{n-1}\quad(n=1,2,3,\ldots).\]

The corresponding recursive rule is \(f(1)=4\), followed by \(f(n)=3f(n-1)\) for integers \(n\ge2\). If the starting index were 0, the exponent would instead count n multiplying steps.

After the example: the general geometric rule

Starting at index 1: \(f(n)=f(1)r^{n-1}\). Starting at index 0: \(f(n)=f(0)r^n\). In recursive form, specify the starting term and use \(f(n)=r f(n-1)\) at each later integer index.

For a gap of two indices, the output is multiplied by \(r^2\), not just r. For a gap of five indices it is multiplied by \(r^5\). A multiplying factor larger than 1 can make negative terms more negative, so it does not automatically mean the terms are increasing.

Go reminder — Check the indices

The exercise is a review of arithmetic and geometric rules, so look for a consistent constant difference or constant ratio per one index step.

In 12–14, the printed indices skip values. In 12 and 15, the first listed index is 0. In 14, you may anchor the recursive rule at the first supplied index, 2, and give its valid later indices; do not silently label that supplied term as \(f(1)\).

5. Additional Go problems — Complete worked solutions

In your MVP packetSection 3.2 · Go · p. 10 · Problems 10–15

Practice the same sequence skills with new problems: negative differences, missing indices, and geometric rules.

A. Arithmetic rule with a negative difference

Problem. The terms at indices 1, 2, 3 are 9, 5, 1. Assume the sequence is arithmetic. Write explicit and recursive rules.

  1. Compute both one-step differences: \(5-9=-4\) and \(1-5=-4\), so \(d=-4\).
  2. Index n is \(n-1\) steps after the starting index 1. Hence \(\boxed{f(n)=9-4(n-1)=13-4n}\), for integers \(n\ge1\).
  3. The recursive version must give the starting term: \(\boxed{f(1)=9}\), then \(\boxed{f(n)=f(n-1)-4}\) for \(n\ge2\), with integer indices.
  4. Check the explicit rule: at indices 1, 2, 3 it gives \(13-4=9\), \(13-8=5\), and \(13-12=1\). The recursive rule generates \(9\to5\to1\) as well.

B. Arithmetic rule with missing indices

Problem. \(f(2)=7\), \(f(5)=19\), and \(f(6)=23\). Assume an arithmetic sequence defined at every integer index beginning at 2. Write both rules.

  1. From index 2 to index 5, the output rises by \(19-7=12\) across \(5-2=3\) steps. Therefore \(d=12\div3=4\). The next one-step difference is \(23-19=4\), confirming the same model.
  2. Starting with \(f(2)=7\), index n is \(n-2\) steps later: \(\boxed{f(n)=7+4(n-2)=4n-1}\), for integers \(n\ge2\).
  3. The recursive version is \(\boxed{f(2)=7}\), then \(\boxed{f(n)=f(n-1)+4}\) for \(n\ge3\), with integer indices.
  4. Check: \(4(2)-1=7\), \(4(5)-1=19\), and \(4(6)-1=23\). The intermediate terms are \(f(3)=11\) and \(f(4)=15\), so the recursive path is \(7\to11\to15\to19\to23\).

The three displayed rows are not three consecutive terms. Using a “difference” of 12 would give the wrong one-step rule.

C. Geometric rule beginning at zero, with negative terms

Problem. The values at indices 0, 1, 2 are −3, −9, −27. Assume a geometric sequence. Write both rules.

  1. Compute \((-9)\div(-3)=3\) and \((-27)\div(-9)=3\). The ratio is positive 3, not −3.
  2. Index n is n steps after index 0, so \(\boxed{f(n)=-3\cdot3^n}\), for integers \(n\ge0\).
  3. The recursive form is \(\boxed{f(0)=-3}\), then \(\boxed{f(n)=3f(n-1)}\) for \(n\ge1\), with integer indices.
  4. Check: \(-3\cdot3^0=-3\), \(-3\cdot3^1=-9\), and \(-3\cdot3^2=-27\). Multiplying the previous term by 3 gives the same results.

These terms are decreasing: −27 is smaller than −9. The negative starting term matters when interpreting the pattern.

D. Geometric rule with a two-step gap

Problem. \(f(2)=6\), \(f(4)=54\), and \(f(5)=162\). Assume a geometric sequence defined at every integer index beginning at 2. Write both rules.

  1. The neighboring indices 4 and 5 give \(r=162\div54=3\).
  2. Check the gap from 2 to 4: two multiplying steps give \(6(3)(3)=54\). The ratio \(54\div6=9\) is the two-step factor \(r^2\), not the one-step ratio.
  3. From index 2 to index n there are \(n-2\) steps, so \(\boxed{f(n)=6\cdot3^{n-2}}\), for integers \(n\ge2\).
  4. The recursive form is \(\boxed{f(2)=6}\), then \(\boxed{f(n)=3f(n-1)}\) for \(n\ge3\), with integer indices.
  5. Check: \(6\cdot3^0=6\), \(6\cdot3^2=54\), and \(6\cdot3^3=162\). The missing term is \(f(3)=18\), and the recursive path is \(6\to18\to54\to162\).

Final check for any sequence rule: substitute all supplied indices into the explicit rule; generate the intermediate terms with the recursive rule; confirm both methods agree. Keep the index, the output, and the number of steps separate.
