A function can be described by a graph, a table, a rule, or a situation. This lesson practices translating between those descriptions: finding inputs and outputs, comparing functions, describing their features, and making a graph that meets given conditions. Use it alongside Section 3.6 of your MVP packet.

2. Ready — Find a line intersection algebraically

In your MVP packetSection 3.6 · Ready · p. 30 · Problems 1–4

Set two rules equal and find their intersection.

At an intersection, both rules produce the same output. If they are already written as functions of x, set their expressions equal, solve for x, and substitute that x into either rule to find y. Check the other rule too. The answer is the ordered pair \((x,y)\).

Worked example 6 — Solve and check both coordinates

Find the intersection of \(f(x)=2x+1\) and \(g(x)=-x+7\).

\[\begin{aligned}2x+1&=-x+7\\3x+1&=7\\3x&=6\\x&=2.\end{aligned}\]

At \(x=2\), \(f(2)=2(2)+1=5\) and \(g(2)=-2+7=5\). Since the question asks for the intersection point, report both coordinates: \((2,5)\), not just \(x=2\).

Worked example 7 — Clear a fractional coefficient

Find the intersection of \(a(x)=\frac12x-1\) and \(b(x)=-x+5\).

\[\begin{aligned}\tfrac12x-1&=-x+5\\x-2&=-2x+10\\3x&=12\\x&=4.\end{aligned}\]

Substitution gives \(a(4)=2-1=1\) and \(b(4)=-4+5=1\). The intersection is \((4,1)\).

Note: In the second step, we multiply both sides by 2 to clear the fraction and make the arithmetic easier. When clearing fractions, multiply every term on both sides, including constant terms.

Interpret distance and population models.

Try problems 9 and 10 on your own first. They ask you to interpret function notation and use an exponential model. Then read through the solutions to check your work.

Problem 9 — Fran’s walking distance

Original problem

“Fran collected data on the number of feet she could walk each second and wrote the following rule to model her walking rate \(d(t)=4t\).”

  1. What is Fran looking for if she writes \(d(12)=\) ___?
  2. In this situation, what does \(d(t)=100\) tell you?
  3. How can the function rule be used to indicate a time of 16 seconds was walked?
  4. How can the function rule be used to indicate that a distance of 200 feet was walked?

Possible wording clarification: The rule models distance, not walking rate. A small wording change would make that clearer: “Fran collected data on the distance she could walk each second and wrote the following rule to model her distance: \(d(t)=4t\).”

Answers

  1. \(d(12)=4(12)=48\). After 12 seconds, Fran has walked 48 feet.
  2. \(d(t)=100\) asks for the time \(t\) when Fran has walked 100 feet.
  3. Write \(d(16)\) to represent the distance after 16 seconds. The rule gives \(d(16)=4(16)=64\) feet.
  4. Write \(d(t)=200\) to represent a distance of 200 feet. Solving \(4t=200\) gives \(t=50\) seconds.

Problem 10 — Mr. Multbank’s population model

Original problem

“Mr. Multbank has developed a population growth model for the rodents in the field by his house. He believes that starting each spring the population can be modeled based on the number of weeks with the function \(p(t)=8\cdot2^t\).”

  1. Find \(p(t)=128\).
  2. Find \(p(4)\).
  3. Find \(p(10)\).
  4. Find the number of weeks it will take for the population to be over 20,000.
  5. In a year with 16 weeks of summer, how many rodents would he expect by the end of the summer using Mr. Multbank’s model? What are some factors that could change the actual result from your estimate?

Answers

  1. When the population is 128: Solve \(8\cdot2^t=128\). Divide by 8 to get \(2^t=16=2^4\), so \(t=4\) weeks.
  2. After 4 weeks: \(p(4)=8\cdot2^4=8\cdot16=128\) rodents.
  3. After 10 weeks: Since \(2^{10}=1024\), \(p(10)=8\cdot1024=8{,}192\) rodents.
  4. More than 20,000: Start with the model inequality \(8\cdot2^t>20{,}000\). Divide both sides by 8: \(2^t>20{,}000/8=2{,}500\). Since \(2^{11}=2{,}048\) is below 2,500 and \(2^{12}=4{,}096\) is above it, the population first exceeds 20,000 after 12 weeks.
  5. After 16 weeks: Substitute 16 into the original model: \(p(16)=8\cdot2^{16}\). Because \(2^{16}=2^{10}\cdot2^6=1{,}024\cdot64=65{,}536\), \(p(16)=8\cdot65{,}536=524{,}288\) rodents. This is what the model predicts; the actual population could differ because of limited food or space, predators, disease, or other changes in conditions.
