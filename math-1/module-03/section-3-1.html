Turn a real situation into a graph

In your MVP packetSection 3.1 · Main task · p. 1 · Problem 1

Learn to label the axes and show changes in water level as you prepare to sketch Sylvia’s pool graph.

A graph can show how one quantity depends on another. In a pool story, the independent variable is elapsed time: it belongs on the horizontal (x) axis. The dependent variable is the height of the water: it belongs on the vertical (y) axis. One point, such as , means at 4 minutes, the water was 2 feet high.

Worked example 2 — Tell the story from the graph.

“Sylvia began with four feet of water. During the first two minutes, the water level decreased slowly while she used one bucket. For the next two minutes the level decreased more quickly because three people used buckets. She then drained the rest with a hose until the pool was empty at eight minutes. The level remained constant at zero while she cleaned and took a break. At thirteen minutes she started refilling; the level increased steadily until the pool reached four feet at twenty-one minutes.”

Each change of activity corresponds to a different part of the graph. The drain and fill hose segments have slopes and feet per minute—equal steepness, opposite directions.

Why this water-height graph is a function

In your MVP packetSection 3.1 · Main task · p. 1 · Problem 3

Explain why each moment in time has exactly one water height.

A function assigns exactly one output to each input. At any single instant, the pool has one water height, so water height is a function of time. The same water height can occur at many different times; that does not break the rule.

You can check that every time on the horizontal axis matches exactly one plotted height. Different choices of times and rates can produce different valid graphs, but any accurate graph of a single pool’s height at each moment will still be a function.

Do not reverse the test. One time cannot have two water heights. However, one height can correspond to two times—for example, while draining and then later while filling. Also, do not draw a vertical segment when the activity changes; that would make the water height jump instantly.
Packet clarification — Section 3.1, main task, problems 1–2What does “same rate” mean for the hoses?

The packet specifies that draining and filling with a hose occur at the same rate, but gives no dimensions or numerical times. The straight pieces and equal-but-opposite slopes above are a reasonable simplified model if the pool’s sides are vertical and the volume-flow rates are equal. In a pool whose sides widen or narrow, equal flow rates of water by volume do not automatically mean equal rates of change in water height. You may sketch a qualitative graph without numerical slope values.

Add a few points to understand the curve.

Three points meet the packet’s instruction, but extra points can help you sketch an exponential curve. Keep using the same rule, k(x) = 4 · 2x.

For negative inputs, 2−1 = ½ and 2−2 = ¼. Thus k(−1) = 4 · ½ = 2 and k(−2) = 4 · ¼ = 1. Add the points (−1, 2) and (−2, 1). Each step left halves the output: 4, 2, 1, ½, and so on. This helps show why the curve approaches zero while staying above the x-axis.

To the right, k(3) = 4 · 8 = 32. Add (3, 32) to see how quickly the curve rises. Draw a smooth curve through the points rather than connecting them with straight segments.

Ready checklist. (1) Pick three x-values, often 0, 1, and 2. (2) Calculate each output carefully, using exponents before multiplication. (3) Write three ordered pairs. (4) Choose and label a suitable axis scale. (5) Plot the points; use a straight line for a linear function and a smooth curve for a continuous exponential function. When outputs grow too large, choose smaller or negative x-values, or a larger vertical scale.

Compare graph characteristics

In your MVP packetSection 3.1 · SET · p. 4 · Problems 12–13

Compare direction, steepness, and whether each graph is connected or has breaks.

A straight-line graph is linear and has a constant rate of change. A curved graph is nonlinear, and its steepness may change as you move along it. For Problem 12, both graphs decrease, but the straight line maintains the same steepness while the curve does not.

For Problem 13, distinguish these descriptions:

  • Continuous graph: the graph is connected, with no breaks or jumps over the interval being described.
  • Discontinuous graph: the graph has breaks or jumps. Its pieces can still include whole intervals of inputs.
  • Discrete graph: only separate input values are allowed, typically shown as isolated points. This is different from a graph made of horizontal segments.
Worked example 6 — Compare the graphs in Problem 13.

Similarity: both graphs show an overall upward trend from left to right.

Difference: one is a connected straight line that rises steadily. The other consists of separated horizontal segments: the output stays constant along each segment, and later segments sit at higher levels. This graph has breaks, so the intended comparison is continuous versus discontinuous.

Each horizontal segment contains an interval of input values, not just one input. Do not treat these segments as isolated points or label the graph discrete simply because its pieces are separated.

The domain is the set of allowed inputs; the range is the set of outputs. In Problem 13, the connected line includes the inputs and output heights along the line. The separated segments include the inputs along each segment and the output height of each segment, with gaps between the pieces. An overall upward trend does not imply identical domains or ranges.

Packet clarification — Section 3.1, Set, Problem 13Disconnected does not mean discrete

Read the horizontal marks as segments, rather than replacing them with isolated plotted points. For this exercise, describe the broken graph as discontinuous. A discrete domain, whole-number outputs, and breaks in a graph are different ideas.

Teacher’s review of the source packet

Section 3.1, Set, descriptions (c) and (d) — Clarify the time window

The gasoline description should identify the period between completed deliveries. The watermelon description should specify time throughout one Thursday, rather than a count recorded each Thursday. The wording clarification in the Set section makes these intended time windows explicit.

Section 3.1, Set, Problem 13 — Discontinuous versus discrete

The upper graph uses separated horizontal segments. These segments contain intervals of inputs, so they should not be reinterpreted as isolated points. The useful classroom comparison is a connected linear graph versus a discontinuous graph with constant pieces.

This connects to the distinction raised in Module 2: whole-number outputs do not by themselves make a domain discrete. Counts can change in steps over time. Keep the allowed inputs separate from whether the graph has breaks or jumps.
