1. Turn a real situation into a graph

In your MVP packetSection 3.1 · Main task · p. 1 · Problem 1

Learn to label the axes and show changes in water level as you prepare to sketch Sylvia’s pool graph.

A graph can show how one quantity depends on another. In a pool story, the independent variable is elapsed time: it belongs on the horizontal (x) axis. The dependent variable is the height of the water: it belongs on the vertical (y) axis. One point, such as , means at 4 minutes, the water was 2 feet high.

Worked example 2 — Tell the story from the graph.

“Sylvia began with four feet of water. During the first two minutes, the water level decreased slowly while she used one bucket. For the next two minutes the level decreased more quickly because three people used buckets. She then drained the rest with a hose until the pool was empty at eight minutes. The level remained constant at zero while she cleaned and took a break. At thirteen minutes she started refilling; the level increased steadily until the pool reached four feet at twenty-one minutes.”

Why this water-height graph is a function

In your MVP packetSection 3.1 · Main task · p. 1 · Problem 3

Explain why each moment in time has exactly one water height.

A function assigns exactly one output to each input. At any single instant, the pool has one water height, so water height is a function of time. The same water height can occur at many different times; that does not break the rule.

You can check that every time on the horizontal axis matches exactly one plotted height. Different choices of times and rates can produce different valid graphs, but any accurate graph of a single pool’s height at each moment will still be a function.

Do not reverse the test. One time cannot have two water heights. However, one height can correspond to two times—for example, while draining and then later while filling. Also, do not draw a vertical segment when the activity changes; that would make the water height jump instantly.
Packet clarification — Section 3.1, main task, problems 1–2What does “same rate” mean for the hoses?

The packet specifies that draining and filling with a hose occur at the same rate, but gives no dimensions or numerical times. The straight pieces and equal-but-opposite slopes above are a reasonable simplified model if the pool’s cross-sectional area is constant and the volume-flow rates are equal. In a pool whose sides widen or narrow, equal flow rates of water by volume do not automatically mean equal rates of change in water height. You may sketch a qualitative graph without numerical slope values.

Compare graph characteristics precisely

In your MVP packetSection 3.1 · SET · p. 4 · Problems 12–13

List similarities and differences such as continuous/discrete, linear/nonlinear, and increasing/decreasing.

Two common distinctions are especially important for Set problems 12–13:

  • Continuous graph: a connected graph includes points for all input values throughout the interval shown, with no gaps or jumps. For example, water height can be measured at any instant during a filling process.
  • Discrete graph: only specified input values are included, often shown as isolated points or marks. For example, if a school records enrollment only on the first day of each month, a graph of those observations should not pretend that measurements were supplied for every day in between.

A straight-line graph is linear and has a constant rate of change. A curved graph is nonlinear, and its steepness may change as you move along it. For example, two graphs may both be decreasing even though only one has a constant rate of change. For Set Problem 12, look at both the direction and the changing steepness of the graphs already printed in your packet.

Read the whole graph, not just whether it rises or falls. A discrete graph can show an increasing trend without containing every point between the displayed marks. A straight continuous line also contains all the intervening points. Similar direction does not imply identical domains or ranges.

The domain is the set of allowed x-values (inputs), while the range is the set of resulting y-values (outputs). For Set Problem 13, examine the disconnected marks and the connected straight line in the original packet. A connected line includes intermediate inputs along the line, whereas a set of separately plotted observations does not automatically include inputs between its marks. Compare what each graph actually shows; do not assume the two have identical domains or ranges just because they both rise.

Worked example 6 — Write a similarity and a difference.

Imagine two graphs that both go upward from left to right. The first is a straight line. The second is a collection of rising, separated points.

Similarity: both show increasing output as input increases.

Difference: the first is continuous and linear; the second is discrete and does not include inputs between its marked points. To decide whether the second is itself linear as a rule, you would need its exact point pattern or underlying formula.

Apply this to the graphs on page 4 of your packet. In Problem 12, identify what the downward straight line and the downward curve share, then explain which one maintains the same steepness throughout. In Problem 13, compare the rising connected line with the separated short horizontal marks: discuss how the graphs can show a similar trend while differing in which input values are represented.

Teacher’s review of the source packet

Section 3.1, Main Task, Problem 1 — Ambiguity about rates

The task states that the pool drains and fills at the same rate, but does not specify whether this means volume per minute or water height per minute. These are not necessarily equivalent: in a pool with varying cross-sectional area, a constant volumetric flow rate does not produce a constant rate of change in water height.

Suggested correction: Specify that the pool has vertical sides and that filling and draining produce equal-magnitude, constant rates of change in water height, or ask students to sketch only the qualitative behavior.

Section 3.1, Set, Problem 11 — Continuous curve representing a count

The graph apparently intended to represent the number of watermelons remaining for sale shows a smooth, decreasing curve. But watermelons are counted in whole numbers, so the actual quantity changes in discrete steps as individual watermelons are sold.

A continuous curve could reasonably approximate an overall trend, but the packet does not distinguish such an approximation from an exact mathematical representation.

Suggested correction: Use a step function to represent the actual inventory over time, or explicitly identify the smooth curve as an approximation.

Section 3.1, Set, Problem 13 — Confusion between discrete and discontinuous functions

The upper graph consists of a sequence of short horizontal line segments. This is not an accurate representation of a function defined only at isolated input values. Each horizontal segment, interpreted literally, contains infinitely many points. Nor is this a properly specified step function: the gaps and endpoint behavior are not explained.

The problem is more serious than an unfortunate choice of plotting symbols. It raises concerns about the curriculum’s treatment of discrete and continuous functions.

In standard mathematics, a function with a discrete domain is defined only at separated input values, such as the nonnegative integers. A discontinuous function, by contrast, may be defined for every real number in an interval but have jumps or other discontinuities. These are different mathematical concepts.

The preceding MVP module does not consistently make this distinction clear. In Module 2, Section 2.2, Problems 1–5, students consider the number of books shipped, downloaded, or shelved. Problem 4 asks whether fractional numbers of books are possible, and Problem 5 immediately asks which situations represent discrete or continuous functions.

This progression risks teaching students that a function is discrete simply because its outputs must be whole numbers. That is incorrect. For example, the number of books downloaded as a function of elapsed time can have a continuous interval of real-valued inputs while its output changes in integer jumps. Such a function is a step function, not a discrete-domain function.

The same conceptual ambiguity appears in Problem 13. It is unclear whether the upper graph is intended to represent a function defined only at discrete inputs or a step-like function defined over short intervals. The diagram does not specify the full domain or what happens in the gaps. These interpretations are mathematically different, and the diagram does not adequately establish either one.

Suggested correction:

  • If the intended function has a discrete domain, specify the permitted input values and represent the graph with isolated filled points.
  • If the intended function is a step function, draw the horizontal intervals accurately, specify the domain, and indicate endpoint inclusion.
  • Explicitly distinguish discrete domains, integer-valued outputs, and discontinuities. These properties must not be treated as interchangeable.

The curriculum should establish these distinctions clearly rather than expecting students to infer the intended mathematical meaning from ambiguous diagrams.
