1. In class: A Develop Understanding Task

This section explains some of the ideas you explored in class through the guided exercises. Use it alongside the “A Develop Understanding Task” portion of Section 7.2 in your MVP packet.

A compass transfers an exact distance; a straightedge joins two located points. For the basic methods, see the 7.1 draft’s construction-tool explanation and angle-copying steps.

Try the packet task on your own first

Attempt the constructions, then use these steps and explanations to check your work or work through a difficulty.

Construct an equilateral triangle on ST

An equilateral triangle has three equal side lengths. The opening prompt on p. 7 asks you to locate its third vertex when \(ST\) is one side.

  1. Set the compass opening to \(ST\). Draw arcs centered at \(S\) and \(T\), without changing that opening.
  2. Label either intersection \(U\). Join \(SU\) and \(TU\).

Why it works: \(SU=ST\) because \(U\) lies on the circle centered at \(S\). Similarly, \(TU=ST\). All three sides are equal. Choosing the intersection below the segment gives an equally valid triangle.

Construct a parallel line by copying an angle

Parallel lines lie in the same plane and never meet. A line crossing two lines is a transversal. If the two lines make equal corresponding angles—angles in the same relative position—with that transversal, they are parallel.

Main p. 7 — Copy the angle at B to C

Original problem: Construct a line through \(C\) parallel to \(AB\), using the angle \(\angle ABC\).

  1. Extend ray \(BC\) past \(C\). This forward extension is the first side of the angle you will copy at \(C\).
  2. Center a compass arc at \(B\), crossing ray \(BA\) at \(P\) and ray \(BC\) at \(Q\).
  3. With the same opening, draw an arc centered at \(C\), crossing the forward extension at \(Q'\).
  4. Set the opening to \(PQ\). Center the compass at \(Q'\), and mark the new arc at \(P'\) on the same side of line \(BC\) as \(A\).
  5. Draw the line through \(C\) and \(P'\). It is parallel to \(BA\).

Why it works: The copied radius and distance \(PQ\) reproduce the angle opening. The equal angles occupy corresponding positions along the transversal \(BC\).

Copy onto the forward extension at C as described. Using ray CB instead would reverse the first side of the angle; copying onto the wrong side can give a different slant.

Complete a parallelogram

A parallelogram has two pairs of opposite sides parallel. After constructing the parallel to \(BA\) through \(C\), construct a parallel to \(BC\) through \(A\) by the same angle-copying method. Label their intersection \(D\). The vertex order is \(B,A,D,C\); opposite sides are parallel by construction.

Main 1–4 — Inscribe a regular hexagon

In your MVP packetSection 7.2 · Main task · 1–4 · pp. 8–9

A regular polygon has equal sides and equal angles. A hexagon has six sides. A polygon is inscribed in a circle when every vertex lies on the circle. The same circle is circumscribed about the polygon. A chord is a segment joining two points on a circle; a diameter is a chord through the center.

Main 1–3 — Why the compass opening equals the radius

Original problems: Sketch the six-triangle decomposition, identify the center, and explain why the six triangles are equilateral.

Answers:

1. Join the hexagon’s center \(O\) to all six vertices, as shown below. Three diagonals through opposite vertices create the same six triangles.

2. The center of the circle is \(O\), the common intersection of those three diagonals.

3. Each central angle is \(360^\circ\div6=60^\circ\). Each triangle has two equal radii, so it is isosceles and its other two angles are equal. They total \(180^\circ-60^\circ=120^\circ\), making each \(60^\circ\). Thus every triangle is equilateral, and each hexagon side equals the circle’s radius.

Main 4 — Step around the circle

Original problem: Illustrate and describe how to construct the regular hexagon in the given circle.

  1. Set the compass opening to the circle’s radius, from its marked center to any point on the circle.
  2. Choose a starting vertex \(A\). Center an arc at \(A\) and mark its intersection with the circle as \(B\).
  3. Keep the same opening and step from \(B\) to \(C\), then to \(D,E,F\), continuing in one direction around the circle. At each step choose the new intersection ahead, rather than the previous vertex.
  4. The next step returns to \(A\). Join consecutive vertices with the straightedge.

Each side spans one \(60^\circ\) central angle. Six steps complete \(360^\circ\). If your last mark misses the first, check the compass opening and the accuracy of your marks.

Main 5 — Inscribe an equilateral triangle

Original problem: Modify the hexagon construction to construct an equilateral triangle in the given circle.

Worked solution: Locate the six hexagon vertices, then connect every other vertex: \(A,C,E\). Each side spans the same \(120^\circ\) central angle, so the three chords are equal. Every vertex lies on the given circle.

Main 6 — Inscribe a square

Original problem: Describe how to construct a square inscribed in a circle.

  1. Draw a line through the marked center \(O\), meeting the circle at \(A\) and \(C\). This is a diameter.
  2. Construct the perpendicular bisector of \(AC\), using equal-radius arcs from \(A\) and \(C\). It passes through \(O\).
  3. Its intersections with the circle are \(B\) and \(D\). Join \(A,B,C,D\) in order around the circle.

Why it works: The four central angles are \(90^\circ\), so the four chords are equal. Each center-to-side triangle has base angles of \(45^\circ\); two such angles meet at each polygon vertex, making a right angle.

Set 7 — All third vertices for an isosceles triangle

An isosceles triangle has at least two equal sides. “All points” asks for a whole set of positions, sometimes called a locus, rather than a single example. Since the question does not specify which sides are equal, consider three cases:

  • If \(AC=BC\), \(C\) lies on the perpendicular bisector of \(AB\).
  • If \(AC=AB\), \(C\) lies on the circle centered at \(A\) with radius \(AB\).
  • If \(BC=AB\), \(C\) lies on the circle centered at \(B\) with radius \(AB\).

Draw all three sets. Exclude every point on line \(AB\), including \(A\) and \(B\): three collinear points do not form a triangle. The equilateral possibilities are included because they have at least two equal sides.

Set 8 — All third vertices for a right triangle

The right angle may be at \(A\), at \(B\), or at \(C\):

  • A right angle at \(A\): \(C\) lies on the perpendicular to \(AB\) through \(A\), excluding \(A\).
  • A right angle at \(B\): \(C\) lies on the perpendicular to \(AB\) through \(B\), excluding \(B\).
  • A right angle at \(C\): \(C\) lies on the circle whose diameter is \(AB\), excluding \(A\) and \(B\).

Construct the midpoint \(O\) of \(AB\), then draw the circle centered at \(O\) with radius \(OA\). To construct the endpoint perpendiculars, extend \(AB\) past each endpoint, mark equal distances on both sides of that endpoint, and bisect that new segment perpendicularly, as in the 7.1 draft’s endpoint-perpendicular method.

Why the diameter circle gives a right angle

For a point \(C\) on the circle, \(OA=OB=OC\). The two triangles \(AOC\) and \(BOC\) are isosceles. If their base angles at \(C\) are \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\), their angles at \(O\) are \(180^\circ-2\alpha\) and \(180^\circ-2\beta\). Those angles add to the straight angle \(180^\circ\), so \(\alpha+\beta=90^\circ\). This is \(\angle ACB\).

Conversely, in a right triangle the midpoint of the hypotenuse is equally distant from all three vertices. One way to see this is to complete the triangle to a rectangle: its equal diagonals bisect each other. Thus a right angle at \(C\) places \(C\) on that same circle.

4. Go — Recursive and explicit sequence rules

In your MVP packetSection 7.2 · Go · 9–13 · p. 12

A recursive rule gives a starting value and explains how to get the next term from the previous term. An explicit rule gives the value directly from the step number \(n\).

For a constant difference \(d\), use \(f(n+1)=f(n)+d\); for a constant multiplier \(r\), use \(f(n+1)=r f(n)\). Starting at step 1, the corresponding explicit rules are \(f(n)=f(1)+d(n-1)\) and \(f(n)=f(1)r^{n-1}\). If the first supplied step is 2, anchor the exponent or difference at \(n-2\) instead.

For the dot pattern, count the center and the five arms. For the square pattern, count the individual square cells shown, then look at how one step grows into the next. Try the rules before checking.

Check your work — Go 9–13

9. The first three counts are \(6,11,16\). There is one center dot and five arms with \(n\) dots each.

Recursive:

\[f(1)=6,\qquad f(n+1)=f(n)+5,\quad n=1,2,3,\ldots\]

Explicit:

\[f(n)=5n+1,\quad n=1,2,3,\ldots\]

10. The individual square-cell counts are \(1,5,25\). Each step contains five copies of the previous pattern.

Recursive:

\[f(1)=1,\qquad f(n+1)=5f(n),\quad n=1,2,3,\ldots\]

Explicit:

\[f(n)=5^{n-1},\quad n=1,2,3,\ldots\]

11. The values \(1,11,21,31\) increase by 10.

Recursive:

\[f(1)=1,\qquad f(n+1)=f(n)+10,\quad n=1,2,3,\ldots\]

Explicit:

\[f(n)=10n-9,\quad n=1,2,3,\ldots\]

12. The supplied table begins at \(n=2\), with \(f(2)=16\), and halves at each step.

Recursive:

\[f(2)=16,\qquad f(n+1)=\frac12 f(n),\quad n=2,3,4,\ldots\]

Explicit:

\[f(n)=16\left(\frac12\right)^{n-2},\quad n=2,3,4,\ldots\]

Extending the same pattern back to step 1 would give \(f(1)=32\).

13. The values \(-5,25,-125,625\) multiply by \(-5\), so their signs alternate.

Recursive:

\[f(1)=-5,\qquad f(n+1)=-5 f(n),\quad n=1,2,3,\ldots\]

Explicit:

\[f(n)=(-5)^n,\quad n=1,2,3,\ldots\]

The parentheses make the negative number the base of the power.
