Use a few matching sides and angles to determine when a whole triangle is fixed—and when more than one triangle is still possible.

1. In class: A Solidify Understanding Task

This section explains some of the ideas you explored in class through the guided exercises. Use it alongside the “A Solidify Understanding Task” portion of Section 7.4 in your MVP packet.

In your MVP packetSection 7.4 · Main task · 1–12 · pp. 17–20

Read the correspondence and the included parts

Congruent triangles have the same size and shape: a sequence of rigid motions can make their corresponding vertices coincide. Matching tick marks indicate equal side lengths; matching angle arcs indicate equal angle measures.

An included angle is the angle between two specified sides. For sides \(AB\) and \(BC\), it is \(\angle ABC\), at their shared endpoint \(B\). An included side joins the vertices of two specified angles: between angles at \(A\) and \(B\), it is \(AB\).

The order in a congruence statement matters. In \(\triangle ABC\cong\triangle RST\), the matching vertices are \(A\leftrightarrow R\), \(B\leftrightarrow S\), \(C\leftrightarrow T\). Thus \(AB\leftrightarrow RS\), \(BC\leftrightarrow ST\), and \(AC\leftrightarrow RT\).

Try the packet task on your own first

Use the markings and tracing paper to investigate Main 1–12. Then use the explanations and sample answers to check your reasoning.

Main 1–5 — ASA: two angles and the included side

Angle–Side–Angle (ASA)

If two pairs of corresponding angles and the side between them are equal, the triangles are congruent.

Original problems 1–2: Identify the marked equal parts and what else must be established for congruence.

Answers: The diagram gives \(\angle A\cong\angle R\), \(\angle B\cong\angle S\), and \(\overline{AB}\cong\overline{RS}\). To conclude congruence, we must show that the remaining vertex also lands in the corresponding position under the motions; then all remaining sides and angles agree.

Original problems 3–4: Find and list a sequence of motions.

Sample sequence: Translate \(A\) onto \(R\). Rotate about \(R\) until the translated ray \(AB\) lies along ray \(RS\); the equal side lengths place \(B\) on \(S\). Reflect across line \(RS\) to put \(C\) on the same side as \(T\).

Problem 5 — Why C must land on T: After the base endpoints coincide, the equal angles at both endpoints fix the two rays containing the third vertex, on the chosen side of the base. Those two rays meet at only one point. Both the moved \(C\) and \(T\) must be that intersection, so they coincide. This proves ASA for every pair satisfying the givens, not just for the pictured pair.

Main 6 — SAS: the angle must be between the two sides

Side–Angle–Side (SAS)

If two corresponding sides and their included angles are equal, the triangles are congruent.

Why it works: Align one equal side by translation and rotation, reflecting if needed to put the triangles on the same side. The included angle fixes the direction of the second side. Its given length fixes that side’s endpoint, so the third vertex has only one position.

Main 6 — Original problem: Identify the criteria, decide whether the triangles are congruent, list motions, and justify the decision.

Answer: SAS. The marked sides are \(JK=QP\), \(KL=QR\), and the included angles are \(\angle JKL=\angle PQR\). Thus \(\triangle JKL\cong\triangle PQR\).

Sample motions: Translate \(K\) onto \(Q\); rotate about \(Q\) to align ray \(KL\) with ray \(QR\), placing \(L\) on \(R\); reflect across \(QR\) to put \(J\) on the same side as \(P\). The equal included angle and length \(KJ=QP\) force \(J\) to coincide with \(P\).

Main 7 — AAA fixes shape but does not fix size

Angle–Angle–Angle (AAA) gives equal corresponding angles. It establishes similarity: the same shape, with corresponding sides in one common ratio. It does not force that ratio to be 1, so it does not guarantee congruence.

Main 7 answer: \(I\leftrightarrow P\), \(O\leftrightarrow Q\), \(U\leftrightarrow R\). The triangles are similar by AAA, but the markings do not establish congruence or a rigid-motion sequence. For example, triangles with side lengths \(3,4,5\) and \(6,8,10\) have the same angles but different sizes. Equal angles alone cannot make their sides coincide.

Main 8 — SSA can leave two different triangles

Side–Side–Angle (SSA) gives two sides and an angle that is not between them. Unlike SAS, this can leave more than one possible location for the third vertex.

Main 8 answer: The markings give \(GH=XY\), \(HI=YZ\), and \(\angle I=\angle Z\), with correspondence \(G\leftrightarrow X\), \(H\leftrightarrow Y\), \(I\leftrightarrow Z\). These are SSA, so congruence is not guaranteed. A general rigid-motion sequence cannot be justified from these givens.

Original counterexample — Same SSA data, different third sides

Take \(A=(0,0)\), \(B=(8,4)\), \(C_1=(5,0)\), and \(C_2=(11,0)\). Both triangles use the same side \(AB=4\sqrt5\). Both have \(BC=5\), since the horizontal and vertical differences from \(B\) to either \(C\) are 3 and 4. Both have the same angle at \(A\), because \(AC_1\) and \(AC_2\) follow the same ray.

Yet \(AC_1=5\) while \(AC_2=11\). The triangles are not congruent. The angle at \(A\) is opposite the known side \(BC\), so these are SSA data, not SAS.

Main 9 — SSS fixes all three side lengths

Side–Side–Side (SSS)

If all three corresponding side lengths are equal, the triangles are congruent.

Why it works: Align one side. The third vertex must lie on a circle centered at each endpoint, with radii equal to its two remaining side lengths. The circles have at most two intersections, one on each side of the base. Those two choices are reflections of each other, so they produce congruent triangles.

Main 9 answer: The marks give \(AB=PQ\), \(BC=QR\), \(AC=PR\), so \(\triangle ABC\cong\triangle PQR\) by SSS. Translate \(A\) onto \(P\), rotate about \(P\) to align ray \(AB\) with \(PQ\), then reflect across \(PQ\) to put the third vertex on the same side as \(R\). The two prescribed distances to \(P\) and \(Q\) fix that vertex on this side.

Main 10–12 — Two extensions of the criteria

10. The successful criteria established so far are ASA, SAS, and SSS. AAA and SSA do not guarantee congruence.

Angle–Angle–Side (AAS) — Main 11

Two corresponding angles and a corresponding side not between them also guarantee congruence. The third angles agree because each triangle’s angles sum to \(180^\circ\). Use one of the original angles and the third angle with the given side to obtain ASA.

Example: If \(\angle A=\angle R=40^\circ\), \(\angle B=\angle S=70^\circ\), and \(AC=RT=8\), then \(\angle C=\angle T=70^\circ\). Angles at \(A,C\) and included side \(AC\) match angles at \(R,T\) and side \(RT\), so ASA proves congruence.

Hypotenuse–Leg (HL) — Main 12

For right triangles only, equal hypotenuses and one pair of equal legs guarantee congruence. The hypotenuse is the side opposite the right angle. If its length is \(c\) and the known leg is \(a\), the other leg has length \(b=\sqrt{c^2-a^2}\). It is therefore equal in both triangles, giving SSS.

Example: A hypotenuse of 13 and a leg of 5 force the other leg to be \(\sqrt{13^2-5^2}=\sqrt{144}=12\). Both triangles have sides \(5,12,13\). The known right angle is the extra information that makes HL succeed even though SSA fails in general.

Choosing a criterion

Match vertices first, then inspect the marked parts. Three sides suggest SSS; two sides require checking whether the marked angle is included (SAS); two angles with a side give ASA or AAS. For HL, confirm both right angles and identify the hypotenuses before using the rule.

Check your work — Ready 1–2

1. Triangle correspondence

\(A\leftrightarrow T\), \(B\leftrightarrow S\), \(C\leftrightarrow R\), so \(\triangle ABC\cong\triangle TSR\).

Angles:

\[\angle A\cong\angle T,\quad \angle B\cong\angle S,\quad \angle C\cong\angle R.\]

Sides:

\[\overline{AB}\cong\overline{TS},\quad \overline{BC}\cong\overline{SR},\quad \overline{AC}\cong\overline{TR}.\]

The marked givens are \(\angle C\cong\angle R\), \(BC=RS\), and \(AC=RT\); SAS gives the remaining congruences. Reflected? Yes: the labeled vertex order reverses, so a reflection is needed.

2. Quadrilateral correspondence

\(E\leftrightarrow X\), \(F\leftrightarrow Y\), \(G\leftrightarrow Z\), \(H\leftrightarrow W\). The matching angles are:

\[\angle E\cong\angle X,\quad\angle F\cong\angle Y,\quad\angle G\cong\angle Z,\quad\angle H\cong\angle W.\]

The corresponding sides are:

\[\overline{EF}\leftrightarrow\overline{XY},\quad\overline{FG}\leftrightarrow\overline{YZ},\quad\overline{GH}\leftrightarrow\overline{ZW},\quad\overline{HE}\leftrightarrow\overline{WX}.\]

The explicitly marked equal side pairs are \(EF=XY\) and \(EH=XW\). Reflected? No reflection is needed: the order \(E,F,G,H\) matches the order \(X,Y,Z,W\) around the second figure. Translation and rotation can preserve that order.

This identifies whether a reflection is needed. A drawing cannot reveal every motion someone actually performed; two reflections can cancel the reversal.

4. Go — Solve for t and write recursive rules

In your MVP packetSection 7.4 · Go · 11–18 · p. 23

For equations, combine like terms and apply the same operation to both sides. Letters other than \(t\) represent quantities you leave in the answer when solving a formula for \(t\).

Original refresher example — Isolate t in a formula

For \(Q=3t-r\), add \(r\) to both sides to get \(Q+r=3t\), then divide by 3:

\[t=\frac{Q+r}{3}.\]

A recursive sequence rule gives a starting value and a rule for the next term. Add a constant difference for an arithmetic sequence; multiply by a constant ratio for a geometric sequence. For more examples, see the 7.2 draft’s sequence review. Try the exercises before checking.

Check your work — Go 11–18

Equations 11–14

11. For \(\frac{3t-4}{5}=5\), multiply by 5, then add 4:

\[3t-4=25,\qquad 3t=29,\qquad t=\frac{29}{3}.\]

12. \(10-t=4t+12-3t\) simplifies to \(10-t=t+12\). Thus \(-2=2t\), so \(t=-1\).

13. \(P=5t-d\). Add \(d\) and divide by 5:

\[t=\frac{P+d}{5}.\]

14. \(xy-t=13t+w\). Add \(t\) and subtract \(w\): \(xy-w=14t\), hence

\[t=\frac{xy-w}{14}.\]

Recursive rules 15–18

15. \(5,15,45,\ldots\): multiply by 3.

\[f(1)=5,\qquad f(n+1)=3f(n),\quad n=1,2,3,\ldots\]

16. \(\frac12,0,-\frac12,-1,\ldots\): subtract \(\frac12\).

\[f(1)=\frac12,\qquad f(n+1)=f(n)-\frac12,\quad n=1,2,3,\ldots\]

17. \(3,-6,12,-24,\ldots\): multiply by \(-2\).

\[f(1)=3,\qquad f(n+1)=-2f(n),\quad n=1,2,3,\ldots\]

18. \(\frac12,\frac14,\frac18,\ldots\): multiply by \(\frac12\).

\[f(1)=\frac12,\qquad f(n+1)=\frac12f(n),\quad n=1,2,3,\ldots\]

Teacher/parent review of the source packet

Set 5 and 8, printed p. 22 / PDF p. 24: The SSA markings do not determine congruence or similarity. The forced “congruent, similar, or neither” classification should allow “insufficient information,” rather than treating a failed congruence criterion as proof that the figures are neither. Likewise, AAA in Set 4 guarantees similarity but does not establish that a congruent special case is impossible.

Ready 1–2, p. 21: Interpret the reflection question as asking whether an orientation-reversing motion is needed. The final figures cannot identify the full historical sequence, since a pair of reflections can preserve orientation.
