In your MVP packet · Section 7.5 · Main · Part 1: 1–5; Part 2: 1–4 · pp. 24–26

For a refresher on ASA, SAS, and SSS, see the 7.4 draft. A conjecture is a claim you think is true; a proof explains why it follows from established facts. Testing many drawings supports a conjecture, but does not prove it for every figure.

The proof pattern

Identify two triangles. List the matching sides or angles and a reason for each. Establish triangle congruence with a valid criterion. Then use corresponding parts of congruent triangles to obtain the desired conclusion.

Try the packet task on your own first

Attempt both parts, then compare your reasoning with the proofs and sample exploration below.

Part 1, 1–5 — Prove the symmetry of an isosceles triangle

An isosceles triangle has at least two equal sides. Label those sides \(AB=AC\), and let \(M\) be the midpoint of the base \(BC\). Draw \(AM\).

Part 1, Problem 3 — Start with SSS, not assumed symmetry

Original problem: Which criterion establishes congruence of the two triangles, and why are its required parts equal?

Worked solution:

  • \(AB=AC\): the given equal sides.
  • \(BM=CM\): \(M\) is the midpoint.
  • \(AM=AM\): both triangles share this side.

Therefore \(\triangle ABM\cong\triangle ACM\) by SSS, with \(A\leftrightarrow A\), \(B\leftrightarrow C\), \(M\leftrightarrow M\). This does not assume the very symmetry we want to prove.

4. Base angles: Corresponding angles give \(\angle ABM=\angle ACM\), so the two base angles are equal.

5. Crease line: The angles \(\angle BMA\) and \(\angle CMA\) are equal and together make a straight angle. Each is therefore \(90^\circ\). Thus \(AM\) is the perpendicular bisector of \(BC\). Also, \(\angle BAM=\angle MAC\), so \(AM\) bisects the vertex angle.

1–2. Symmetry conclusion: Zac’s claim is true. A reflection across \(AM\) fixes \(A\) and interchanges \(B,C\), because \(AM\) is the perpendicular bisector of \(BC\). It therefore carries the whole triangle onto itself. Knowing that the crease passes through the vertex and midpoint is not enough by itself; establishing perpendicularity provides the required reflection line.

Part 2, 1–4 — A complete sample exploration: rectangle

The packet asks you to choose one quadrilateral. The rectangle example below supplies one complete route; a rhombus or square is also a valid choice. Label vertices \(A,B,C,D\) in order, and diagonals’ intersection \(N\).

A rectangle has four right angles. Its opposite sides are parallel because they are perpendicular to the same lines. A half-turn about the rectangle’s center interchanges opposite vertices, so the diagonals bisect each other and opposite sides are equal. These are facts from symmetry, allowed in Part 2; we will use triangles to prove the further claim that the diagonals have equal length.

Congruent trianglesCriterion and supporting facts
\(\triangle ABC\cong\triangle CDA\)SAS: \(AB=CD\), \(BC=DA\), and both included angles are right angles.
\(\triangle ABD\cong\triangle BAC\)SAS: shared \(AB\), \(AD=BC\), and the included angles at A and B are right angles.
\(\triangle ABN\cong\triangle CDN\)SSS: \(AB=CD\), \(AN=CN\), \(BN=DN\), from the half-turn.
\(\triangle ADN\cong\triangle CBN\)SSS: \(AD=CB\), \(AN=CN\), \(DN=BN\), from the half-turn.

Problem 4 — Diagonals are congruent: From \(\triangle ABD\cong\triangle BAC\), corresponding sides give \(BD=AC\). Notice that the proof used opposite-side lengths and right angles, not equal diagonals as a premise.

If you choose a rhombus: all four sides are equal. Its half-turn gives \(AN=CN\) and \(BN=DN\). Then \(\triangle ABN\cong\triangle ADN\) by SSS: \(AB=AD\), shared \(AN\), and \(BN=DN\). Corresponding angles \(\angle BNA,\angle DNA\) are equal and form a straight angle, so the diagonals are perpendicular. Corresponding angles at A also show that \(AC\) bisects \(\angle BAD\); analogous triangle pairs establish bisection at the other vertices.

If you choose a square: it is both a rectangle and a rhombus, so both arguments apply. Do not assume every listed diagonal property holds for every quadrilateral: a general rectangle need not have perpendicular diagonals, and a general rhombus need not have equal diagonals.

3. Set — Justify the conjectures

In your MVP packet · Section 7.5 · Set · 7–9 · pp. 28–29

State a criterion and justify each of its parts before taking corresponding angles or sides. A segment used in both triangles is equal to itself. Vertical angles, the opposite angles formed by two intersecting lines, have equal measures.

Set 9 · p. 29 — The triangle names give the wrong rotation correspondence

The packet says \(\triangle ADM\) is a half-turn image of \(\triangle CMB\). That vertex order pairs \(M\) with \(D\), although the pictured half-turn fixes the intersection \(M\). The intended ordered statement is that \(\triangle ADM\) is a half-turn image of \(\triangle CBM\), pairing \(C\to A\), \(B\to D\), \(M\to M\). The answer below uses this corrected correspondence.

Try each argument before checking these concise proofs.

Check your work — Set 7–9

7. Conjecture: \(\angle A\cong\angle C\)

(a) Yes.

(b) The givens \(AB=DC\), \(\angle ABD=\angle BDC\), and shared \(BD=DB\) give \(\triangle ABD\cong\triangle CDB\) by SAS. Corresponding angles at A and C are equal. The midpoint statement about M is not needed for this proof.

8. Conjecture: \(\overline{JK}\) bisects \(\angle MKL\)

(a) Yes.

(b) \(JL=JM\), \(\angle KJL=\angle KJM\), and shared \(JK\) give \(\triangle JLK\cong\triangle JMK\) by SAS. Thus \(\angle JKL=\angle JKM\). Ray KJ splits \(\angle MKL\) into two equal angles.

9. Conjecture: \(\triangle ABM\cong\triangle CDM\)

(a) Yes.

(b) In the intended half-turn with the corrected correspondence, \(M\) stays at the diagonals’ intersection, \(C\) maps to \(A\), and \(B\) maps to \(D\). Hence \(AM=CM\) and \(BM=DM\). Vertical angles give \(\angle AMB=\angle CMD\). Therefore \(\triangle ABM\cong\triangle CDM\) by SAS.

The center is M: it is the intersection of the corresponding diagonals, whose endpoints are exchanged by the half-turn.

Check your work — Go 10–17 construction methods

Completed packet constructions not shown. The directions and criteria below explain how to complete and check them; keep useful compass arcs visible.

10. A compass draws circles or arcs with a fixed radius and transfers lengths without estimating. Every point on an arc is the same distance from its center, so equal compass openings guarantee equal lengths.

11. With radius AB centered at A, locate C on the other ray of angle A. Draw arcs of that radius centered at B and C; their intersection other than A is D. Join BD and DC. Rhombus ABDC has all four sides equal to AB and retains the given angle at A.

12. Draw a transversal from N to a point on PR. Copy its angle with PR at N so that the corresponding angles agree, using the correct ray direction. The resulting line through N is parallel to PR. Follow the parallel-line steps.

13. Draw arcs centered at R and S, both with opening RS. Either intersection is the third vertex U. Join RU and SU; all three sides equal RS.

14. Set the opening to the circle’s radius. Step it around the circle to mark six vertices, then join consecutive vertices. Each side equals the radius and each central angle is \(60^\circ\). See the hexagon method.

15. Construct a parallel to CE through D and a parallel to CD through E. Label their intersection F. Parallelogram CDFE has the supplied CD and CE as adjacent sides.

16. Draw equal-radius arcs from L and M, with radius greater than half LM, meeting at two points. Join those intersections to construct the perpendicular bisector. Its intersection with LM is the midpoint, dividing LM into equal lengths.

17. Center an arc at S to meet rays SR and ST at U,V. From U and V, draw equal-radius arcs meeting inside the angle at W. Draw ray SW. It bisects \(\angle RST\); its two resulting angles are equal. See the angle-bisector method.
