Match the names before moving the figure

A rigid motion preserves lengths and angle measures: a translation slides, a rotation turns, and a reflection flips a figure. Figures are congruent if a sequence of rigid motions carries one onto the other. Corresponding parts occupy matching positions after the motions.

In the packet, the marked correspondence is:

\[A\longleftrightarrow Q,\qquad B\longleftrightarrow R,\qquad C\longleftrightarrow S,\qquad D\longleftrightarrow T.\]

Matching only an outline is not enough; the labeled vertices must match too. “Coincide” means occupy the same position.

What a complete description includes

  • Translation: give the horizontal and vertical changes, or say which point moves to which destination. Every point receives the same displacement.
  • Rotation: give the center, angle, and clockwise or counterclockwise direction.
  • Reflection: identify the mirror line, by its equation or by two points on it.

A sequence, also called a composition, applies the motions in the stated order. The output of the first motion is the input of the second. Order can change the result: translating \((1,2)\) right 3 units and then reflecting across the y-axis gives \((-4,2)\); reflecting first and then translating right 3 gives \((2,2)\).

Try the packet task on your own first

Use the markings and tracing paper to find your own sequence. Then compare it with the strategy and sample answer below.

A sample sequence for the packet quadrilaterals

Original problem: Describe a specific sequence of rigid motions carrying \(ABCD\) onto \(QRST\).

Sample answer:

  1. Translate A onto Q. Call the new positions \(A_1,B_1,C_1,D_1\). Now \(A_1=Q\).
  2. Rotate clockwise about Q through the smaller clockwise angle from ray \(QB_1\) to ray \(QR\). This aligns \(B_1\) with \(R\), while \(Q\) stays fixed. Call the resulting other vertices \(C_2,D_2\).
  3. Reflect across line QR. The two already-matched vertices \(Q\) and \(R\) stay fixed. The remaining vertices land on \(S\) and \(T\).

Use the marked congruence to align the figures, rather than estimating an angle in degrees from the drawing. The rotation angle is specified by the two rays. Check all four final vertex matches.

Original worked example — Translate, rotate, reflect

This coordinate example illustrates the strategy with simple exact numbers; it is not a coordinate reading of the packet’s main diagram.

Start with \(A=(0,0)\), \(B=(4,0)\), \(C=(3,2)\), \(D=(0,3)\). The target is \(Q=(6,1)\), \(R=(6,5)\), \(S=(8,4)\), \(T=(9,1)\).

  1. Translate right 6 and up 1.
  2. Rotate \(90^\circ\) counterclockwise about \(Q=(6,1)\).
  3. Reflect across the vertical line \(x=6\), which contains \(Q\) and \(R\).

A\((6,1)\)\((6,1)\)\(Q=(6,1)\)

For instance, after translation, \(C\) is at \((9,3)\). Relative to \(Q\), that is the displacement \((3,2)\). A counterclockwise quarter-turn sends it to \((-2,3)\); adding \(Q\)’s coordinates gives \((4,4)\). Reflecting across \(x=6\) places it 2 units to the other side, at \((8,4)\).

Reverse a sequence

To return to the original, undo the motions in reverse order. In this example: reflect across \(x=6\), rotate \(90^\circ\) clockwise about \(Q\), then translate left 6 and down 1. A reflection undoes itself; a rotation reverses direction; a translation reverses its displacement.

For the last reflection, shift the whole situation down 2 to put the mirror on \(y=x\), swap coordinates, then shift up 2 again. This gives the displayed rule.

A quarter-turn about a center other than the origin

For a center \((h,k)\), subtract the center to get the relative displacement \((u,v)=(x-h,y-k)\). Turn that displacement, then add the center back:

  • Clockwise \(90^\circ\): \((u,v)\mapsto(v,-u)\).
  • Counterclockwise \(90^\circ\): \((u,v)\mapsto(-v,u)\).

Ready 3 — Track one vertex through both motions

Original problem: Reflect the hexagon across the y-axis, then rotate \(90^\circ\) clockwise about \(P'\).

Worked solution for K: \(K=(3,-3)\) reflects to \(K'=(-3,-3)\). The center is \(P'=(-2,-6)\), not the origin. The displacement from \(P'\) to \(K'\) is \((-1,3)\). Turn it clockwise to \((3,1)\), then add the center:

\[K''=(-2+3,-6+1)=(1,-5).\]

The center \(P'\) stays in place during the rotation.

4. Go — Graph and compare functions

For a refresher on using sample points to graph lines and exponential functions, see Section 3.1 Ready. For linear comparisons, see Section 3.2 Ready. Equal intercepts show where graphs meet on the y-axis; slopes describe how lines rise or fall.

For \(y=-2^x\), exponentiation comes first: \(y=-(2^x)\). It is the reflection of \(y=2^x\) across the x-axis, so its outputs are negative. It is different from using \(-2\) as the base.

Plots not shown. Sample points and comparisons below support drawing the required graphs; they do not replace the plots.

7. \(y=\frac13x-1\) and \(y=-3x-1\)

First line: \((-9,-4)\), \((9,2)\). Second line: \((-3,8)\), \((3,-10)\). Both have y-intercept \((0,-1)\). Their slopes are negative reciprocals, so the lines are perpendicular. The first increases and the second decreases.

8. \(y=-\frac23x+5\) and \(y=\frac32x+5\)

First line: \((-6,9)\), \((9,-1)\). Second line: \((-8,-7)\), \((2,8)\). Both have y-intercept \((0,5)\). Their slopes are negative reciprocals, so they are perpendicular. The first decreases and the second increases.

9. As printed: \(y=\frac14x+2\) and \(y=\frac74\)

First line: \((-8,0)\), \((8,4)\). Second line: \((-8,\frac74)\), \((8,\frac74)\). One increases while the other is constant. They meet at \((-1,\frac74)\). Their y-intercepts are 2 and \(\frac74\), respectively.

If the missing x is restored: the second line is \(y=-\frac14x+2\), with points \((-8,4)\), \((8,0)\). Both meet at \((0,2)\); they are reflections across the y-axis. Their slopes are opposites, so one rises as the other falls.
