Teacher’s review

You know several kinds of numbers, from integers to complex numbers. Today we ask what happens when we operate on two numbers from the same group. For example, adding two integers still gives an integer—but dividing them might not. This simple idea links the arithmetic of numbers with the arithmetic of polynomials.

1. Number systems and closure

In your MVP packetSection 3.10 · Main task · PDF pp. 65–68 (first task page has no printed number)

A set is a collection of objects. Its elements are members of that collection. A set is closed under an operation when operating on any two members (where the operation is defined) produces another member of that same set.

SetMeaning and examples
Natural numbers \(\mathbb N\)Counting numbers \(1,2,3,\ldots\); the packet uses this convention.
Integers \(\mathbb Z\)\(\ldots,-2,-1,0,1,2,\ldots\)
Rational numbers \(\mathbb Q\)Fractions \(p/q\) where \(p,q\in\mathbb Z\), \(q\ne0\).
Irrational real numbersReal numbers not in \(\mathbb Q\), such as \(\sqrt2\) and \(\pi\).
Real numbers \(\mathbb R\)Rational and irrational numbers together.
Complex numbers \(\mathbb C\)\(a+bi\), with \(a,b\) real and \(i^2=-1\).
How to answer “always, sometimes, or never”

Always: explain why every permitted pair works. Sometimes: provide a pair that works and a pair that does not. Never: explain why no permitted pair can work. A few successful examples alone cannot prove an “always” claim.

Worked example 1 — Integer addition is always closed

For any integers \(m,n\), \(m+n\) is an integer; this is a basic property of integer arithmetic. Instances: \(3+8=11\), \(-4+9=5\), and \(-6+(-2)=-8\). These examples illustrate the general fact, rather than establish it by themselves.

2. Addition and multiplication across the number systems

In your MVP packetSection 3.10 · Main task · PDF pp. 65–68 (first task page has no printed number)

Multiplication and addition preserve several important sets. When two rational numbers are added, for instance, their denominators can be combined into a new integer denominator:

\[\frac ab+\frac cd=\frac{ad+bc}{bd},\qquad b\ne0,\ d\ne0.\]

Because \(ad+bc\) and \(bd\) are integers and \(bd\ne0\), the answer is rational.

Worked example 2 — Rational addition and multiplication (conjectures 2 and 8)

Add \(\frac23\) and \(-\frac54\):

\[\frac23-\frac54=\frac{8-15}{12}=-\frac7{12}.\]

Multiply them:

\[\frac23\left(-\frac54\right)=-\frac{10}{12}=-\frac56.\]

In general, multiplying \(a/b\) and \(c/d\) gives \(ac/bd\), also rational. Therefore both conjectures are always.

Worked example 3 — Irrational plus irrational is sometimes irrational (conjecture 3)

Both \(\sqrt2\) and \(-\sqrt2\) are irrational, but their sum is \(0\), which is rational. On the other hand, \(\sqrt2+\sqrt2=2\sqrt2\) is irrational. Therefore the correct answer is sometimes.

Why? If \(2\sqrt2\) were rational, dividing by 2 would make \(\sqrt2\) rational—a contradiction.

Worked example 4 — Irrational times irrational is sometimes irrational (conjecture 10)\[\sqrt3\,\sqrt3=3\quad\text{(rational)},\qquad \sqrt2\,\sqrt3=\sqrt6\quad\text{(irrational)}.\]

Thus the claim is sometimes, not always. Notice how different this is from multiplying two rational numbers.

Worked example 5 — Complex arithmetic (conjectures 5 and 12)

Write complex numbers as \(a+bi\) and \(c+di\). Then

\[(a+bi)+(c+di)=(a+c)+(b+d)i.\] \[(a+bi)(c+di)=(ac-bd)+(ad+bc)i.\]

Both results are still of the form “real part + imaginary part times \(i\).” So complex numbers are always closed under both operations. As a numerical example,

\[(2+3i)(4-i)=8-2i+12i-3i^2=11+10i.\]

These statements answer main-task conjectures 1–6, 8, 10–12 (with the separate quotient discussion below).

3. Why quotients require more care

In your MVP packetSection 3.10 · Main task · PDF pp. 65–68 (first task page has no printed number)

The integer quotient \(8\div4=2\) is an integer, but \(8\div3=8/3\) is not. This proves that integer division is sometimes an integer—not always.

Worked example 6 — Integer quotient (conjecture 7)

Successful pairs: \(12\div3=4\), \(-15\div5=-3\), \(0\div7=0\). A counterexample: \(5\div2=2.5\), not an integer. Thus sometimes.

Worked example 7 — Quotient of rational numbers (conjecture 9)

Provided the divisor is not zero,

\[\frac{a/b}{c/d}=\frac{ad}{bc},\qquad b,c,d\ne0.\]

This is rational. For example, \((2/3)\div(5/7)=14/15\). So it is always rational when the quotient is defined. Division by zero has no value; it is not a counterexample that produces an irrational number.

Worked example 8 — The circle and \(\pi\) (main task, problem 13)

For a circle, \(C=\pi d\), with diameter \(d>0\). If both \(C\) and \(d\) were rational, their quotient \(C/d\) would be rational, but it equals \(\pi\), which is irrational. Therefore a circle cannot have both a positive rational diameter and a rational circumference.

4. Polynomials behave like integers under three operations

In your MVP packetSection 3.10 · Main task · PDF pp. 65–68 (first task page has no printed number)

A polynomial in \(x\) is a sum of terms with nonnegative whole-number exponents: \(a_nx^n+\cdots+a_1x+a_0\). Constant terms correspond to exponent zero. Unlike \(x^{-1}\) or \(\sqrt x\), each allowed power is a nonnegative integer.

Worked example 9 — Add polynomials (conjecture P1)\[(2x^2-3x+4)+(x^2+5x-9)=3x^2+2x-5.\]

Combining like terms leaves only allowed powers of \(x\). So sums of polynomials are always polynomials.

Worked example 10 — Subtract polynomials (conjecture P2)\[(4x^3+x-2)-(x^3-2x+8)=3x^3+3x-10.\]

Distribute the minus sign across every term of the second polynomial; the result is still a polynomial. Thus always.

Worked example 11 — Multiply polynomials (conjecture P3)\[(x^2+2x-3)(x-4)=x^3-2x^2-11x+12.\]

The exponents produced by multiplication add: \(x^mx^n=x^{m+n}\). Adding two nonnegative integers cannot create a negative or fractional exponent, so products are always polynomials.

Polynomials: the three main-task conjectures

P1 — Addition: Always a polynomial.

P2 — Subtraction: Always a polynomial.

P3 — Multiplication: Always a polynomial.

Polynomial division need not produce a polynomial: \(1\div x=1/x\). This last example helps explain why the task specifically asks about addition, subtraction, and multiplication.

5. Ready — Compare linear, quadratic, and exponential functions

In your MVP packetSection 3.10 · Ready · Problems 1–2 · p. 66

Linear functions have constant first differences for equally spaced inputs; quadratics have constant second differences; and exponentials have a constant multiplicative ratio across equally spaced inputs, when written \(ab^x\) with \(b>0\).

Function familyExampleValues at \(x=0,1,2,3\)Recognizable feature
Linear\(y=2x+1\)1, 3, 5, 7First difference \(+2\)
Quadratic\(y=x^2\)0, 1, 4, 9First differences 1,3,5; second differences 2
Exponential\(y=2^x\)1, 2, 4, 8Consecutive ratio \(\times2\)
Worked example 12 — Describe a quadratic in different forms (Ready 1)

Consider \(y=x^2-4x+3=(x-1)(x-3)=(x-2)^2-1\). Its x-intercepts are \((1,0)\), \((3,0)\); its y-intercept is \((0,3)\); the vertex is \((2,-1)\); and the axis of symmetry is \(x=2\). Since the leading coefficient is positive, the parabola opens upward.

Worked example 13 — Compare behavior (Ready 2)

For \(y=2x+1\), moving one unit right always raises the output by 2. For \(y=x^2-4x+3\), the change depends on position and the graph turns at \(x=2\). For \(y=2^x\), moving one unit right doubles the positive output. A linear graph is a straight line, a quadratic is a parabola, and the exponential curve bends upward without a vertex.

6. Set — Which operations require a larger number system?

In your MVP packetSection 3.10 · Set · Problems 3–8 · pp. 67–68

The number sets fit into a chain \(\mathbb N\subset\mathbb Z\subset\mathbb Q\subset\mathbb R\subset\mathbb C\). Subtracting \(5-8=-3\) forces us beyond the counting numbers. Dividing \(2\div3=2/3\) forces us beyond integers. Solving \(x^2=2\) leads to an irrational real number, and \(x^2=-1\) leads to a complex number.

Worked example 14 — Classify operation outcomes (Set 3–8)

Two integers added: never irrational, because integer addition always produces an integer. Two naturals subtracted: always an integer, although not always natural; \(2-9=-7\). Two integers divided: sometimes an integer (\(8/2=4\)) and sometimes not (\(8/3\)).

Two irrationals subtracted: sometimes irrational: \(\sqrt5-\sqrt5=0\) is rational, whereas \(\sqrt5-(-\sqrt5)=2\sqrt5\) is irrational. Two complex numbers subtracted: always complex, since \((a+bi)-(c+di)=(a-c)+(b-d)i\).

Packet clarification — Section 3.10, Set, problems 4 and 7Incorrect set symbol in the prompt

The packet labels “Irrational numbers” using \(\mathbb Q\), but \(\mathbb Q\) is the conventional symbol for the rational numbers. Interpret these prompts as referring to irrational real numbers, which are \(\mathbb R\setminus\mathbb Q\). The numerical conclusion still follows from the words and examples in the problems.

7. Go — Quadratic equations and radical notation

In your MVP packetSection 3.10 · Go · Problems 9–24 · pp. 68–69

Predict the kind of roots before solving (problems 9–12)

In your MVP packetSection 3.10 · Go · Problems 9–24 · pp. 68–69

For \(ax^2+bx+c=0\), the discriminant is \(D=b^2-4ac\). Its sign tells whether real roots exist. A positive perfect-square discriminant with integer coefficients gives rational roots; a positive nonsquare discriminant gives irrational real roots (assuming rational coefficients); and a negative discriminant gives nonreal complex roots.

Positive discriminant

\(D>0\): two distinct real roots.

Zero discriminant

\(D=0\): one repeated real root.

Negative discriminant

\(D<0\): two nonreal complex conjugate roots.

Worked example 15 — Integer and fractional roots

Solve \(3x^2-5x-2=0\). Factor:

\[(3x+1)(x-2)=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x=-\frac13\ \text{or}\ x=2.\]

Both roots are rational; one is an integer and the other is not. It is important to classify each root accurately, not to assume that all rational roots are integers.

Worked example 16 — Irrational real roots

Solve \(x^2-2x-7=0\). Here \(D=(-2)^2-4(1)(-7)=32\).

\[x=\frac{2\pm\sqrt{32}}2=1\pm2\sqrt2.\]

Both roots are real and irrational because \(D\) is positive but not a perfect square.

Worked example 17 — Nonreal complex roots

Solve \(x^2+2x+6=0\):

\[D=4-24=-20,\qquad x=\frac{-2\pm\sqrt{-20}}2=-1\pm i\sqrt5.\]

There are no real x-intercepts, but both complex solutions exist.

Simplify radicals (problems 13–18)

In your MVP packetSection 3.10 · Go · Problems 9–24 · pp. 68–69

For even roots, the principal root is nonnegative; extracting an even power of a real variable may require absolute-value signs. For odd roots, negatives stay negative and absolute values are not needed.

Even root of an even power\[\sqrt{x^2}=|x|,\qquad\sqrt[4]{x^4}=|x|.\]
Odd root of an odd power\[\sqrt[3]{x^3}=x,\qquad\sqrt[5]{x^5}=x.\]
Worked example 18 — Fourth and fifth roots

Assuming \(x,y\) are real,

\[\sqrt[4]{81x^8y^{12}}=3x^2|y|^3.\]

Here \(81=3^4\), \(x^8=(x^2)^4\), and \(y^{12}=(y^3)^4\). Meanwhile, for a fifth root,

\[\sqrt[5]{32a^{10}b^{15}}=2a^2b^3.\]

No absolute values are necessary for the fifth root.

Worked example 19 — Simplify inside a square root

For real \(a,b\), with \(a\ne0\),

\[\sqrt{\frac{a^7b^{10}}{a^3}}=\sqrt{a^4b^{10}}=a^2|b|^5.\]

The absolute value is needed because the square root cannot be negative.

Worked example 20 — Multiply numerical radicals\[(\sqrt8)(\sqrt{18})(2)=2\sqrt{144}=24.\]

Both radicands are nonnegative, so combining the two square roots is legitimate. Also \(\sqrt[3]{-27}=-3\): odd roots of negatives are real and negative.

Convert radicals to rational exponents (problems 19–24)

In your MVP packetSection 3.10 · Go · Problems 9–24 · pp. 68–69
Radical-to-exponent rule\[\sqrt[n]{a^m}=a^{m/n}\]

For even \(n\), use the usual real-number domain restrictions; formulas with variables must respect the principal-root convention.

Worked example 21 — Translate each factor\[\sqrt[4]{2^7\cdot4^5}=2^{7/4}\cdot4^{5/4}.\]

Both factors share the fourth-root index. Another conversion:

\[16^{3/2}\cdot4^{1/2}=\sqrt{16^3}\cdot\sqrt4.\]
Worked example 22 — Two variables and tenth roots\[\sqrt[10]{x^{23}y^{31}}=x^{23/10}y^{31/10}\]

provided the real tenth-root expression is defined (in particular, for positive \(x,y\)). If signs are unknown, retain the combined radicand and check the even-root domain rather than applying real rational powers indiscriminately.

Additional fully worked Go problems

In your MVP packetSection 3.10 · Go · Problems 9–24 · pp. 68–69
New Go problem A — Straightforward quadratic

Predict and solve \(x^2-7x+12=0\).

\(D=49-48=1\), so expect two rational roots. Factor:

\[(x-3)(x-4)=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\boxed{x=3,\;4}.\]

Both are integers; the prediction was correct.

New Go problem B — Harder quadratic

Predict and solve \(2x^2+4x+7=0\).

\(D=16-56=-40\), so expect nonreal complex conjugates:

\[x=\frac{-4\pm\sqrt{-40}}4=\frac{-4\pm2i\sqrt{10}}4=\boxed{-1\pm\frac{i\sqrt{10}}2}.\]
New Go problem C — Even-root simplification

Simplify \(\sqrt[4]{16p^{12}q^8}\), with real \(p,q\).

\[\sqrt[4]{16p^{12}q^8}=2|p|^3q^2.\]

Although \(q^2\) is already nonnegative, \(p^3\) may be negative, so we keep \(|p|^3\).

New Go problem D — Rational-exponent conversion

Write \(\sqrt[5]{32u^8v^{11}}\) in rational-exponent form and simplify the fifth-root power where possible.

\[\sqrt[5]{32u^8v^{11}}=2u^{8/5}v^{11/5}=2uv^2\sqrt[5]{u^3v}.\]

The fifth root is odd, so this works for any real \(u,v\).

8. Packet clarifications

Packet clarification — Section 3.10, Main task, conjectures 7 and 9Division by zero

Interpret quotient claims only when the divisor is nonzero. Division by zero is undefined, so it does not belong to the set of possible quotient results.

Packet clarification — Section 3.10, Main task, The Arithmetic of PolynomialsPolynomial exponents include zero

The description states that the exponents are positive integers. A complete polynomial definition also permits exponent \(0\), which is how constant terms are included. The task itself has a constant coefficient in its displayed expression; use nonnegative integer exponents for the formal definition.

Teacher’s review — For teachers and parents

Serious issue 1 — Set problems 4 and 7: the printed worksheet calls irrational numbers \(\mathbb Q\), contradicting the standard meaning of that symbol (rational numbers). Replace this symbol with “irrational real numbers” or \(\mathbb R\setminus\mathbb Q\).

Serious issue 2 — Polynomial definition in the main task: “positive integer” exponents exclude a constant term although the polynomial form includes it. Say “nonnegative integer” exponents instead.

Division qualification: Main-task conjecture 9 should specify a nonzero divisor. The ordinary mathematical convention is clear, but explicitly noting it avoids treating an undefined quotient as a member of a number set.

All other exercises can be understood using the standard conventions addressed in this lesson.
