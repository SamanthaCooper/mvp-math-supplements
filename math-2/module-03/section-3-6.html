Suppose you know a rectangle’s area but not its side lengths. If one side is a fixed amount longer than the other, a quadratic equation describes the missing dimensions. A different quadratic equation appears when a business changes its prices and the number of customers changes in response. Both problems start with ordinary relationships, not with a formula to memorize.

1. In class: A Solidify Understanding Task

Revisit the ideas from class

Write a quadratic equation from a rectangular area

In your MVP packetSection 3.6 · Main task · Problems 1–12 · pp. 36–40

For a rectangle, area equals length times width. If its width is \(x\) and its length is \(n\) units greater, its area is \(x(x+n)\). Both lengths must be positive for a physical rectangle.

Area equation — build it from the dimensions

Width: \(x\)

Length: \(x+n\)

Area: \(A\)

\[\boxed{x(x+n)=A}\]

Expanding and moving everything to one side gives \(x^2+nx-A=0\). The geometry requires \(x>0\) and \(x+n>0\).

Worked example 1 — A square with area 48 square inches

A square has four equal sides, so \(x^2=48\). Take the nonnegative root because a length cannot be negative.

\[x=\sqrt{48}=\sqrt{16\cdot3}=4\sqrt3\approx6.93\text{ inches}.\]

The logo is \(4\sqrt3\) inches by \(4\sqrt3\) inches. Do not use the negative solution for a side length.

Worked example 2 — A rectangle 8 inches longer than it is wide

The area is 48 square inches. Let width be \(x\) inches; length is \(x+8\).

\[x(x+8)=48\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x^2+8x-48=0.\]

Find integers whose product is \(-48\) and whose sum is \(8\): 12 and −4.

\[(x+12)(x-4)=0.\]

So \(x=-12\) or \(x=4\). Only \(x=4\) is a possible width, giving a 4-inch by 12-inch rectangle. Check: \(4\cdot12=48\).

Worked example 3 — A rectangle 13 inches longer than it is wide

Again let width be \(x\):

\[x(x+13)=48\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x^2+13x-48=0.\] \[(x+16)(x-3)=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x=-16\text{ or }x=3.\]

Discard the negative width; the rectangle is 3 inches by 16 inches.

When factoring is not convenient, use a different strategy

In your MVP packetSection 3.6 · Main task · Problems 1–12 · pp. 36–40

The same quadratic can be viewed as an equation, \(x^2+nx-A=0\), or a function, \(y=x^2+nx-A\). Its algebraic solutions are exactly the x-values of the graph’s x-intercepts. A graph can help estimate solutions; algebra gives exact values.

Worked example 4 — Completing the square for an awkward dimension

Find a rectangle of area 48 square inches whose length is 6 inches greater than its width.

\[x^2+6x-48=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x^2+6x=48.\]

Add \((6/2)^2=9\) to both sides:

\[(x+3)^2=57\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x=-3\pm\sqrt{57}.\]

The usable width is \(\sqrt{57}-3\approx4.55\) inches and the length is \(\sqrt{57}+3\approx10.55\) inches. The exact product is \((\sqrt{57}-3)(\sqrt{57}+3)=57-9=48\).

Worked example 5 — The quadratic formula, independently checking the result

Apply the formula to \(x^2+6x-48=0\) with \(a=1\), \(b=6\), \(c=-48\):

\[x=\frac{-6\pm\sqrt{6^2-4(1)(-48)}}{2(1)}=\frac{-6\pm\sqrt{228}}2=-3\pm\sqrt{57}.\]

The formula agrees with completing the square. This gives two algebraic roots, but only one valid width.

Choose a method based on the structure

Factoring: use when the quadratic has easily recognized factors, as in \(x^2+8x-48\).

Completing the square: use to isolate one squared expression, as in \((x+3)^2=57\).

Quadratic formula: use for any quadratic equation \(ax^2+bx+c=0\), where \(a\ne0\).

\[\boxed{x=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}}\]

Graphing: use the x-intercepts to visualize or check solutions; a sketch usually supplies estimates rather than exact roots.

Worked example 6 — A driveway mascot, area 16 square feet

A rectangle is 6 feet longer than it is wide. Write \(x(x+6)=16\).

\[x^2+6x-16=(x+8)(x-2)=0.\]

The valid width is 2 feet and length is 8 feet, since \(2\cdot8=16\).

If the rectangle is instead 10 feet longer than wide, write \(x^2+10x-16=0\). Complete the square:

\[(x+5)^2=41\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x=-5\pm\sqrt{41}.\]

The positive width is \(\sqrt{41}-5\approx1.40\) feet; the length is \(\sqrt{41}+5\approx11.40\) feet.

Algebra versus context. A quadratic equation may have two real solutions, but a context can eliminate some. A negative length is not a second possible rectangle; it is only a root of the equation.

Build a revenue model from price and quantity

In your MVP packetSection 3.6 · Main task · Problems 1–12 · pp. 36–40

Suppose a service can sell 100 driveway mascots at $20 each. For each $5 price increase, it sells 10 fewer. Let \(x\) be the number of $5 increases. In the survey model, both price and quantity are linear functions of \(x\).

Price and quantity — distinct relationships

Price per mascot:

\[\boxed{P(x)=20+5x}\]

Quantity sold:

\[\boxed{Q(x)=100-10x}\]

Revenue = price × quantity:

\[\boxed{R(x)=(20+5x)(100-10x)}\]

For integer price-increase steps with a nonnegative selling price and a nonnegative number sold, the main task uses \(0\le x\le10\). The polynomial can be graphed beyond that interval algebraically, but those values need not have business meaning.

Multiplying and combining like terms gives

\[R(x)=2000+300x-50x^2.\]

Revenue is quadratic because it is the product of two linear expressions. It first rises and then falls: eventually the lower sales outweigh the higher price.

Price \(P(x)\) rises in a straight line; sales \(Q(x)\) falls in a straight line. Because their vertical units differ, avoid interpreting their heights as comparable quantities even if both are plotted on a shared coordinate grid, as the packet requests.

Solve a target-revenue equation

In your MVP packetSection 3.6 · Main task · Problems 1–12 · pp. 36–40

The twins want $2,000 profit after $250 of fixed supplies. Since profit = revenue − cost, their revenue target is \(2000+250=2250\) dollars. The problem gives a fixed cost estimate; it does not provide separate per-mascot costs.

Worked example 8 — Two prices reach the same revenue

Set the revenue function equal to $2,250:

\[(20+5x)(100-10x)=2250.\]

Expand and simplify:

\[2000+300x-50x^2=2250\] \[-50x^2+300x-250=0\] \[x^2-6x+5=0\] \[(x-1)(x-5)=0.\]

Therefore \(x=1\) or \(x=5\). Translate increments back into prices:

\[P(1)=20+5(1)=\$25,\qquad P(5)=20+5(5)=\$45.\]

Check: at $25, 90 mascots give $2,250; at $45, 50 mascots also give $2,250. Both are valid within the model. After subtracting $250, profit is $2,000 either way.

Worked example 9 — Explain the peak without relying on the graph alone

Complete the square in the revenue equation:

\[R(x)=-50(x^2-6x)+2000=-50(x-3)^2+2450.\]

The squared term is never negative, so \(-50(x-3)^2\le0\). Therefore maximum revenue is $2,450, at \(x=3\), meaning a price of \(20+5(3)=\$35\). The graph is symmetric around \(x=3\), which explains why \(x=1\) and \(x=5\) yield the same revenue.

2. Ready — x- and y-intercepts of linear equations

Study the worked examples first, then try the problems yourself. An answer key at the bottom of this section is provided to check your work.

In your MVP packetSection 3.6 · Ready · Problems 1–4 · p. 41

For an x-intercept, set \(y=0\) and solve for \(x\). For a y-intercept, set \(x=0\) and solve for \(y\). Record intercepts as ordered pairs. This works regardless of whether the line appears in standard, slope-intercept, or point-slope form.

Worked example 10 — Standard form

Find the intercepts of \(3x+4y=24\).

Set \(y=0\): \(3x=24\), so the x-intercept is \((8,0)\).

Set \(x=0\): \(4y=24\), so the y-intercept is \((0,6)\).

Worked example 11 — Slope-intercept form

Find the intercepts of \(y=-2x+10\).

The y-intercept \((0,10)\) is immediately visible. For the x-intercept, \(0=-2x+10\) gives \((5,0)\).

Worked example 12 — Point-slope form

Find the intercepts of \(y-3=2(x+4)\).

Set \(x=0\): \(y-3=8\), giving \((0,11)\). Set \(y=0\): \(-3=2(x+4)\); divide by 2 and subtract 4 to get \(x=-11/2\). The x-intercept is \((-11/2,0)\).

Choosing the most convenient line form

\(y=mx+b\): the y-intercept \((0,b)\) is already shown.

\(Ax+By=C\): set one variable to zero to find either intercept directly.

\(y-y_1=m(x-x_1)\): substitute zero for one coordinate and solve; this form makes the given point \((x_1,y_1)\) visible.

Check your work — Ready 1–4 answer key
  1. a. \((4,0)\); b. \((\frac35,0)\); c. \((\frac14,0)\); d. \((\frac14,0)\); e. \((-10,0)\); f. \((2,0)\).
  2. Standard form \(Ax+By=C\) is convenient: setting \(y=0\) immediately gives \(Ax=C\). Slope-intercept form also works directly by setting its output to zero.
  3. a. \((0,3)\); b. \((0,-3)\); c. \((0,1)\); d. \((0,1)\); e. \((0,20)\); f. \((0,-5)\).
  4. Slope-intercept form \(y=mx+b\) displays the y-intercept \((0,b)\) directly. Standard form is also convenient: setting \(x=0\) gives \(By=C\).

3. Set — turn quadratic equations back into rectangles

Study the worked examples first, then try the problems yourself. An answer key at the bottom of this section is provided to check your work.

In your MVP packetSection 3.6 · Set · Problems 5–12 · p. 42

An equation of the form \(x^2+nx-A=0\) can be rearranged as \(x(x+n)=A\). The rectangle has a width \(x\), length \(x+n\), and area \(A\) square units. The interpretation requires both dimensions to be positive; an equation with a positive area can still have a negative algebraic solution.

Worked example 13 — Read dimensions from the equation

Interpret and solve \(x^2+9x-36=0\).

Write \(x(x+9)=36\). A width of \(x\) and length \(x+9\) give area 36. Factoring:

\[(x+12)(x-3)=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x=-12\text{ or }x=3.\]

Only \(x=3\) is a valid width. The rectangle measures 3 by 12.

Worked example 14 — When the area model does not factor over integers

Interpret \(x^2+4x-18=0\), or \(x(x+4)=18\). The length is 4 units greater than the width and the area is 18.

\[(x+2)^2=22\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x=-2\pm\sqrt{22}.\]

Only \(x=\sqrt{22}-2\approx2.69\) is positive. The length is \(\sqrt{22}+2\approx6.69\). Their exact product is 18.

Packet clarification — Section 3.6, Set, Problems 5–8

“What constraints have been placed on the dimensions?” refers to the physical restrictions on the rectangle: both lengths must be positive, and the longer side is the fixed amount greater than the shorter side. The quadratic may have a negative solution, but that solution does not represent a possible width.

4. Go — factor quadratic expressions thoroughly

Study the worked examples first, then try the problems yourself. An answer key at the bottom of this section is provided to check your work.

In your MVP packetSection 3.6 · Go · Problems 13–21 · p. 42

The Go homework asks for factored expressions, not necessarily solutions to equations. In particular, \(x^2-5x+6=(x-2)(x-3)\) is a factorization, but writing \(x=2\) or \(x=3\) would only be appropriate if an equation equal to zero were given.

Factoring checklist — use in this order

1. Greatest common factor (GCF): remove any factor shared by every term.

2. Trinomial \(x^2+bx+c\): find \(m,n\) with \(m+n=b\) and \(mn=c\), then write \((x+m)(x+n)\).

3. Difference of squares: \(u^2-v^2=(u-v)(u+v)\).

4. Perfect-square trinomial: \(u^2\pm2uv+v^2=(u\pm v)^2\).

New Go Problem A — Straightforward trinomial

Factor \(x^2+9x+20\).

Two numbers must multiply to 20 and add to 9: 4 and 5.

\[\boxed{x^2+9x+20=(x+4)(x+5).}\]

Check by expansion: \(x^2+5x+4x+20=x^2+9x+20\).

New Go Problem B — More challenging, negative constant

Factor \(x^2-7x-60\).

We need a negative product and a negative sum. The pair is −12 and 5:

\[\boxed{x^2-7x-60=(x-12)(x+5).}\]

Verify: the outer and inner terms add to \(-12x+5x=-7x\).

New Go Problem C — Greatest common factor

Factor \(6x^2+18x\).

Both terms contain \(6x\); pull it out rather than trying to form two binomials:

\[\boxed{6x^2+18x=6x(x+3).}\]
New Go Problem D — A perfect square

Factor \(x^2-14x+49\).

Since \(49=7^2\) and \(-14x=-2\cdot7\cdot x\), this is a perfect-square trinomial:

\[\boxed{x^2-14x+49=(x-7)^2.}\]
New Go Problem E — Factor out a negative common term

Factor \(-2x^2+18x\).

Factor out \(-2x\) so the binomial begins with a positive \(x\):

\[\boxed{-2x^2+18x=-2x(x-9).}\]

Expanding \(-2x\cdot(-9)\) gives the positive \(18x\) term.

New Go Problem F — Difference of squares after taking out a GCF

Factor \(3x^2-75\).

First remove 3: \(3(x^2-25)\). Now \(x^2-25=x^2-5^2\) is a difference of squares:

\[\boxed{3x^2-75=3(x-5)(x+5).}\]

Teacher’s review — serious packet issues

No major mathematical error was identified in Section 3.6. The main task's implicit modeling assumption deserves attention: the survey's linear relationship between price and quantity sold is treated as reliable at $5 steps in the modeled interval. It is a model based on survey estimates, not a guaranteed count of customers at every price.

Section 3.6, Set, Problems 5–8: “What constraints have been placed on the dimensions?” can be read as asking only for an algebraic equation rather than real geometric constraints. A clearer version would be: “Identify the two side-length expressions and state which values of \(x\) make both side lengths positive.”
