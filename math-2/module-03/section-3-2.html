In Section 3.1, you saw that exponential relationships grow by multiplying by the same factor for equal-sized increases in the input. This lesson uses that idea in a bank-account situation. A constant yearly percentage means a constant yearly multiplier—not a constant number of dollars in interest.

1. In class: A Solidify Understanding Task

Revisit the ideas from class

This section explains some of the ideas you explored in class through the guided exercises. Use it alongside the “A Solidify Understanding Task” portion of Section 3.2 in your MVP packet.

Compound interest over whole years

In your MVP packetSection 3.2 · Main task · Problems 1–13 · pp. 7–11

Suppose an account contains $382.88 now and earns 5% interest each year, compounded annually. Five percent of the current balance is added at each year’s end. Every year the new balance is 105% of the previous balance.

\[\text{New balance}=\text{Old balance}+0.05(\text{Old balance})=1.05(\text{Old balance})\]
Worked example 1 — Build the year-by-year table

Multiply by 1.05 for each complete year. Retain the unrounded value during calculation and round to cents when displaying money.

\[A(0)=382.88,\quad A(1)=382.88(1.05)=402.024\approx\$402.02.\]

After five years, repeat the multiplication five times, or use an exponent:

\[A(5)=382.88(1.05)^5\approx\$488.66.\]
Years from nowExact calculation before roundingBalance rounded to cents
0\(382.88(1.05)^{0}\)$382.88
1\(382.88(1.05)^{1}\)$402.02
2\(382.88(1.05)^{2}\)$422.13
3\(382.88(1.05)^{3}\)$443.23
4\(382.88(1.05)^{4}\)$465.39
5\(382.88(1.05)^{5}\)$488.66

Each multiplication contributes one year of growth. Therefore, the compact rule is

\[\boxed{A(n)=382.88(1.05)^n}\qquad\text{for whole-number years }n\geq 0.\]

This is an exponential function. Its input is elapsed time, its initial value is 382.88, and its constant annual growth factor is 1.05. The exponential equation gives the same balances as repeated year-by-year multiplication because the power counts the number of factors.

Work backward to past balances

In your MVP packetSection 3.2 · Main task · Problems 1–13 · pp. 7–11

To reverse a year of 5% growth, divide by 1.05. You should not subtract 5% of the current balance: that is not the reverse operation of multiplying by 1.05.

Worked example 2 — Find the balance five years ago

One year ago, the balance was \(382.88\div1.05\). Five years ago:

\[A(-5)=\frac{382.88}{(1.05)^5}=382.88(1.05)^{-5}\approx\$300.00.\]

Check: growing that starting balance forward for five years returns approximately $382.88.

Recall: For positive \(b\), \(b^{-n}=1/b^n\). A negative exponent means repeated division, not a negative account balance.

What does half a year mean?

In your MVP packetSection 3.2 · Main task · Problems 1–13 · pp. 7–11

The same exponential model can assign a balance between whole years. We want a half-year multiplier that produces the full-year multiplier 1.05 after it is used twice. Call that multiplier \(q\).

\[q\cdot q=1.05\quad\Longrightarrow\quad q^2=1.05\quad\Longrightarrow\quad q=\sqrt{1.05}\approx1.024695\]

We use the positive square root because this is a positive growth multiplier. Thus the exponent \( frac12\) means the square-root multiplier:

\[1.05^{1/2}=\sqrt{1.05}\]
Worked example 3 — Compare two half-year estimates

Simple half-of-the-annual-interest estimate: Half of \(0.05(382.88)\) is \(\$9.572\), giving \(382.88+9.572=\$392.452\approx\$392.45\).

Consistent exponential half-year factor:

\[A(1/2)=382.88(1.05)^{1/2}=382.88\sqrt{1.05}\approx\$392.34.\]

The answers differ because dividing the dollar increase in half is additive, whereas an exponential model divides the multiplication into equal factors.

The exponential half-year rule is a mathematical model consistent with continuous interpolation of the exponential function. A real bank’s payment for part of a year depends on its interest and compounding contract; the packet is comparing mathematical models rather than stating a universal banking policy.

The difference grows clearer over longer time spans. Half of the dollar growth over four years is not the correct growth over two years. Because \(1.05^4\) is the four-year factor, the two-year factor must be \(1.05^2\), whose square is \(1.05^4\).

Worked example 4 — Why halfway in time is not halfway in dollars\[A(4)=382.88(1.05)^4\approx\$465.39,\qquad A(2)=382.88(1.05)^2\approx\$422.13.\]

Arithmetic midpoint: \((382.88+465.39)/2\approx\$424.14\), not the two-year balance.

Quarters of a year and three-quarters of a year

In your MVP packetSection 3.2 · Main task · Problems 1–13 · pp. 7–11

For equal quarter-year steps, we need a multiplier \(r\) that becomes 1.05 after four identical multiplications. Therefore \(r^4=1.05\) and \(r=\sqrt[4]{1.05}=1.05^{1/4}\). Three quarter-year steps multiply by this factor three times.

\[A(3/4)=382.88\left(\sqrt[4]{1.05}\right)^3=382.88(1.05)^{3/4}\]
Worked example 5 — Account balance after nine months

Calculate with full precision, rounding only the final amount:

\[A(3/4)=382.88(1.05)^{3/4}\approx \$397.15.\]

Interest earned is approximately \($397.15-382.88=\$14.27\).

Here \(1.05^{3/4}\) may be understood as taking a fourth root to obtain a quarter-year factor, then multiplying by that factor three times. This explains the fractional exponent; detailed rules for operating with rational exponents will be revisited in later sections.

Essential idea. For a positive growth factor \(b\): \(b^{1/2}\) is the factor for half the time; \(b^{1/4}\) for a quarter of the time; and \(b^{3/4}\) for three quarters of the time. Repeating these equal subintervals reproduces the original whole-year multiplication.

2. Ready: simplify square, cube, fourth, and fifth roots (problems 1–9)

Study the worked examples first, then try the problems yourself. An answer key at the bottom of this section is provided to check your work.

In your MVP packetSection 3.2 · Ready · Problems 1–9 · p. 12

A radical is a root. The small index tells you the number of equal factors: \(\sqrt[3]{8}=2\) because \(2^3=8\), while \(\sqrt[4]{81}=3\) because \(3^4=81\). Without a written index, \(\sqrt{\phantom x}\) means a square root, and \(\sqrt{25}=5\), the nonnegative principal square root.

To simplify a radical, look for the largest convenient perfect power whose exponent matches its root index. Factor it out of the number under the radical (the radicand).

Worked example 6 — Square root\[\sqrt{48}=\sqrt{16\cdot3}=\sqrt{16}\sqrt3=4\sqrt3.\]

Choose a perfect square factor, not simply any factor.

Worked example 7 — Cube root\[\sqrt[3]{40}=\sqrt[3]{8\cdot5}=2\sqrt[3]{5}.\]

Here 8 is a perfect cube: \(2^3=8\).

Worked example 8 — Fifth root\[\sqrt[5]{224}=\sqrt[5]{32\cdot7}=2\sqrt[5]{7}.\]

Because \(2^5=32\), exactly one 2 emerges from the fifth root.

For the worksheet, problems 1, 2, 4, 6, and 8 use square roots; 3 and 9 use cube roots; 5 uses a fourth root; and 7 uses a fifth root. Not every radicand contains a perfect-power factor larger than 1.

Avoid two common mistakes: \(\sqrt{a+b}\) is not generally \(\sqrt a+\sqrt b\). Also, \(\sqrt{a^2}=|a|\) for real \(a\)—not necessarily \(a\)—because square roots are nonnegative.

3. Set: arithmetic means, geometric means, and interval factors (problems 10–18)

Study the worked examples first, then try the problems yourself. An answer key at the bottom of this section is provided to check your work.

In your MVP packetSection 3.2 · Set · Problems 10–18 · pp. 13–14

An arithmetic mean lies halfway between two numbers on an equally spaced additive sequence. Insert several arithmetic means by finding the common difference:

\[d=\frac{\text{last value}-\text{first value}}{\text{number of intervals}}.\]
Worked example 9 — Insert four arithmetic means

Insert four equally spaced values between 7 and 32. There are five intervals, not four:

\[d=\frac{32-7}{5}=5.\]

The full list is \(7,12,17,22,27,32\). The four inserted means are 12, 17, 22, and 27.

A geometric mean fills a position in a multiplicative sequence. When the given endpoints are positive, the common positive ratio \(r\) must satisfy \(r^k= ext{last}/ ext{first}\), where \(k\) is the number of intervals.

Worked example 10 — Insert two geometric means

Insert two geometric means between 5 and 135. Three equal multiplications lead from 5 to 135:

\[5r^3=135,\qquad r^3=27,\qquad r=3.\]

The sequence is \(5,15,45,135\); the means are 15 and 45.

In worksheet problems 16–18, the input values are \(0, frac12,1, frac32,2\). The whole-step factor \(F_w\) advances the input by 1; the column factor \(F_c\) advances it by \( frac12\). Two columns equal one whole step, so \(F_c^2=F_w\).

Worked example 11 — Reconstruct a growth table

Suppose \(y(0)=6\) and \(y(1)=24\). Then

\[F_w=\frac{24}{6}=4,\quad F_c=\sqrt4=2.\]

Multiply by 2 each column:

4. Go: evaluating functions and finding inputs (problems 19–24)

Study the worked examples first, then try the problems yourself. An answer key at the bottom of this section is provided to check your work.

In your MVP packetSection 3.2 · Go · Problems 19–24 · p. 14

A function rule turns an input into an output. To evaluate \(f(c)\), substitute \(c\) into every occurrence of the input variable. To find the input for a given output, set the formula equal to that output and solve. This homework includes linear, quadratic, and exponential functions, including fractional and negative inputs.

Worked Go problem A — Straightforward linear function

Let \(f(x)=4x+3\). Find \(f(-2)\), find the input where \(f(x)=27\), and find \(f(\tfrac12)\).

\[f(-2)=4(-2)+3=-5.\] \[4x+3=27\Rightarrow4x=24\Rightarrow x=6.\] \[f(1/2)=4(1/2)+3=5.\]

Substitute using parentheses to preserve negative signs.

Worked Go problem B — Quadratic function

Let \(h(x)=x^2-5x-14\). Evaluate \(h(-3)\), solve \(h(x)=0\), and evaluate \(h(\tfrac12)\).

\[h(-3)=9+15-14=10.\] \[x^2-5x-14=(x-7)(x+2)=0\Rightarrow x=7\text{ or }-2.\] \[h(1/2)=\frac14-\frac52-14=-\frac{65}{4}.\]

Unlike a linear equation, the quadratic equation may have two inputs with the same output.

Worked Go problem C — Exponential function

Let \(g(t)=80(1.25)^t\). Find \(g(-2)\), \(g(\tfrac12)\), and the input for which \(g(t)=125\).

\[g(-2)=\frac{80}{(1.25)^2}=51.2.\] \[g(1/2)=80\sqrt{1.25}\approx89.44.\] \[80(1.25)^t=125\Rightarrow (1.25)^t=\frac{125}{80}=1.5625=(1.25)^2\Rightarrow t=2.\]

For negative exponents, take the reciprocal; for a half exponent, take the positive square root. This equation was chosen so its input can be recognized without logarithms (a later technique).

Worked Go problem D — A power outside parentheses

Let \(m(x)=3(4^x)\). Evaluate \(m(-1)\), solve \(m(x)=12\), and evaluate \(m(\tfrac12)\).

\[m(-1)=3/4,\qquad 3(4^x)=12\Rightarrow4^x=4\Rightarrow x=1,\qquad m(1/2)=3\sqrt4=6.\]

A factor outside the power is not part of the exponent: \(3(4^x)\neq (12)^x\).

For packet problem 21(b), \(210(1.08)^t=420\) leads to \((1.08)^t=2\). The exact input need not be a simple fraction or integer. Without logarithms, estimate using a table or graph of powers of 1.08 until the output is close to 420; a calculator can refine the estimate. Do not confuse an approximate numerical input with an exact algebraic solution.

Packet clarificationsSection 3.2, main task problems 7–13

The story says the bank will pay a “half-year portion” of interest but does not define the bank’s actual partial-year interest convention. Carlos’s half-of-annual-interest amount and Clarita’s exponential half-year interpolation represent different mathematical models. The latter preserves a constant multiplicative growth rate across equal intervals; it does not by itself establish what a real bank is legally required to pay.

Section 3.2, Go problem 21(b): The equation \(210(1.08)^t=420\) requires a non-obvious time value. As the lesson has not yet introduced logarithms, a numerical estimate from tables or technology is reasonable unless the instructor supplies another method.

6. Teacher’s review (teachers and parents)

Main task, problems 7–13: The statement about a bank paying a “half-year portion” of interest lacks a specified bank convention. The subsequent preference for \(A(t)=382.88(1.05)^t\) is mathematically appropriate for an exponential interpolation model, but is not automatically a statement about contractual bank interest. Suggested revision: “Assume the account follows the exponential model \(A(t)=382.88(1.05)^t\) for every real number of years \(t\). Compare this with paying half the nominal annual interest.”

Ready introductory explanation: The packet suggests that square roots of non-square numbers are necessarily irrational; this is acceptable for positive integers that are not perfect squares, but false for arbitrary non-square rational numbers (for example \(\sqrt{1/4}=1/2\)). State the integer condition explicitly.

Go 21(b): Finding an input from \(210(1.08)^t=420\) is not readily accomplished exactly with the algebra introduced at this point. Clarify that a numerical approximation or graph is acceptable, unless logarithms have been taught elsewhere.
