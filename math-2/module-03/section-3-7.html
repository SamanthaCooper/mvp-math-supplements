You already know several methods for quadratic equations. The new challenge is fluency: understanding why each method works, noticing when it gives an exact result and when it only gives an estimate, and choosing an efficient one for the equation in front of you.

1. What does solving a quadratic mean?

In your MVP packetSection 3.7 · Main task · pp. 43–45

To solve \(x^2-2x-15=0\), we need inputs that make the left side zero. If we define \(f(x)=x^2-2x-15\), the solutions are the zeros of the function, or the x-coordinates of its x-intercepts. These are three descriptions of the same condition: \(f(x)=0\).

Worked example 1 — Check a proposed solution

Try \(x=5\):

\[5^2-2(5)-15=25-10-15=0.\]

Therefore 5 is a solution. Try \(x=0\): \(0-0-15=-15\), so 0 is not a solution. Checking confirms a candidate; it does not automatically reveal every solution.

2. Carlos and Zac: tables and graphs

In your MVP packetSection 3.7 · Main task · pp. 43–45

Carlos’s table method evaluates the related function at chosen x-values. An output of zero identifies an exact solution. If no table entry equals zero, values on either side may help locate an approximate crossing, but a finite table alone does not prove that every zero has been found.

Worked example 2 — Locate zeros in a table
\(x\)−4−3−2035
\(f(x)=x^2-2x-15\)90−7−15−120

Zeros occur at \(x=-3\) and \(x=5\). A complete quadratic graph or an algebraic method can establish that these are the two real solutions.

Zac’s graph method finds where \(y=f(x)\) crosses or touches the x-axis. It provides a visual estimate and shows whether the parabola has two, one, or no x-intercepts. Use algebra if exact roots are required.

Graph-reading caution. A zero such as \(2+2\sqrt3\) cannot ordinarily be read exactly from a standard coordinate graph. State graph readings as estimates unless exact coordinates are explicitly given.

3. Clarita: factor and use the zero-product property

In your MVP packetSection 3.7 · Main task · pp. 43–45

The zero-product property says that if \(AB=0\), then \(A=0\) or \(B=0\). This is why factoring can turn one quadratic equation into two simple equations.

Factoring procedure

1. Move every term to one side so the other side is zero.

2. Factor completely, taking out a greatest common factor if needed.

3. Set each factor equal to zero.

4. Solve and check the roots.

Worked example 3 — Solve by factoring\[x^2-2x-15=0\] \[(x-5)(x+3)=0\] \[x-5=0\quad\text{or}\quad x+3=0\] \[\boxed{x=5\quad\text{or}\quad x=-3.}\]

Factoring is especially efficient here because the trinomial has simple integer factors.

Worked example 4 — A leading coefficient other than 1

Solve \(3x^2+14x-5=0\). Split the middle term using 15 and −1:

\[3x^2+15x-x-5=0\] \[3x(x+5)-1(x+5)=0\] \[(3x-1)(x+5)=0\] \[\boxed{x=\frac13\quad\text{or}\quad x=-5.}\]

Notice that \(3x-1=0\) gives \(x=1/3\), not 1.

4. Tia: complete the square

In your MVP packetSection 3.7 · Main task · pp. 43–45

Completing the square rewrites part of a quadratic as the square of a binomial. Then the square-root property isolates x. Both the positive and negative roots are necessary whenever the right side is positive.

Completing-the-square steps for \(x^2+bx+c=0\)

1. Move the constant: \(x^2+bx=-c\).

2. Add \((b/2)^2\) to both sides.

3. Factor the perfect square on the left.

4. Take both square-root branches and solve for x.

Worked example 5 — Solve an equation that does not factor over integers

For \(x^2+4x-8=0\):

\[x^2+4x=8\] \[x^2+4x+4=12\] \[(x+2)^2=12\] \[x+2=\pm\sqrt{12}=\pm2\sqrt3\] \[\boxed{x=-2\pm2\sqrt3.}\]

Decimal approximations are about 1.464 and −5.464. The radical answers are exact.

Worked example 6 — First divide by a nonzero coefficient

Solve \(-2x^2+3x+1=0\). Divide by −2:

\[x^2-\frac32x-\frac12=0\] \[x^2-\frac32x=\frac12\] \[\left(x-\frac34\right)^2=\frac12+\frac9{16}=\frac{17}{16}\] \[x-\frac34=\pm\frac{\sqrt{17}}4\] \[\boxed{x=\frac{3\pm\sqrt{17}}4.}\]

Completing the square works even when factors are not integers. Keep fractions exact rather than rounding midway.

5. Tehani: use the quadratic formula

In your MVP packetSection 3.7 · Main task · pp. 43–45

The quadratic formula is obtained by completing the square in the general quadratic equation. For real-coefficient equations where the expression under the square root is nonnegative, it returns every real root.

Quadratic formula

First arrange the equation as \(ax^2+bx+c=0\), with \(a\ne0\). Identify the coefficients carefully.

\[\boxed{x=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}}\]

The expression \(b^2-4ac\) is called the discriminant. If it is positive, there are two distinct real roots; if it is zero, there is one repeated real root. If it is negative, there are no real roots; the module addresses the broader number system later.

Worked example 7 — Formula with a negative leading coefficient

In \(-2x^2+3x+1=0\), \(a=-2\), \(b=3\), \(c=1\):

\[x=\frac{-3\pm\sqrt{3^2-4(-2)(1)}}{2(-2)}\] \[=\frac{-3\pm\sqrt{17}}{-4}=\boxed{\frac{3\pm\sqrt{17}}4}.\]

The two signs represent the same pair of solutions after reversing the sign of the denominator. This agrees with completing the square.

Worked example 8 — Apply the formula to a nonfactorable quadratic\[x^2+4x-8=0\quad(a=1,\ b=4,\ c=-8)\] \[x=\frac{-4\pm\sqrt{16+32}}2=\frac{-4\pm4\sqrt3}2=\boxed{-2\pm2\sqrt3}.\]

Check agreement with Worked Example 5: methods change the work, not the solution set.

Worked example 9 — Compare three packet equations

Equation 1: \(x^2-2x-15=0\). Factoring gives \(x=-3,5\).

Equation 2: \(-2x^2+3x+1=0\). Formula or completing the square gives \(x=(3\pm\sqrt{17})/4\).

Equation 3: \(x^2+4x-8=0\). Completing the square or formula gives \(x=-2\pm2\sqrt3\).

In the packet, students are asked to demonstrate all five methods for each equation. Use the table or graph to estimate the irrational roots; use algebra to obtain the exact answers.

7. The main-task challenge: where two graphs meet

In your MVP packetSection 3.7 · Main task · pp. 43–45

A solution of a system is an ordered pair that satisfies both equations. For a line and parabola, equate their y-expressions. Their common inputs solve a quadratic equation, and substitution gives the corresponding outputs.

Worked example 10 — Main-task quadratic–linear system

Find the intersections:

\[y=x^2-4x+1\qquad y=x-3.\]

Set outputs equal:

\[x^2-4x+1=x-3\] \[x^2-5x+4=0\] \[(x-1)(x-4)=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x=1,4.\]

Use the line equation \(y=x-3\): at \(x=1\), \(y=-2\); at \(x=4\), \(y=1\).

\[\boxed{(1,-2)\ \text{and}\ (4,1).}\]

Check in the parabola: \(1-4+1=-2\) and \(16-16+1=1\). The table and graph methods can confirm these points; factoring, completing the square, or the formula solves the resulting quadratic.

Method for a linear–quadratic system

1. Isolate the same variable (usually y) in both equations.

2. Equate expressions and solve the resulting quadratic.

3. Substitute each x-value into either original equation to find y.

4. Check each ordered pair in both original equations.

8. Ready — symmetry and the maximum area of rectangles

In your MVP packetSection 3.7 · Ready · Problems 1–4 · p. 46

If fencing encloses a rectangle of fixed perimeter, increasing one side forces the other to decrease. The area functions in Ready have the form \(A(x)=x(S-x)\), with \(0\le x\le S\). This is a downward-opening quadratic.

Worked example 11 — Evaluate a rectangular area model

Suppose \(A(x)=x(18-x)\). Then

\[A(5)=5(18-5)=65,\qquad A(13)=13(18-13)=65.\]

The two equal areas occur at x-values equally far from 9. At \(x=0\) or \(x=18\), the area is zero.

Worked example 12 — Find the maximum using a square\[A(x)=x(18-x)=-x^2+18x\] \[=-(x-9)^2+81.\]

Since \((x-9)^2\ge0\), the area is at most 81. The maximum is \(81\) square units when \(x=9\), a 9-by-9 square. The symmetry line is \(x=9\). For the packet’s four Ready problems, apply the same method with \(S=10,50,75,48\) respectively.

Area maximum when \(A(x)=x(S-x)\)

The two sides sum to S, so the vertex is at \(x=S/2\), where the sides are equal.

\[\boxed{x=\frac S2}\] \[\boxed{A_{\max}=\frac{S^2}{4}}\]

The function’s x-intercepts are \(0\) and \(S\). This result comes from completing the square or the symmetry of the parabola; it is not a rule for arbitrary quadratics.

9. Set — solve efficiently, then explain the method

In your MVP packetSection 3.7 · Set · Problems 5–13 · p. 47

Problems 5–10 require choosing an effective method and using at least three different methods across the assignment. Problems 11–13 require a written description of completing the square, factoring, and the quadratic formula. The examples below model the level of explanation expected.

Worked example 13 — Factoring is quickest\[x^2+13x+40=0\] \[(x+5)(x+8)=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\boxed{x=-5,-8}.\]

Because 5 and 8 multiply to 40 and add to 13, factoring is direct.

Worked example 14 — Completing the square is direct\[x^2-10x=-7\] \[x^2-10x+25=18\] \[(x-5)^2=18\] \[\boxed{x=5\pm3\sqrt2}.\]

Here the coefficient of \(x^2\) is 1 and the middle coefficient is even, so a perfect square appears quickly.

Worked example 15 — Use the formula for less convenient factors

Solve \(2x^2+5x-4=0\):

\[x=\frac{-5\pm\sqrt{5^2-4(2)(-4)}}{2(2)}=\boxed{\frac{-5\pm\sqrt{57}}4}.\]

The discriminant 57 is not a perfect square. Exact radicals communicate the solutions without premature rounding.

Set writing tip. Do not merely list the names of the methods. For each, explain its key algebraic justification: zero-product property for factoring, equal changes to both sides for completing the square, and substitution into a general valid formula for the quadratic formula.

10. Go — graphs, intercepts, and systems

In your MVP packetSection 3.7 · Go · Problems 14–19 · pp. 47–48

To graph \(f(x)=ax^2+bx+c\), find its axis of symmetry \(x=-b/(2a)\), compute the vertex output, find the y-intercept \((0,c)\), and solve \(f(x)=0\) for x-intercepts. Sketch the parabola with symmetry and its opening direction.

Worked example 16 — Quadratic graph features

Consider the new function \(f(x)=x^2-6x+5\):

\[f(x)=(x-3)^2-4=(x-1)(x-5).\]

Axis: \(x=3\). Vertex: \((3,-4)\). x-intercepts: \((1,0)\), \((5,0)\). y-intercept: \((0,5)\). The parabola opens upward.

For the Go homework’s systems, distinguish linear–linear systems (usually eliminate or substitute) from linear–quadratic systems (substitute and then solve the quadratic). Always check ordered pairs.

Additional Go problem A — straightforward linear system

Solve \(2x+y=11\) and \(x-y=1\).

Add equations to eliminate y: \(3x=12\), so \(x=4\). Substitute: \(4-y=1\), so \(y=3\).

\[\boxed{(4,3)}\]

Check: \(2(4)+3=11\) and \(4-3=1\).

Additional Go problem B — harder linear–quadratic intersection

Solve \(y=x^2-6x+6\) and \(y=2x-9\).

\[x^2-6x+6=2x-9\] \[x^2-8x+15=0\] \[(x-3)(x-5)=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x=3,5.\]

Substitute into the line: \(y(3)=-3\) and \(y(5)=1\). Thus:

\[\boxed{(3,-3)\ \text{and}\ (5,1)}.\]

Check in the quadratic: \(9-18+6=-3\) and \(25-30+6=1\).

Additional Go problem C — another line–parabola system

Solve \(y=x^2+2x-4\) and \(y=x+2\).

\[x^2+2x-4=x+2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x^2+x-6=0\] \[(x+3)(x-2)=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x=-3,2.\]

The corresponding y-values from \(y=x+2\) are −1 and 4. Hence \(\boxed{(-3,-1),(2,4)}\).

Additional Go problem D — quadratic graph with irrational x-intercepts

Sketch \(g(x)=x^2-4x-2\) and name its features.

\[g(x)=(x-2)^2-6.\]

Its axis is \(x=2\), vertex \((2,-6)\), y-intercept \((0,-2)\). Solve \((x-2)^2=6\): the x-intercepts are \((2-\sqrt6,0)\) and \((2+\sqrt6,0)\). Their approximate x-values are −0.449 and 4.449.

Teacher’s review (teachers and parents)

Main task, use of tables and graphs: The packet asks students to “demonstrate” all five methods on equations whose roots are sometimes irrational. Without a stated precision standard, numerical tables and hand-sketched graphs cannot supply the exact irrational roots that the algebraic methods do. A clearer direction is: “Use tables and graphs to estimate the solutions, and algebraic methods to find exact solutions when possible.”
