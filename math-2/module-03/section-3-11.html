In previous sections you solved quadratic equations such as \(x^2-5x+6=0\). Today you solve quadratic inequalities, which often have infinitely many solutions. The familiar skills of factoring, graphing, and completing the square will do most of the work.

From a quadratic equation to a quadratic inequality

In your MVP packetSection 3.11 · Main task · Problems 1–10 · pp. 70–72

An equation asks, “Where are two quantities equal?” The equation \(x^2-5x+6=0\) is true only when \(x=2\) or \(x=3\). An inequality asks, “For which inputs is one quantity larger or smaller?” For example, \(x^2-5x+6<0\) asks where the corresponding graph is below the x-axis.

Factor: \(x^2-5x+6=(x-2)(x-3)\). Its zeroes, \(2\) and \(3\), divide the number line into three intervals. On an interval that contains no zeros, neither factor changes sign, so their product keeps the same sign there.

Worked example 1 — Find the interval, not just the roots

Solve \(x^2-5x+6<0\).

Step 1. Find boundary values by solving \((x-2)(x-3)=0\): \(x=2,3\).

Step 2. Test the three intervals. For \(x=0\), \((-2)(-3)=6>0\). For \(x=2.5\), \((0.5)(-0.5)=-0.25<0\). For \(x=4\), \((2)(1)=2>0\).

Step 3. We need the negative interval, giving \(\boxed{2<x<3}\). The endpoints are excluded because the inequality is strict.

Strict inequality: \(<\) or \(>\)Use open endpoints; the boundary roots do not satisfy the inequality.
Inclusive inequality: \(\le\) or \(\ge\)Include boundary roots where the expression is defined.
Meaning of “and”For \(2<x<3\), both conditions must hold, so the answer is one interval.
Meaning of “or”For \(x\le-5\) or \(x\ge2\), the answer consists of two separate intervals.

A reliable procedure for quadratic inequalities

In your MVP packetSection 3.11 · Main task · Problems 1–10 · pp. 70–72

The idea is the same whether you use a sign chart, a sketch, or a table. Put everything on one side, find the zeros of the corresponding quadratic, then determine the sign of the quadratic in each interval.

Step 1 — RearrangeWrite the inequality with \(0\) on one side.
Step 2 — Find boundary rootsFactor, complete the square, or use the quadratic formula to solve the corresponding equation.
Step 3 — Determine signsTest one value in each interval, or use the parabola’s position above/below the x-axis.
Step 4 — Choose endpointsInclude equality only for \(\le\) and \(\ge\).
Step 5 — Apply contextRestrict to physically meaningful prices, lengths, times, or quantities when the problem supplies such restrictions.
Worked example 2 — Outside the roots

Solve \(x^2+3x-10\ge0\).

Factor: \((x+5)(x-2)\ge0\). The zeros are \(-5\) and \(2\). Outside the roots, both factors are negative (to the far left) or both positive (to the far right), so the product is nonnegative. Between the roots the product is negative.

\(\boxed{x\le-5\quad\text{or}\quad x\ge2}\).

Worked example 3 — Moving a constant across

Solve \(2x^2-5x<12\).

First subtract 12: \(2x^2-5x-12<0\). Factor: \((2x+3)(x-4)<0\). The zeros are \(-\frac32\) and \(4\). Test \(x=0\): \((3)(-4)=-12<0\); the graph lies below zero between the roots.

\(\boxed{-\frac32<x<4}\).

Common mistake. Solving only \(2x^2-5x-12=0\) finds the boundaries, not the inequality’s solution. After finding roots, always determine which intervals satisfy the requested sign.

Quadratic inequalities in a revenue problem

In your MVP packetSection 3.11 · Main task · Problems 1–10 · pp. 70–72

Carlos and Clarita’s driveway-mascot situation is revisited in the packet. They can sell 100 mascots at $20 each, but sell 10 fewer for every $5 price increase. Let \(x\) be the number of $5 price increases. Thus the price is \(20+5x\) dollars, the quantity is \(100-10x\), and the revenue is

\[R(x)=(100-10x)(20+5x)=-50x^2+300x+2000.\]

To model nonnegative prices and quantities, the meaningful interval is \(-4\le x\le10\). If “price increase” is meant literally, add \(x\ge0\); if only whole $5 increments are allowed, \(x\) must also be an integer. We will first treat \(x\) as continuous, as the packet’s graphs suggest, and then explain the discrete interpretation.

Worked example 4 — Meeting an exact revenue target

The twins want $2,000 in profit after $250 in supplies, so they need $2,250 in revenue. Solve

\[(100-10x)(20+5x)=2250.\]

Expand, subtract 2250, and divide by \(-50\):

\[-50x^2+300x-250=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x^2-6x+5=0.\]

Factor: \((x-1)(x-5)=0\), so \(x=1\) or \(x=5\). The two prices are \(20+5(1)=\$25\) and \(20+5(5)=\$45\). Both produce exactly $2,250.

Worked example 5 — At least $2,250

“At least” means \(R(x)\ge2250\), not equality. Rearranging:

\[-50(x-1)(x-5)\ge0.\]

Divide by \(-50\), reversing the inequality:

\[(x-1)(x-5)\le0.\]

The product is nonpositive between its roots, inclusive: \(\boxed{1\le x\le5}\). The possible prices therefore run from \(\boxed{\$25\text{ to }\$45}\), inclusive, under the continuous model. For whole $5 increments the allowable prices are $25, $30, $35, $40, and $45.

Worked example 6 — Maximize revenue

Complete the square:

\[R(x)=-50(x^2-6x)+2000=-50(x-3)^2+2450.\]

Since a square is never negative, \(-50(x-3)^2\le0\). Revenue is largest at \(x=3\), for a price of \(20+5(3)=\$35\). Maximum revenue is \(\boxed{\$2450}\); after $250 in supply costs, profit is \(\boxed{\$2200}\).

Quadratic inequalities with geometric constraints

In your MVP packetSection 3.11 · Main task · Problems 1–10 · pp. 70–72

Suppose a rectangular logo has width \(x\) inches and length \(x+8\) inches. Its area is \(A=x(x+8)\). The requirement “less than 48 square inches” translates to \(x(x+8)<48\).

Worked example 7 — Solve first, then restrict to valid dimensions

Move all terms left: \(x^2+8x-48<0\). Factor:

\[(x+12)(x-4)<0.\]

This is negative between the zeros, giving \(-12<x<4\) as a purely algebraic solution. But a physical width must be positive. Intersect with \(x>0\):

\(\boxed{0<x<4}\) inches; the length \(x+8\) satisfies \(\boxed{8<x+8<12}\) inches. Each pair is linked by \(\text{length}=\text{width}+8\); the width and length cannot be chosen independently.

Packet clarification — Section 3.11, Problems 5–7

Problem 5 asks about a physical rectangle, so negative or zero widths must be excluded. Problem 6 explicitly removes the physical context: its answer is \(-12<x<4\), whereas the dimensions in Problem 5 require \(0<x<4\). In both cases “less than” excludes an area exactly equal to 48 square inches.

Compare a parabola to a line in two equivalent ways

In your MVP packetSection 3.11 · Main task (page headed Section 3.11H) · Problems 11–12 · p. 73

Not every inequality begins with zero on one side. For example, \(x^2-4\le4x+1\) asks where the quadratic expression is no greater than the linear expression. Two graphs can be compared directly, or the expressions can be combined to create a single quadratic.

Worked example 8 — Carlos’s method: one difference graph

Subtract the right side to create \(x^2-4x-5\le0\). Factor:

\[(x-5)(x+1)\le0.\]

The graph \(y=x^2-4x-5\) is on or below the x-axis between the roots: \(\boxed{-1\le x\le5}\).

Worked example 9 — Clarita’s method: two graphs

Graph \(y=x^2-4\) and \(y=4x+1\). The graphs meet where \(x^2-4=4x+1\), or \((x-5)(x+1)=0\), at \(x=-1\) and \(x=5\). Between them, the parabola lies below the line (at \(x=0\), \(-4<1\)); include the intersection inputs because the inequality uses \(\le\). Again, \(\boxed{-1\le x\le5}\).

Keep the graph question straight. For an inequality in \(x\) alone, the solution is a set of x-values. When the homework uses \(y<f(x)\) or \(y>f(x)\), it instead asks for an entire shaded region in the xy-plane.

2. Ready — Factor polynomials completely (Problems 1–10)

In your MVP packetSection 3.11 · Ready · Problems 1–10 · p. 74

Factoring translates a quadratic into a product and makes its zeros visible. For a monic trinomial \(x^2+bx+c\), seek numbers \(m,n\) with \(m+n=b\) and \(mn=c\). For \(ax^2+bx+c\), where \(a\ne1\), use the product \(ac\), split the middle term, and factor by grouping. Check by distributing.

Worked example 10 — Leading coefficient 1

Factor \(x^2-4x-21\). Find numbers whose product is \(-21\) and sum is \(-4\): \(-7,3\). Therefore \(\boxed{(x-7)(x+3)}\).

Worked example 11 — Leading coefficient other than 1

Factor \(3x^2+7x-6\). Since \(ac=-18\), use \(9\) and \(-2\):

\[3x^2+9x-2x-6=3x(x+3)-2(x+3)=(3x-2)(x+3).\]

The product is fully factored. Its zeroes \(\frac23\) and \(-3\) are the graph’s x-intercepts.

Worked example 12 — Perfect-square trinomial

Factor \(x^2+10x+25\). Since \(25=5^2\) and \(10x=2(5)x\), \(\boxed{(x+5)^2}\). The corresponding parabola touches the x-axis once at \(-5\), rather than crossing it.

Factored form and x-interceptsFor \(f(x)=a(x-r_1)(x-r_2)\), the x-intercepts occur at \(x=r_1\) and \(x=r_2\), provided these roots are real.
Factored form and symmetryWhen there are two real roots, their midpoint \(x=\frac{r_1+r_2}{2}\) is the axis of symmetry.

3. Set — Solve inequalities and match shaded graphs (Problems 11–23)

In your MVP packetSection 3.11 · Set · Problems 11–23 · pp. 74–75

For the shared ideas used in these exercises, revisit the In class explanation.

Problems 11–19 ask for intervals of inputs. They include both product signs and inequalities such as \(x^2\le25\), which can be factored by difference of squares.

Worked example 13 — An inclusive interval

Solve \(x^2\le36\):

\[x^2-36\le0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad(x-6)(x+6)\le0.\]

The product is nonpositive between the roots: \(\boxed{-6\le x\le6}\).

Worked example 14 — A strict interval

Solve \(3x^2-12x-15<0\). Factor \(3(x-5)(x+1)<0\). Since 3 is positive, the product is negative between \(-1\) and \(5\): \(\boxed{-1<x<5}\).

Problems 20–23 ask you to match two-variable inequalities to graphs. The boundary is the parabola \(y=f(x)\); the solution consists of all points above it for \(y>f(x)\) or below it for \(y<f(x)\). A strict inequality uses a dashed boundary.

Shading above the curve\(y>f(x)\) means points whose y-values are greater than the curve’s y-values at the same x.
Shading below the curve\(y<f(x)\) means points whose y-values are less than the curve’s y-values at the same x.
Boundary styleDashed for \(<\) and \(>\); solid for \(\le\) and \(\ge\).
Worked example 15 — Reading a two-variable inequality graph

Sketch \(y>x^2-4\). Draw the upward-opening parabola with vertex \((0,-4)\), passing through \((-2,0)\) and \((2,0)\). Make the curve dashed, because equality is excluded. Shade the region above the curve. To check, \((0,0)\) is a solution because \(0>-4\).

Packet clarification — Section 3.11, Set, Problems 20–23

The matching graphs show shaded regions in two dimensions, not solution intervals on the x-axis. The boundaries are dashed. Compare (1) whether the parabola opens up or down, (2) its vertex and intercepts, and (3) whether the shading is above or below the boundary. In the provided graphs the matches are 20 → b, 21 → c, 22 → d, and 23 → a.

4. Go — Write quadratic functions in vertex form (Problems 24–29)

In your MVP packetSection 3.11 · Go · Problems 24–29 · p. 76

The homework finishes with a familiar skill from earlier modules: turning standard or factored form into vertex form, \(f(x)=a(x-h)^2+k\). The vertex is \((h,k)\). To rewrite an expression, complete the square carefully; remember to factor out the leading coefficient before completing the square when it is not 1.

Worked example 16 — A monic quadratic

Rewrite \(f(x)=x^2+8x+11\). Half of 8 is 4; its square is 16:

\[f(x)=(x^2+8x+16)-16+11=\boxed{(x+4)^2-5}.\]

The vertex is \((-4,-5)\).

Worked example 17 — From factored form

Rewrite \(g(x)=(x-4)(x+2)\). Expand, then complete the square:

\[g(x)=x^2-2x-8=(x^2-2x+1)-9=\boxed{(x-1)^2-9}.\]

Check: the vertex x-coordinate is also the midpoint of the roots 4 and −2, namely 1.

Worked example 18 — A leading coefficient of 3

Rewrite \(h(x)=3x^2+18x+14\). Factor out 3 from the variable terms:

\[h(x)=3(x^2+6x)+14.\]

Complete the square inside: \(x^2+6x=(x+3)^2-9\).

\[h(x)=3[(x+3)^2-9]+14=\boxed{3(x+3)^2-13}.\]

The vertex is \((-3,-13)\). Subtracting 9 outside the parentheses would give the wrong constant because the bracket is multiplied by 3.

New, fully worked Go practice

In your MVP packetSection 3.11 · Go · Problems 24–29 · p. 76
Problem A — Straightforward

Convert \(p(x)=x^2-10x+21\) to vertex form and identify its vertex.

Solution: Half of \(-10\) is \(-5\); square it to get 25. Add and subtract 25:

\[p(x)=x^2-10x+25-25+21=\boxed{(x-5)^2-4}.\]

Vertex: \(\boxed{(5,-4)}\). Verification: \((x-5)^2-4=x^2-10x+25-4\).

Problem B — More challenging

Convert \(q(x)=-2x^2+12x-7\) into vertex form. State whether the vertex gives a maximum or a minimum.

Solution: Factor out \(-2\) from the quadratic and linear terms:

\[q(x)=-2(x^2-6x)-7.\]

Now \(x^2-6x=(x-3)^2-9\), so

\[q(x)=-2[(x-3)^2-9]-7=\boxed{-2(x-3)^2+11}.\]

The vertex is \((3,11)\); it is a maximum because the coefficient of the square is negative.

Problem C — From two roots

Convert \(r(x)=2(x-1)(x-7)\) to vertex form.

Solution: The symmetry line lies halfway between roots 1 and 7, so \(x=4\). Evaluate \(r(4)=2(3)(-3)=-18\). Therefore, with leading coefficient 2,

\[\boxed{r(x)=2(x-4)^2-18}.\]

Check by expanding: \(2(x^2-8x+16)-18=2x^2-16x+14=2(x-1)(x-7)\).

Problem D — Fractional coefficients

Convert \(s(x)=\frac12x^2-3x+8\) to vertex form.

Solution: Factor \(\frac12\): \(s(x)=\frac12(x^2-6x)+8\). Complete the square with 9:

\[s(x)=\frac12[(x-3)^2-9]+8=\boxed{\frac12(x-3)^2+\frac72}.\]

The vertex is \(\left(3,\frac72\right)\).

Three easy-to-miss details: (1) Rearrange before solving; (2) when multiplying or dividing by a negative number, reverse the inequality sign; (3) even when a formula gives mathematically valid roots, real-world quantities can impose stricter bounds.

6. Teacher’s review (for teachers and parents)

No serious mathematical errors in the assigned Section 3.11 problems were identified. Two scope distinctions are worth making explicit for students:

Main-task Problems 5–7: Problem 5’s dimensions require \(x>0\); Problem 6 deliberately drops this physical restriction. Students should distinguish the algebraic and contextual solution sets.

Main-task Problems 1–4: Treating the count of “additional $5” increases as continuous gives price intervals and a vertex; if sales price may change only in increments of $5, use discrete admissible prices. A clearer prompt would state explicitly whether fractional values of \(x\) are allowed.
