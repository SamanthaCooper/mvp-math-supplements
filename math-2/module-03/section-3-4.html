Previously you learned that fractional exponents describe roots, and exponent laws continue to hold for positive bases. This lesson turns that understanding into a dependable way to simplify expressions and solve equations. We will use both notations for the same mathematics.

1. A root reverses a power

In your MVP packetSection 3.4 · Main task · PDF pp. 22–26 (no printed page numbers)

Since \(3^3=27\), the number whose cube is 27 is 3. We write \(\sqrt[3]{27}=3\). Since \(5^2=25\), the principal square root of 25 is \(\sqrt{25}=5\). The small raised number on a radical is its index; the expression underneath is its radicand.

OperationExampleWhy
Square root\(\sqrt{49}=7\)\(7^2=49\)
Cube root\(\sqrt[3]{-8}=-2\)\((-2)^3=-8\)
Fourth root\(\sqrt[4]{81}=3\)\(3^4=81\); the radical means the nonnegative root

A fractional exponent describes the same operation: \(a^{1/n}=\sqrt[n]{a}\), and \(a^{m/n}=\sqrt[n]{a^m}=(\sqrt[n]{a})^m\) when the real expressions are defined (we generally assume \(a>0\) for the algebraic rules below). The numerator gives the power; the denominator gives the root.

Worked example 1 — Translate the notation

Evaluate \(64^{2/3}\). Take a cube root, then square:

\[64^{2/3}=(\sqrt[3]{64})^2=4^2=16.\]

The order can also be reversed: \(\sqrt[3]{64^2}=16\). The answer is unchanged.

The sign matters. \(\sqrt{x^2}=|x|\), not automatically \(x\). For instance, \(\sqrt{(-6)^2}=6\). By contrast, odd roots preserve negative signs: \(\sqrt[3]{x^3}=x\) for every real \(x\). We can write \(\sqrt{x^2}=x\) only when \(x\ge 0\).

2. Radical properties and exponent properties express the same rules

In your MVP packetSection 3.4 · Main task · PDF pp. 22–26 (no printed page numbers)

For nonnegative \(a,b\) and a positive integer index \(n>1\), roots of products can be taken factor by factor. For quotients, assume the denominator is positive. When simplifying with even roots and variables, check signs before removing a root.

Root and exponent rules (displayed separately)

Root of a product:

\[\sqrt[n]{ab}=\sqrt[n]{a}\sqrt[n]{b}.\]

Root of a quotient:

\[\sqrt[n]{\frac ab}=\frac{\sqrt[n]a}{\sqrt[n]b}\quad(b>0).\]

Rational exponent:

\[a^{m/n}=\sqrt[n]{a^m}.\]

Product of powers:

\[a^r a^s=a^{r+s}.\]

Power of a power:

\[(a^r)^s=a^{rs}.\]

Power of a product:

\[(ab)^r=a^rb^r.\]

Power of a quotient:

\[\left(\frac ab\right)^r=\frac{a^r}{b^r}.\]

Quotient of powers:

\[\frac{a^r}{a^s}=a^{r-s}.\]

Negative exponent:

\[a^{-r}=\frac1{a^r}.\]

For the exponent identities with arbitrary rational exponents, assume \(a,b>0\). Specific odd-root identities also extend to negative inputs, but even roots need sign and domain restrictions.

Worked example 2 — The same simplification two ways

Find \(\sqrt[3]{54}\) by factoring a perfect cube.

Radical method: \(54=27\cdot2\), so

\[\sqrt[3]{54}=\sqrt[3]{27}\sqrt[3]2=3\sqrt[3]2.\]

Exponent method:

\[54^{1/3}=(27\cdot2)^{1/3}=27^{1/3}2^{1/3}=3\cdot2^{1/3}.\]

These are two notations for the same number.

3. Simplify radicals by extracting perfect powers

In your MVP packetSection 3.4 · Main task · PDF pp. 22–26 (no printed page numbers)

The general strategy is to separate factors with exponents divisible by the index. For cube roots, collect groups of three matching factors; for fourth roots, collect groups of four. What remains stays inside the radical.

Worked example 3 — Variables under a cube root

Simplify \(\sqrt[3]{x^8}\), assuming \(x\) is real.

\[\sqrt[3]{x^8}=\sqrt[3]{x^6x^2}=\sqrt[3]{(x^2)^3}\sqrt[3]{x^2}=x^2\sqrt[3]{x^2}.\]

In exponent notation: \(x^{8/3}=x^{6/3+2/3}=x^2x^{2/3}\). This is valid even for negative \(x\), since \(x^2\) is nonnegative.

Worked example 4 — A cube root with two variables

Assume \(x,y>0\). Simplify \(\sqrt[3]{40x^7y^5}\).

\[\begin{aligned}\sqrt[3]{40x^7y^5}&=\sqrt[3]{8x^6y^3\cdot5xy^2}\\&=2x^2y\sqrt[3]{5xy^2}.\end{aligned}\]

The exponent method divides the exponents by 3: \(40^{1/3}x^{7/3}y^{5/3}=2x^2y\,(5xy^2)^{1/3}\).

Worked example 5 — An even root requires absolute value

Simplify \(\sqrt{18x^6}\), for real \(x\).

\[\sqrt{18x^6}=\sqrt{9\cdot2}\sqrt{(x^3)^2}=3|x^3|\sqrt2.\]

When the problem expressly assumes \(x\ge0\), this becomes \(3x^3\sqrt2\). Without that assumption, dropping the absolute-value bars can make the answer wrong.

Worked example 6 — Simplifying a root of a quotient

Assume \(x,y>0\). Simplify \(\sqrt[3]{\frac{128x^{10}}{8y^6}}\).

\[\begin{aligned}\sqrt[3]{\frac{128x^{10}}{8y^6}}&=\sqrt[3]{\frac{16x^9x}{y^6}}\\&=\frac{2x^3\sqrt[3]{2x}}{y^2}.\end{aligned}\]

Check the numerical factor: \(\sqrt[3]{16}=2\sqrt[3]2\). Keeping perfect cubes separate avoids a frequent arithmetic error.

Do not split addition inside a root. In general \(\sqrt{a+b}\ne\sqrt a+\sqrt b\). For example, \(\sqrt{9+16}=5\), while \(\sqrt9+\sqrt{16}=7\). Only product and quotient rules were used above.

4. Solving equations with powers: odd versus even

In your MVP packetSection 3.4 · Main task · PDF pp. 22–26 (no printed page numbers)

To solve \(u^n=k\), first isolate the power expression. When \(n\) is odd, a real right side has exactly one real \(n\)th root. When \(n\) is even and \(k>0\), there are two real possibilities: \(u=\sqrt[n]k\) or \(u=-\sqrt[n]k\).

Worked example 7 — An odd power has one real solution

Solve \((x+6)^3=-125\).

\[x+6=\sqrt[3]{-125}=-5,\qquad x=-11.\]

Substitute: \((-11+6)^3=(-5)^3=-125\). There is no plus/minus sign for odd powers.

Worked example 8 — An even power has two possible roots

Solve \((x-1)^2=18\).

\[x-1=\pm\sqrt{18}=\pm3\sqrt2,\qquad\boxed{x=1\pm3\sqrt2}.\]

Both solutions work because squaring either \(+3\sqrt2\) or \(-3\sqrt2\) gives 18. Writing \(x-1=\sqrt{18}\) alone would lose a solution.

Worked example 9 — Isolate a squared expression first

Solve \(5(x+2)^2=45\).

\[\begin{aligned}(x+2)^2&=9\\x+2&=\pm3\\x&=-2\pm3.\end{aligned}\]

Thus \(\boxed{x=1\text{ or }x=-5}\). The equation has two answers because both \(3^2\) and \((-3)^2\) equal 9.

Worked example 10 — A fifth power with an outside coefficient

Solve \(3(x-4)^5-2=94\).

\[3(x-4)^5=96,\quad (x-4)^5=32,\quad x-4=\sqrt[5]{32}=2,\quad\boxed{x=6}.\]

Check: \(3(2)^5-2=94\). Odd powers do not require a second sign.

5. A graph reveals whether a solution was missed

In your MVP packetSection 3.4 · Main task · PDF pp. 22–26 (no printed page numbers)

An equation \(L(x)=R(x)\) can be viewed as the intersection of the graphs \(y=L(x)\) and \(y=R(x)\). This is a check on an algebraic solution, not a replacement for finding exact values.

For example, \((x-1)^2=9\) means finding where the parabola \(y=(x-1)^2\) meets the horizontal line \(y=9\). The parabola reaches that height once on either side of its vertex, at \(x=-2\) and \(x=4\). A cube-shaped curve such as \(y=(x-1)^3\) instead crosses a horizontal line only once.

6. READY — Recognize and factor quadratic expressions (1–12)

In your MVP packetSection 3.4 · Ready · Problems 1–12 · p. 24

A quadratic in standard form is \(ax^2+bx+c=0\), where \(a\ne0\). The three coefficients include their signs. A missing \(x\) term has coefficient \(b=0\); a missing constant has \(c=0\).

Worked example 11 — Identify coefficients precisely

In \(4x^2-9x+7=0\), we have \(a=4\), \(b=-9\), and \(c=7\). In \(-x^2+6=0\), \(a=-1\), \(b=0\), \(c=6\).

To factor \(x^2+bx+c\), find two numbers whose product is \(c\) and whose sum is \(b\). Then write \((x+m)(x+n)\).

Worked example 12 — Factor a monic quadratic

Factor \(x^2-2x-15\). We want a product of \(-15\) and a sum of \(-2\). The numbers are \(-5\) and 3:

\[x^2-2x-15=(x-5)(x+3).\]

Distribute to verify: \(x^2+3x-5x-15=x^2-2x-15\).

Worked example 13 — Factor when the leading coefficient is not 1

Factor \(3x^2+11x+6\). Multiply \(ac=3\cdot6=18\), then split the middle coefficient into 9 and 2:

\[\begin{aligned}3x^2+11x+6&=3x^2+9x+2x+6\\&=3x(x+3)+2(x+3)\\&=(3x+2)(x+3).\end{aligned}\]

Factoring is a review skill needed for Ready, not a new quadratic-solving technique for later packet sections.

Worked example 14 — Recognize a perfect-square trinomial\[x^2-10x+25=(x-5)^2,\]

because \((x-5)(x-5)=x^2-10x+25\). The repeated factor creates a repeated x-intercept when the expression represents a function.

7. SET — Convert, simplify, and solve (13–22)

In your MVP packetSection 3.4 · Set · Problems 13–22 · p. 25

In Set 13–20, the two columns deliberately describe the same expression in radical and exponential form. Begin by translating between the two, then simplify the numerical powers and group variables according to the root index. If the packet supplies no sign conditions on variables with even roots, keep absolute values when appropriate.

Worked example 15 — Convert a product under one cube root

Translate and simplify \(\sqrt[3]{5^8\cdot2^4}\).

\[\begin{aligned}\sqrt[3]{5^8 2^4}&=5^{8/3}2^{4/3}\\&=5^2\cdot2\sqrt[3]{5^2\cdot2}\\&=50\sqrt[3]{50}.\end{aligned}\]

Each whole multiple of 3 can leave the cube root; the fractional part stays.

Worked example 16 — Convert variables, then simplify

Assuming positive \(x,y\), rewrite \(\sqrt[5]{x^{12}y^8}\).

\[x^{12/5}y^{8/5}=x^2y\sqrt[5]{x^2y^3}.\]

The exponents are split as \(12/5=2+2/5\) and \(8/5=1+3/5\). The simplified radical and the exponential expression are equivalent.

Worked example 17 — A quotient with fifth roots

For \(x,y>0\), simplify \(\sqrt[5]{\frac{64x^{12}}{2y^{10}}}\).

\[\sqrt[5]{32x^{12}y^{-10}}=\frac{2x^2\sqrt[5]{x^2}}{y^2}.\]

Dividing the numerical coefficients first makes the perfect fifth power, 32, visible.

Worked example 18 — Solve fourth- and odd-power equations

For \((x+3)^4=16\): take both fourth-root signs, giving \(x+3=\pm2\), so \(\boxed{x=-1,-5}\).

For \(2(x-1)^3+5=59\): subtract 5, divide by 2, then take the cube root:

\[(x-1)^3=27,\quad x-1=3,\quad\boxed{x=4}.\]

8. GO — Intercepts and sketches (23–26)

In your MVP packetSection 3.4 · Go · Problems 23–26 · p. 26

At an x-intercept, the output equals zero: solve \(f(x)=0\) and write points \((x,0)\). At the y-intercept, the input equals zero: compute \(f(0)\) and write \((0,f(0))\). The graph shape depends on the function family; the intercepts alone do not fully determine a sketch.

Quadratic functions: two, one, or no x-intercepts

In your MVP packetSection 3.4 · Go · Problems 23–26 · p. 26
Worked Go Problem A — Straightforward quadratic

Find the intercepts and sketch \(f(x)=(x+1)(x-3)\).

For x-intercepts, set each factor to zero: \(x=-1\) or \(x=3\); the intercepts are \((-1,0)\) and \((3,0)\). At \(x=0\), \(f(0)=1(-3)=-3\), so the y-intercept is \((0,-3)\). Because the coefficient of \(x^2\) is positive, the parabola opens upward. The symmetry line is halfway between \(-1\) and 3, namely \(x=1\); \(f(1)=-4\), so the vertex is \((1,-4)\).

Linear functions: a constant rate of change

In your MVP packetSection 3.4 · Go · Problems 23–26 · p. 26
Worked Go Problem B — A line

Find intercepts and sketch \(h(x)=-2(x+2)\).

For \(h(x)=0\), \(-2(x+2)=0\), so \(x=-2\). The x-intercept is \((-2,0)\). At \(x=0\), \(h(0)=-4\), so the y-intercept is \((0,-4)\). The slope is \(-2\); draw a decreasing straight line through these points.

Exponential functions: the output never reaches zero

In your MVP packetSection 3.4 · Go · Problems 23–26 · p. 26
Worked Go Problem C — Exponential function

Find intercepts and sketch \(g(x)=3\cdot2^x\).

Since \(2^x>0\) for every real \(x\), \(3\cdot2^x\) is always positive: no x-intercept. At \(x=0\), \(g(0)=3\), so the y-intercept is \((0,3)\). The curve grows to the right and approaches, but does not touch, the horizontal asymptote \(y=0\) to the left.

Worked Go Problem D — More challenging quadratic

Find intercepts and sketch \(k(x)=x^2-4\).

Set the output to zero: \(x^2-4=(x-2)(x+2)=0\), so \(x=-2,2\). Thus the x-intercepts are \((-2,0)\) and \((2,0)\). The y-intercept is \((0,-4)\), which is also the vertex. The graph is an upward-opening parabola symmetric about the y-axis.

Worked Go Problem E — A more challenging exponential

Find the intercepts of \(p(x)=7(3^{x-2})\) and explain its graph.

The output cannot equal zero because both 7 and \(3^{x-2}\) are positive, so there is no x-intercept. At zero:

\[p(0)=7\cdot3^{-2}=\frac79.\]

The y-intercept is \((0,7/9)\). The graph rises as \(x\) increases, goes through \((2,7)\), and approaches \(y=0\) to the left. Common error: mistakenly solving \(x-2=0\) to claim an x-intercept at 2; the exponent being zero makes the output 7, not zero.

Worked Go Problem F — A quadratic with one x-intercept

Find the intercepts of \(q(x)=2(x-3)^2\).

Set it equal to zero: \(2(x-3)^2=0\), so \(x=3\). This is a double root; the graph touches the x-axis at \((3,0)\), rather than crossing it there. At zero \(q(0)=18\), so the y-intercept is \((0,18)\).

9. Packet clarifications

Packet clarificationSection 3.4, main task: simplifying expressions with variables

The main task begins by stating radical identities for positive real inputs, but later displays variables in expressions without repeatedly specifying their signs. Some even-root extractions require an absolute value for unrestricted real inputs, such as \(\sqrt{x^2}=|x|\). Treat positive-variable simplifications as using an implicit nonnegative-variable convention; for unrestricted variables retain absolute values where mathematically required.

Packet clarificationSection 3.4, main task: the squared-equation examples and problems 1–3

Applying the principal square root to both sides of \(u^2=k\) actually yields \(|u|=\sqrt{k}\). Therefore solving the original equation requires \(u=\pm\sqrt{k}\), when \(k>0\). The one-sign solutions shown in the task are intentionally incomplete; both intersections and both algebraic solutions must be retained.

Teacher’s review (teachers / parents)

Section 3.4 main task, square-root worked examples and questions 1–3: The initial procedures take a square root and display only the positive choice even though the original quadratic equations have two real solutions. The packet later identifies this issue, but the intermediate steps are not equivalent to the original equations. A mathematically correct rewrite is \(|x-2|=\sqrt{50}\), hence \(x=2\pm\sqrt{50}\), and for \(9(x-3)^2=4\), \(x=3\pm\frac23\).

Section 3.4 main task and Set 17–20: Variable domains are not fully specified for even-index radicals and mixed fractional powers. Require positive variables when using the simplified forms of exponent rules for all rational powers; when working over unrestricted real variables, retain absolute values for even-root extraction and observe denominator nonzero requirements. This matters because \(\sqrt{x^2}\ne x\) for negative \(x\).
