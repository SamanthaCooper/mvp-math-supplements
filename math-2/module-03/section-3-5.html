Coverage

You already know that a parabola is symmetrical and that its x-intercepts occur wherever the function equals zero. When factoring is inconvenient, we can still find the intercepts exactly by working from the vertex. The distance to an intercept provides a useful bridge between graphing and algebra.

1. In class: A Develop Understanding Task

Revisit the ideas from class

This section explains some of the ideas you explored in class through the guided exercises. Use it alongside the “A Develop Understanding Task” portion of Section 3.5 in your MVP packet.

X-intercepts come in symmetric pairs

In your MVP packetSection 3.5 · Main task · Problems 1–8 · pp. 27–32

For a quadratic function, an x-intercept is a point \( (x,0) \) at which \(f(x)=0\). The axis of symmetry is the vertical line \(x=h\), passing through the vertex \((h,k)\). If the parabola crosses the x-axis at two points, the points have equal horizontal distance from this axis.

The symmetry pattern

When the intercepts exist, write them as two separate points:

\[ (h-d,0) \qquad\text{and}\qquad (h+d,0), \]

where \(d\) is the nonnegative horizontal distance from the axis \(x=h\) to either intercept. If \(d=0\), the points coincide.

Worked example 1 — A parabola whose roots are easy to find

Find the vertex, axis, and x-intercepts of \(f(x)=x^2-2x-3\).

Factor first: \(f(x)=(x-3)(x+1)\). Its roots are \(x=3\) and \(x=-1\); the axis lies halfway between them: \(h=(-1+3)/2=1\). The vertex height is \(f(1)=1-2-3=-4\), so the vertex is \((1,-4)\).

\[d=3-1=2,\quad h-d=-1,\quad h+d=3.\]

Both intercepts are 4 units above the vertex, because their y-coordinate is zero and the vertex's is \(-4\).

Completing the square reveals the vertex

In your MVP packetSection 3.5 · Main task · Problems 1–8 · pp. 27–32

The expression \(x^2+bx\) becomes part of a perfect square when we add \((b/2)^2\). We use that fact without changing the function's value by adding and subtracting the same number.

Worked example 2 — A quadratic with irrational intercepts

Rewrite \(f(x)=x^2-6x+4\) in vertex form.

\[\begin{aligned}x^2-6x+4&=(x^2-6x+9)-9+4\\&=(x-3)^2-5.\end{aligned}\]

Therefore the vertex is \((3,-5)\) and the axis is \(x=3\). For the intercepts, set the output to zero:

\[ (x-3)^2-5=0,\quad (x-3)^2=5,\quad x-3=\pm\sqrt5. \]\[\boxed{x=3-\sqrt5\quad\text{or}\quad x=3+\sqrt5}.\]

The intercept distance is \(d=\sqrt5\), approximately 2.236. The intercepts are approximately \(0.764\) and \(5.236\).

The same method works with a vertical stretch

In your MVP packetSection 3.5 · Main task · Problems 1–8 · pp. 27–32
Worked example 3 — Leading coefficient other than one

For \(f(x)=2x^2-8x+5\), factor 2 from the first two terms before completing the square:

\[\begin{aligned}2x^2-8x+5&=2(x^2-4x)+5\\&=2[(x-2)^2-4]+5\\&=2(x-2)^2-3.\end{aligned}\]

The vertex is \((2,-3)\). Set the function equal to zero:

\[2(x-2)^2-3=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad (x-2)^2=\frac32.\]\[\boxed{x=2\pm\sqrt{\frac32}=2\pm\frac{\sqrt6}{2}}.\]

The distance is \(d=\sqrt{3/2}\), approximately 1.225. Therefore the x-intercepts are approximately \(0.775\) and \(3.225\).

Why the ± sign is essential. If \(u^2=5\), then both \(u=\sqrt5\) and \(u=-\sqrt5\) work. The radical symbol alone indicates the principal (nonnegative) root; the equation has two solutions when the right-hand side is positive.

Develop the quadratic formula, not just memorize it

In your MVP packetSection 3.5 · Main task · Problems 1–8 · pp. 27–32

Take the general quadratic \(f(x)=ax^2+bx+c\), with \(a\ne0\). We want to solve \(ax^2+bx+c=0\). First divide through by \(a\), which is allowed because \(a\) is nonzero.

Worked example 4 — General derivation by completing the square\[\begin{aligned}ax^2+bx+c&=0\\x^2+\frac ba x&=-\frac ca\\x^2+\frac ba x+\left(\frac b{2a}\right)^2&=\left(\frac b{2a}\right)^2-\frac ca\\\left(x+\frac b{2a}\right)^2&=\frac{b^2-4ac}{4a^2}.\end{aligned}\]

When the numerator is nonnegative, take square roots on both sides:

\[\left|x+\frac b{2a}\right|=\frac{\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2|a|}.\]

Choosing both signs and rearranging gives the equivalent familiar formula (the ± sign absorbs the sign of \(a\)):

\[\boxed{x=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}}.\]

Notice that \(h=-b/(2a)\), so this formula has the same center as the symmetry axis.

Quadratic formula — for \(ax^2+bx+c=0\) with \(a\ne0\)\[\boxed{x=\frac{-b+\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}\quad\text{or}\quad x=\frac{-b-\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}}\]

Steps: Write the equation equal to zero; identify signed coefficients \(a,b,c\); substitute into each expression; simplify the square root and fraction when possible; check with substitution or the graph.

Worked example 5 — Apply the formula

Solve \(x^2-3x-1=0\). Here \(a=1\), \(b=-3\), and \(c=-1\).

\[\begin{aligned}x&=\frac{-(-3)\pm\sqrt{(-3)^2-4(1)(-1)}}{2(1)}\\&=\frac{3\pm\sqrt{9+4}}2\\&=\boxed{\frac{3\pm\sqrt{13}}2}.\end{aligned}\]

Approximations are \(x\approx-0.303\) and \(x\approx3.303\). Either can be checked by substitution; the exact answers use radicals.

When are there two real intercepts?

In your MVP packetSection 3.5 · Main task · Problems 1–8 · pp. 27–32

The quantity beneath the square root is \(b^2-4ac\), often called the discriminant. It tells whether the square root in the formula is real, and thus whether the parabola reaches the x-axis. This is a brief interpretation of today's formula; complex solutions belong to a later section.

Value of \(b^2-4ac\)What it means for the real x-intercepts
PositiveTwo distinct real x-intercepts
ZeroOne touching point (a repeated root)
NegativeNo real x-intercepts

Derive the same formula from symmetric intercepts

In your MVP packetSection 3.5 · Main task · Problems 1–8 · pp. 27–32

The packet's second derivation starts not by completing a square but by naming the axis \(x=h\) and its intercept distance \(d\). If real intercepts exist, the roots are \(h-d\) and \(h+d\). Thus

\[\begin{aligned}f(x)&=a[x-(h-d)][x-(h+d)]\\&=a[(x-h)+d][(x-h)-d]\\&=a[(x-h)^2-d^2]\\&=ax^2-2ahx+a(h^2-d^2).\end{aligned}\]

Compare coefficients with \(ax^2+bx+c\):

Coefficient matching — one equation at a time

Coefficient of \(x\):

\[b=-2ah.\]

Constant term:

\[c=a(h^2-d^2).\]

The first equation gives \(h=-b/(2a)\). Substitute that into the second:

\[\begin{aligned}d^2&=h^2-\frac ca\\&=\frac{b^2}{4a^2}-\frac{4ac}{4a^2}\\&=\frac{b^2-4ac}{4a^2}.\end{aligned}\]

Therefore, when the discriminant is nonnegative, \(d=\sqrt{b^2-4ac}/(2|a|)\). The two roots are \(h-d\) and \(h+d\), giving exactly the same pair as the quadratic formula. This derivation demonstrates why its solutions are balanced around \(-b/(2a)\).

Worked example 6 — A downward-opening parabola

Let \(f(x)=-x^2+4x+5\). Set it equal to zero, so \(a=-1\), \(b=4\), \(c=5\):

\[x=\frac{-4\pm\sqrt{4^2-4(-1)(5)}}{2(-1)}=\frac{-4\pm6}{-2}.\]

The roots are \(x=-1\) and \(x=5\). The axis is \(x=2\), the vertex is \((2,9)\), and the graph opens downward. The formula still works because its derivation never requires \(a>0\).

Packet clarification — Section 3.5, main task, Problems 5 and 7

These general derivations describe the location of real x-intercepts when they exist. Problem 7 begins with a factored form built from two intercepts, so its starting assumption requires \(b^2-4ac\ge0\). The quadratic formula itself still provides a useful algebraic expression when that quantity is negative, but such a parabola has no real x-intercepts.

2. READY — Convert units of area (Problems 1–6)

Study the worked examples first, then try the problems yourself. An answer key at the bottom of this section is provided to check your work.

In your MVP packetSection 3.5 · Ready · Problems 1–6 · p. 33

Area measures two-dimensional space. If a length conversion has factor \(k\), the area conversion has factor \(k^2\). Because one yard is three feet, a square yard contains a \(3\times3\) array of square feet: \(1\,\mathrm{yd}^2=9\,\mathrm{ft}^2\). For reverse conversions, divide by the square conversion factor.

Worked example 7 — Square yards to square feet

Convert \(11\,\mathrm{yd}^2\) to square feet:

\[11\,\mathrm{yd}^2\times\frac{9\,\mathrm{ft}^2}{1\,\mathrm{yd}^2}=\boxed{99\,\mathrm{ft}^2}.\]

Multiplying by 3 instead of 9 would be a linear conversion, not an area conversion.

Worked example 8 — Square inches to square feet

Convert \(288\,\mathrm{in}^2\) to square feet. Since \(1\,\mathrm{ft}=12\,\mathrm{in}\), we have \(1\,\mathrm{ft}^2=144\,\mathrm{in}^2\):

\[288\,\mathrm{in}^2\times\frac{1\,\mathrm{ft}^2}{144\,\mathrm{in}^2}=\boxed{2\,\mathrm{ft}^2}.\]
Worked example 9 — Large and metric area conversions

Since \(1\,\mathrm{mile}=5280\,\mathrm{ft}\), one square mile is \(5280^2=27{,}878{,}400\,\mathrm{ft}^2\). A \(2\,\mathrm{mile}^2\) region contains \(55{,}756{,}800\,\mathrm{ft}^2\). Since \(1\,\mathrm m=100\,\mathrm{cm}\), \(3\,\mathrm m^2=3(100)^2=30{,}000\,\mathrm{cm}^2\).

3. SET — Translate parabolas and track points (Problems 7–9)

Study the worked examples first, then try the problems yourself. An answer key at the bottom of this section is provided to check your work.

In your MVP packetSection 3.5 · Set · Problems 7–9 · pp. 33–34

Recall the parent parabola \(f(x)=x^2\). Subtracting a number outside the square shifts every point downward; adding a number inside the parentheses shifts it left (subtracting shifts it right). Track a point's coordinates, not only the function's intercepts, as it moves.

Coordinate effects

Vertical shift down \(k\): \(y=f(x)-k\) sends \((x,y)\mapsto(x,y-k)\).

Horizontal shift left \(s\): \(y=f(x+s)\) sends \((x,y)\mapsto(x-s,y)\).

Horizontal shift right \(s\): \(y=f(x-s)\) sends \((x,y)\mapsto(x+s,y)\).

Worked example 10 — Track two intercepts through two shifts

Compare \(f(x)=x^2\), \(g(x)=x^2-9\), and \(h(x)=(x+2)^2-9\).

The vertex moves \((0,0)\to(0,-9)\to(-2,-9)\). For \(g\), setting \(x^2-9=0\) gives x-intercepts \((-3,0)\) and \((3,0)\). These points correspond to \((-3,9)\) and \((3,9)\) on the parent \(f\), and to \((-5,0)\) and \((1,0)\) on \(h\). The points on \(h\) come from shifting those on \(g\) left 2 units.

Worked example 11 — A different pair of shifts

Compare \(f(x)=x^2\), \(g(x)=x^2-4\), and \(h(x)=(x-1)^2-4\). The roots of \(g\) are \(-2,2\). After reversing the vertical shift, the corresponding points on \(f\) are \((-2,4)\) and \((2,4)\). Following the horizontal shift right 1 instead gives \((-1,0)\) and \((3,0)\) on \(h\).

That distinction matters: the original x-intercepts need not remain x-intercepts after a vertical shift, but a pure horizontal shift preserves their heights.

4. GO — Evaluate functions and solve for inputs (Problems 10–15)

Study the worked examples first, then try the problems yourself. An answer key at the bottom of this section is provided to check your work.

In your MVP packetSection 3.5 · Go · Problems 10–15 · pp. 34–35

There are two different requests in these exercises. To evaluate \(f(2)\), insert \(x=2\) and calculate the output. To find where \(f(x)=16\), replace the entire output with 16 and solve for the input. The second request may produce two inputs, one, or none.

Worked Go Problem A — Straightforward evaluation and solving

Given \(p(x)=(x-4)^2+1\), find \(p(0)\), \(p(4)\), the inputs for which \(p(x)=1\), and the inputs for which \(p(x)=10\).

\[p(0)=(-4)^2+1=17,\qquad p(4)=0^2+1=1.\]

For \(p(x)=1\): \((x-4)^2=0\), so \(x=4\). For \(p(x)=10\): \((x-4)^2=9\), so \(x-4=\pm3\); thus \(x=1\) or \(7\).

Worked Go Problem B — Standard form and noninteger inputs

Let \(q(x)=x^2+2x-7\). Find \(q(-3)\), the inputs giving zero, and the inputs giving output 8.

First, \(q(-3)=9-6-7=-4\). For zero output, solve \(x^2+2x-7=0\):

\[x=\frac{-2\pm\sqrt{4+28}}2=\boxed{-1\pm2\sqrt2}.\]

For output 8, solve \(x^2+2x-15=0=(x+5)(x-3)\), giving \(x=-5\) or \(x=3\). Notice that the intercept equation and the output-8 equation are different.

Worked Go Problem C — No real answer to an output equation

Let \(r(x)=(x+2)^2+3\). Find the inputs giving output 2.

\[(x+2)^2+3=2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad (x+2)^2=-1.\]

No real number has a negative square, so there is no real input with \(r(x)=2\). This matches the graph: the minimum output is 3. We do not need complex numbers for today's real-function questions.

Worked Go Problem D — Negative leading sign

Given \(s(x)=-(x+3)^2+12\), find \(s(0)\), \(s(-3)\), all real solutions of \(s(x)=0\), and all real solutions of \(s(x)=8\).

Substitute: \(s(0)=-9+12=3\), and \(s(-3)=12\). For zero: \((x+3)^2=12\), so \(x=-3\pm2\sqrt3\). For output 8: \((x+3)^2=4\), so \(x=-5\) or \(x=-1\).

Worked Go Problem E — Difference of squares and interpreting zeros

Let \(t(x)=(x-6)(x+2)\). Find \(t(0)\), \(t(1)\), and where \(t(x)=0\).

Evaluations give \(t(0)=(-6)(2)=-12\) and \(t(1)=(-5)(3)=-15\). The zero-product property gives \(x=6\) or \(x=-2\). Those are the x-coordinates of the graph's intercepts.

Worked Go Problem F — Connecting an output to the graph

For \(u(x)=2(x-1)^2-8\), find all \(x\) such that \(u(x)=10\).

\[2(x-1)^2-8=10\Longrightarrow (x-1)^2=9\Longrightarrow x-1=\pm3.\]

Thus \(x=-2\) or \(x=4\). On the graph, these are the two intersections with the horizontal line \(y=10\).

Packet clarification — Section 3.5, Set 7e and 8e

“The points corresponding to the x-intercepts in \(g\)” means track the same geometric points under each transformation. On the parent graph they usually have positive y-coordinates and are not x-intercepts there. Under a horizontal shift, the corresponding points remain on the x-axis.

Teacher's review — packet issues

No major mathematical errors were identified in the Section 3.5 main task. One wording issue is substantial enough to clarify for students: in Set Problems 7e and 8e, corresponding points on \(f\) are easy to mistake for x-intercepts; those points on the parent parabola generally have nonzero y-coordinate. Suggested wording: “Track the two points that are x-intercepts of \(g\) backward to \(f\), and forward to \(h\); give their coordinates on each graph.”

The formula derivation in main task Problem 7 assumes the quadratic has real intercepts; make that assumption explicit when introducing \(d\). The chapter's separate clarification states the limitation.
