In the previous lessons you solved quadratic equations and found their x-intercepts. Today you will see why some quadratics factor neatly, some touch the x-axis only once, and others never reach it. This puzzle leads to a larger number system, which the next lesson explores in detail.

1. In class: A Develop Understanding Task

Revisit the ideas from class

This section explains some of the ideas you explored in class through the guided exercises, in a more formal, step-by-step way. Use it alongside the “A Develop Understanding Task” portion of Section 3.8 in your MVP packet.

Begin with a quadratic table

In your MVP packetSection 3.8 · Main task · Problems 1–10 · pp. 49–52

The vertex is the turning point. Values at equal distances to either side of its x-coordinate agree; the line through the vertex is the axis of symmetry. For a quadratic table with consecutive x-values, constant second differences are a useful pattern. Here the packet tells us the tables represent quadratic functions; a finite table alone cannot establish that a function is quadratic everywhere.

Worked example 1 — Read the vertex and scale from a table
\(x\)−4−3−2−10
\(y\)41014

The minimum is zero at \(x=-2\), so the vertex is \((-2,0)\). Begin with \(y=a(x+2)^2\). Substituting the point \((-1,1)\) gives \(1=a(1)^2\), so \(a=1\).

\[\boxed{y=(x+2)^2=x^2+4x+4.}\]

This parabola just touches the x-axis at \((-2,0)\).

Write the same quadratic in three forms

In your MVP packetSection 3.8 · Main task · Problems 1–10 · pp. 49–52

Each form emphasizes a different feature. Vertex form displays the turning point. Standard form gives the y-intercept directly. Real factored form shows the x-intercepts—when real x-intercepts exist.

Vertex form\[\boxed{y=a(x-h)^2+k}\]

Vertex \((h,k)\); the sign of \(a\) tells whether the parabola opens up or down.

Standard form\[\boxed{y=ax^2+bx+c}\]

The y-intercept is \((0,c)\). Expand vertex form to obtain it.

Real factored form (when real zeros exist)\[\boxed{y=a(x-r_1)(x-r_2)}\]

Zeros \(r_1,r_2\); if the two roots coincide, the factor repeats.

Worked example 2 — Two distinct x-intercepts (packet problem 1 pattern)

The table has vertex \((-2,-1)\) and includes \((0,3)\). Start with vertex form:

\[y=a(x+2)^2-1,\qquad3=4a-1\Longrightarrow a=1.\]

Now expand, then factor:

\[\boxed{y=(x+2)^2-1}\] \[\boxed{y=x^2+4x+3}\] \[\boxed{y=(x+3)(x+1).}\]

Zeros are \(x=-3\) and \(x=-1\), symmetric about \(x=-2\). Check \(x=0\): \((3)(1)=3\), consistent with the table.

Worked example 3 — One repeated x-intercept (packet problem 3 pattern)

For the vertex \((-2,0)\) and the point \((0,4)\), \(a=1\).

\[\underbrace{(x+2)^2}_{\text{vertex and factored form}}=\underbrace{x^2+4x+4}_{\text{standard form}}.\]

The equation \((x+2)^2=0\) has a repeated root, \(-2\). There is one distinct x-intercept but two equal algebraic roots counted with multiplicity.

Three ways a real quadratic can meet the x-axis

In your MVP packetSection 3.8 · Main task · Problems 1–10 · pp. 49–52

A real x-intercept corresponds to a real number \(x\) for which \(y=0\). An upward-opening parabola whose vertex lies above the x-axis never has \(y=0\); likewise a downward-opening parabola whose maximum is below the x-axis.

Worked example 4 — Why the packet’s problem 4 cannot have real linear factors

The table’s vertex is \((-2,1)\), and \((0,5)\) is on the graph, giving \(a=1\):

\[\boxed{y=(x+2)^2+1=x^2+4x+5.}\]

Setting \(y=0\) would require \((x+2)^2=-1\). But the square of any real number is nonnegative, so there is no real solution and no factorization into real linear factors. Expanding a proposed real factored form and testing values will not rescue it.

Worked example 5 — A downward-opening parabola with no real roots

For \(y=-(x+2)^2+2\), the maximum is \(2\), so the graph crosses the axis in two places. In contrast, \(y=-(x+2)^2-2\) has maximum \(-2\), so the entire graph lies below the x-axis.

Reflecting a quadratic across the x-axis changes the signs of its outputs but does not change its zeros. Moving its vertex vertically can change how many real zeros it has.

Predict real-root behavior without graphing

In your MVP packetSection 3.8 · Main task · Problems 1–10 · pp. 49–52

The quadratic formula from Section 3.5 is

\[x=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a},\quad a\ne0.\]

The quantity inside the square root is called the discriminant, \(D=b^2-4ac\). Its sign tells how many distinct real roots a quadratic has.

Positive discriminant: two distinct real roots\[\boxed{D>0}\]
Zero discriminant: one distinct real root, counted twice\[\boxed{D=0}\]
Negative discriminant: no real roots\[\boxed{D<0}\]

The formula asks us to take a square root of a negative number, which cannot be done in the real number system.

Worked example 6 — Classify before solving

For \(x^2+4x+5=0\), take \(a=1,b=4,c=5\):

\[D=4^2-4(1)(5)=16-20=-4.\]

Because \(D<0\), there are no real roots. For \(x^2+4x+4=0\), \(D=16-16=0\), confirming the repeated root. And for \(x^2+4x+3=0\), \(D=16-12=4>0\), confirming two distinct real roots.

Worked example 7 — Rational versus irrational roots

Compare \(x^2-6x+5=0\) and \(x^2-6x+2=0\). Both have \(D>0\), but:

\[x^2-6x+5=(x-1)(x-5)\Rightarrow x=1,5.\] \[x^2-6x+2=0\Rightarrow x=\frac{6\pm\sqrt{36-8}}2=3\pm\sqrt7.\]

The first roots are integers. The second are irrational real numbers. When integer coefficients are used, a positive perfect-square discriminant leads to rational roots; a positive non-square discriminant leads to irrational roots.

Why “two roots” can mean more than two x-intercepts

In your MVP packetSection 3.8 · Main task · Problems 1–10 · pp. 49–52

The packet introduces the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra: a polynomial of degree \(n\) has \(n\) roots when roots are counted with multiplicity and complex numbers are allowed. A degree-two polynomial therefore has two complex roots, though the graph may have two, one, or zero real x-intercepts.

Worked example 8 — The missing roots point to new numbers

The equation \((x+2)^2=-1\) has no real solution. The equation \(u^2=-1\) motivates a new number, called the imaginary unit, written \(i\), with \(i^2=-1\). Thus we may write the two roots of \((x+2)^2+1=0\) as \(x=-2+i\) and \(x=-2-i\).

These are nonreal complex roots, not x-intercepts on an ordinary real coordinate plane. Today we only identify why such numbers are needed; operations with them are developed in the following lessons.

Packet clarification — Section 3.8, Problem 10How to interpret the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra

The packet’s abbreviated wording says an nth-degree polynomial has n roots. This counts complex roots with multiplicity. It does not assert n different real x-intercepts. For example, \((x+2)^2\) has one distinct real zero counted twice, while \((x+2)^2+1\) has none on the real x-axis.

2. Ready — simplify square roots

In your MVP packetSection 3.8 · Ready · Problems 1–7 · p. 53

Look for the largest perfect-square factor under the radical. For nonnegative \(a,b\), \(\sqrt{ab}=\sqrt a\sqrt b\). A number like \(\sqrt8\) is irrational, yet it can be expressed in simpler radical form.

Worked example 9 — Simplify a square-root radical\[\sqrt{72}=\sqrt{36\cdot2}=6\sqrt2.\]

The six expressions in Ready (problems 1–6) all simplify to an integer multiple of \(\sqrt2\) or \(\sqrt5\), illustrating how apparently different radicals can share a common irrational factor.

Worked example 10 — Recognize equivalent radicals\[\sqrt{50}=5\sqrt2,\qquad\sqrt{98}=7\sqrt2.\]

They are not equal, but both are multiples of \(\sqrt2\). Do not combine unlike radicals by adding the numbers inside them: \(\sqrt2+\sqrt3\ne\sqrt5\).

3. Set — identify the type of x-intercept

In your MVP packetSection 3.8 · Set · Problems 8–18 · pp. 53–54

For the shared ideas used in these exercises, revisit the In class explanation.

The Set questions use graphs, formulas, and polynomial expressions. Read graphs to count real intersections; use factors or the discriminant to classify exact values. Number categories are nested: whole numbers are integers, integers are rational, and rational and irrational numbers are both real.

Given informationWhat you can conclude
Graph crosses the x-axis twiceTwo distinct real x-intercepts; without exact labels, a sketch may not establish whether they are rational.
Graph touches the x-axis onceOne distinct real x-intercept (double root).
Graph stays above or below x-axisNo real x-intercepts.
Exact factored form \((2x-3)(x+4)\)Rational zeros \(3/2\) and \(-4\), with \(-4\) an integer.
Worked example 11 — Classify a quadratic from its factors\[(3x+2)(x-6)=0\]\[x=-\frac23\quad\text{or}\quad x=6.\]

The first root is rational but not an integer; the second is a whole number, an integer, and rational. When choosing the most specific category, classify each root separately.

Worked example 12 — Classify using a discriminant

For \(g(x)=2x^2-4x+7\), \(D=(-4)^2-4(2)(7)=16-56=-40\). There are no real x-intercepts.

Set problems 16–18 ask for the number of roots of higher-degree polynomials. The degree is the largest exponent with a nonzero coefficient. A degree-five polynomial has five complex roots counted with multiplicity, even if its real graph shows fewer x-intercepts.

Worked example 13 — Determine degree even when terms are scrambled\[p(x)=4x^3+7x^6-x^2+5.\]

The largest exponent is 6, so this polynomial has six complex roots counted with multiplicity. You cannot infer how many of these are real merely from the degree.

4. Go — find exact x-intercepts

In your MVP packetSection 3.8 · Go · Problems 19–27 · p. 54

Set the quadratic equal to zero. First look for an easy common factor or integer binomial factors. Otherwise apply the quadratic formula, keeping radicals exact. Report real x-intercepts as ordered pairs \((r,0)\). If the discriminant is negative, write “no real x-intercepts,” rather than plotting imaginary numbers.

Additional Go problem A — straightforward factoring

Find the x-intercepts of \(f(x)=x^2-x-20\).

\[x^2-x-20=(x-5)(x+4)=0\]\[x=5\quad\text{or}\quad x=-4.\]

Therefore the x-intercepts are \(\boxed{(5,0)\text{ and }(-4,0)}\). Check: both substitutions make the output zero.

Additional Go problem B — quadratic formula with irrational solutions

Find the x-intercepts of \(g(x)=3x^2-2x-6\).

\[x=\frac{2\pm\sqrt{(-2)^2-4(3)(-6)}}{2(3)}\] \[=\frac{2\pm\sqrt{76}}6=\frac{1\pm\sqrt{19}}3.\]

Hence the intercepts are \(\boxed{\left(\frac{1+\sqrt{19}}3,0\right)\text{ and }\left(\frac{1-\sqrt{19}}3,0\right)}\). They are irrational. No rounding is needed.

Additional Go problem C — a repeated real root

For \(h(x)=4x^2+12x+9\), factor:

\[h(x)=(2x+3)^2.\]

The only distinct x-intercept is \(\boxed{(-3/2,0)}\); its root has multiplicity two. The graph touches the axis there.

Additional Go problem D — no real intercepts

For \(k(x)=2x^2+4x+8\):

\[D=4^2-4(2)(8)=16-64=-48.\]

There are \(\boxed{\text{no real x-intercepts}}\). The quadratic formula produces nonreal roots, but the ordinary graph does not cross the real x-axis.

Teacher’s review (teachers and parents)

Section 3.8, main-task directions, especially problems 4 and 8: Students are asked to write every quadratic in all three forms, including real factored form. But \((x+2)^2+1\) and \((x+2)^2+2\) have no real linear factorization. Clarify: “Write vertex and standard forms for every function; give real factored form when possible, and otherwise explain why no real linear factorization exists.” Nonreal factors belong to the next lesson.

Section 3.8, problem 10: The theorem statement omits “over the complex numbers, counting multiplicity.” Without this qualifier, graphs with zero or one real intercept appear to contradict it. State the complete theorem explicitly.

Section 3.8, Set problems 8, 9, 12, 13: Unlabeled or hand-plotted graph crossings may establish the number of real intercepts but do not reliably establish the rationality of exact x-coordinates. If number types are intended, provide equations or exact labeled intercepts; otherwise ask only for the number of real intercepts.
