Core idea When an exponential quantity doubles in one unit of time, it must grow by the same proportional factor over every equally long subdivision. Equal time intervals do not add equal amounts.

1. Begin with growth at whole-number inputs

In your MVP packetSection 3.1 · Main task · Problems 1–9 · pp. 1–3

Suppose a quantity starts at 4 and doubles whenever the input increases by 1. The function is \(f(x)=4\cdot 2^x\). The 4 is the initial value; the 2 is the growth factor for one full unit of input.

\(x\)01234
\(f(x)\)48163264
Worked example 1 — Extend a doubling table

Given \(f(x)=4\cdot 2^x\), calculate \(f(3)\):

\[f(3)=4\cdot2^3=4\cdot8=32.\]

Move to the next whole-number input by multiplying 32 by 2, giving \(f(4)=64\). Moving backward from 4 to 3 reverses the multiplication: divide by 2.

A continuous exponential function is defined at inputs between whole numbers as well. The question is how to assign values between 4 and 8, between 8 and 16, and so on, while preserving the same pattern of proportional growth.

2. Why ordinary midpoint averaging is not exponential growth

In your MVP packetSection 3.1 · Main task · Problems 1–9 · pp. 1–3

Travis proposes averaging two consecutive outputs. Between \(f(0)=4\) and \(f(1)=8\), he obtains \((4+8)/2=6\). His values halfway between 8 and 16, and between 16 and 32, would be 12 and 24.

InputStartHalfway by averagingEnd
0 to 1468
1 to 281216
2 to 3162432
3 to 4324864

The two half-interval factors between 4 and 8 would be \(6/4=1.5\) and \(8/6=4/3\). They are different. Thus Travis’s rule does not produce a consistent exponential growth factor over equal half-unit intervals.

Worked example 2 — When averaging is appropriate

A linear function adds the same amount whenever x rises by 1. For \(g(x)=4+4x\), we have \(g(0)=4\), \(g(1)=8\), and \(g(1/2)=6\). The average is correct for this linear function because each half-step adds 2.

For a doubling exponential function, an equal half-step must multiply by the same factor instead.

3. Find the multiplier for a half-step

In your MVP packetSection 3.1 · Main task · Problems 1–9 · pp. 1–3

Let \(r\) be the factor for every increase of \(1/2\) in x. Two half-steps make one whole step. Therefore two multiplications by \(r\) must equal multiplication by 2:

\[r\cdot r=2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad r^2=2,\qquad r=\sqrt2\approx1.4142.\]

We choose the positive number because the model describes positive growth. The square root of 2 is the positive number whose square is 2.

The halfway output is the geometric mean of the two endpoint outputs: for example, \(4\sqrt2\approx5.657\), which is less than Travis’s 6.

Worked example 3 — Halfway between two doubling values

Start from 8 at \(x=1\). One half-step later, multiply by \(\sqrt2\): \(8\sqrt2\approx11.314\). Another half-step later, multiply by \(\sqrt2\) again:

\[8\sqrt2\cdot\sqrt2=8\cdot2=16.\]

The same factor works for both intervals.

Exact \(f(x)\)4\(4\sqrt2\)8\(8\sqrt2\)16\(16\sqrt2\)32\(32\sqrt2\)64

Writing \(2^{1/2}\) is another way of labeling the half-step multiplier \(\sqrt2\) in this context: \(2^{1/2}\cdot2^{1/2}=2\). A later lesson develops the general rules for fractional exponents; here we need only understand the consistent half-step factor.

4. Find the multiplier for a third-step

In your MVP packetSection 3.1 · Main task · Problems 1–9 · pp. 1–3

To split a one-unit doubling into three equal steps, choose a positive factor \(s\) that works three times in a row:

\[s\cdot s\cdot s=2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad s^3=2,\qquad s=\sqrt[3]{2}\approx1.2599.\]

The cube root of 2 is the positive number whose cube is 2. Three consecutive third-steps give \(\sqrt[3]{2}^3=2\).

Worked example 4 — Complete one third-step interval

Starting from \(f(0)=4\), the outputs at \(x=1/3,2/3,1\) are:

\[4\sqrt[3]{2}\approx5.040,\qquad4(\sqrt[3]{2})^2\approx6.350,\qquad4(\sqrt[3]{2})^3=8.\]

Each step multiplies by exactly the same factor, approximately 1.2599.

Estimating a value such as \(f(5/3)\)

In your MVP packetSection 3.1 · Main task · Problems 1–9 · pp. 1–3

Since \(5/3=1+2/3\), start at \(f(1)=8\) and take two third-steps. Multiply by \(\sqrt[3]{2}\) twice: \[f(5/3)=8(\sqrt[3]{2})^2\approx8(1.5874)\approx12.699.\] This also matches extending the third-step table.

A useful pattern to remember for positive exponential growth: when a whole unit multiplies by \(b\), two equal substeps need a factor whose square is \(b\); three equal substeps need a factor whose cube is \(b\).

5. See why the exponential curve bends

In your MVP packetSection 3.1 · Main task · Problems 1–9 · pp. 1–3

The graph becomes steeper because multiplying a larger amount by the same factor produces a larger numerical increase. For example, doubling 4 adds 4, while doubling 32 adds 32. A straight line would have a constant additive rate instead.

6. Ready: arithmetic and geometric sequences (problems 1–11)

In your MVP packetSection 3.1 · Ready · Problems 1–11 · pp. 4–5

A sequence lists values in order. To classify it, compare consecutive terms. A constant difference makes an arithmetic (additive) sequence; a constant ratio makes a geometric (multiplicative) sequence.

FeatureArithmetic sequenceGeometric sequence
Same operationAdd a fixed number \(d\)Multiply by a fixed factor \(r\)
First term \(a\); term number \(n\)\(a_n=a+(n-1)d\)\(a_n=a\cdot r^{n-1}\)
Example5, 9, 13, 17, …5, 10, 20, 40, …

The \(n-1\) is important: the first term requires zero changes, the second term one change, and the third term two changes.

Worked example 5 — Write an arithmetic rule

The sequence 13, 8, 3, −2, … subtracts 5 each time, so \(a=13\), \(d=-5\).

\[a_n=13+(n-1)(-5)=18-5n.\]

Check: \(a_1=13\), \(a_4=-2\).

Worked example 6 — Write a geometric rule, including a negative ratio

The sequence 2, −6, 18, −54, … multiplies by −3 each time. Therefore \(a_n=2(-3)^{n-1}\). The signs alternate because the multiplier is negative. This is a discrete sequence rule; it does not describe the positive continuous growth curves of today’s main task.

Read an exponential graph accurately

In your MVP packetSection 3.1 · Ready · Problems 1–11 · pp. 4–5

For Ready problems 6–11, read \(f(2)\) by moving vertically from input 2 until reaching the curve, then horizontally to the output axis. For \(f(x)=4\), start at height 4 and move horizontally to the curve before reading x. These are different questions: given x, find y versus given y, find x.

Worked example 7 — Read a graph and identify its equation

Suppose the graph contains \((0,1),(2,2),(4,4),(6,8),(8,16)\). Doubling takes two units of x, so each one-unit increase uses the factor \(\sqrt2\). The graph’s rule is \(f(x)=2^{x/2}\), equivalently \(f(x)=(\sqrt2)^x\).

Thus \(f(2)=2\), \(f(6)=8\), and \(f(x)=4\) at \(x=4\). The graph in the packet is intended to represent this approximate pattern; check its axis marks carefully.

7. Set: growth tables with halves and thirds (problems 12–14)

In your MVP packetSection 3.1 · Set · Problems 12–14 · p. 6

The bacteria tables give the starting quantity and the multiplier for one whole year. The blanks include fractional years. Find the positive equal-interval factor first, and use it repeatedly across the row.

Worked example 8 — Tripling every whole year

Start with 2 bacteria units at year 0, and triple in a full year. A half-year factor \(r\) must satisfy \(r^2=3\), so \(r=\sqrt3\).

\[B(1/2)=2\sqrt3\approx3.464,\quad B(1)=2(\sqrt3)^2=6,\quad B(3/2)=6\sqrt3\approx10.392.\]

The year-2 value is \(6\cdot3=18\). Continue multiplying by \(\sqrt3\) for every additional half-year.

Worked example 9 — Tripling with third-year entries

If a full year multiplies by 3, the factor for each third of a year is \(s=\sqrt[3]{3}\approx1.44225\).

At \(t=2/3\), multiply twice from the initial value: \[B(2/3)=2s^2=2(\sqrt[3]{3})^2\approx4.160.\]

After three third-steps, \(B(1)=2s^3=6\).

Worked example 10 — Quadrupling with half-year entries

If the full-year multiplier is 4, then the half-year factor is the positive number whose square is 4: \(r=2\).

From an initial amount of 2, the first four half-year values are \(2,4,8,16,32\) at years \(0,1/2,1,3/2,2\). In this case every half-year entry is a whole number.

Packet clarificationSection 3.1, Set, problems 12–14

The title says “Evaluate the Expressions with Rational Exponents,” but the exercises actually present growth tables. The intended work is to determine a constant half-year or third-year multiplier and use it to fill the table. Exact radical forms and reasonable decimal approximations express the same model. The tables describe modeled quantities, so fractional numerical predictions need not be whole-number bacterial counts.

8. Go: simplify expressions with integer exponents (problems 15–20)

In your MVP packetSection 3.1 · Go · Problems 15–20 · p. 6

These homework items review laws for integer exponents. They do not require the general fractional-exponent rules that the next section develops. Exponent rules assume the stated base is nonzero wherever division is involved.

SituationRuleReason / example
Multiply same base\(a^m a^n=a^{m+n}\)\(3^2\cdot3^4=3^6\)
Divide same base\(a^m/a^n=a^{m-n}\)\(5^7/5^2=5^5\)
Exponent zero\(a^0=1\) for \(a\ne0\)\(11^0=1\)
Negative integer exponent\(a^{-n}=1/a^n\)\(2^{-4}=1/2^4\)
Worked Go problem A — Straightforward (original)

Problem: Simplify \(6^3\cdot6^4\) in exponential form.

Step 1. The bases are both 6. Add the exponents when multiplying equal bases.

\[6^3\cdot6^4=6^{3+4}=\boxed{6^7}.\]

Check: Three factors of 6 and four more factors of 6 give seven factors total. Do not multiply the exponents.

Worked Go problem B — More challenging (original)

Problem: Simplify \(\dfrac{5^{-2}\cdot5^6}{5^3}\) in exponential form.

Step 1. Combine powers across the numerator: \(5^{-2}\cdot5^6=5^4\).

Step 2. Divide powers by subtracting the denominator exponent: \[\frac{5^4}{5^3}=5^{4-3}=\boxed{5^1}.\]

Common error: A negative exponent does not make the value negative; \(5^{-2}=1/25\).

Worked Go problem C — Zero and negative powers (original)

Problem: Simplify \(\dfrac{7^0\cdot7^{-3}}{7^{-5}}\) in exponential form.

\[\frac{7^{0+(-3)}}{7^{-5}}=7^{-3-(-5)}=\boxed{7^2}.\]

Subtracting a negative exponent means adding. Notice also that \(7^0=1\).

Watch the difference. \(2^{-5}\) is a positive small number, \(1/32\), not \(-32\). Similarly, exponent 0 gives 1 for a nonzero base, not 0.

Teacher / parent review: serious packet issues

Section 3.1, Set, problems 12–14: The heading “Evaluate the Expressions with Rational Exponents” does not match the actual table-completion questions. The instruction describes growth but does not explain whether the resulting bacteria entries should be reported exactly or approximately. Suggested revision: “For each table, find the constant growth factor for each indicated fractional year. Fill the entries using exact expressions or approximations rounded to three decimal places.”

Section 3.1, Ready, problems 6–11: Reading exact values from the printed graph requires interpreting a lightly labeled graph; students may give slightly different estimates. Suggested revision: Clearly label the unit of each horizontal grid step and indicate that values read from the graph are approximate unless exact coordinate points are supplied.
