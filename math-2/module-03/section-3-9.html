Teacher’s review

You already know that a quadratic may have two real x-intercepts, one repeated x-intercept, or no real x-intercepts. This lesson explains where irrational real roots fit and how we name the solutions that lie outside the real number system. We start with numbers you can place on a number line.

1. In class: A Solidify Understanding Task

Revisit the ideas from class

This section explains some of the ideas you explored in class through the guided exercises, in a more formal, step-by-step way. Use it alongside the “A Solidify Understanding Task” portion of Section 3.9 in your MVP packet.

Rational and irrational solutions are both real numbers

In your MVP packetSection 3.9 · Main task · Problems 1–15 · pp. 55–59

A rational number can be written as the quotient of two integers, with a nonzero denominator. An irrational number cannot. Both belong to the real numbers, meaning both can be located on the ordinary number line. A radical such as \(\sqrt2\) is an exact number, not merely a calculator approximation.

Worked example 1 — Verify equivalent equations (packet main task, problem 1)

Expand \(4(x-\frac52)(x+\frac32)=0\):

\[\begin{aligned}4\left(x^2-x-\frac{15}{4}\right)&=0\\4x^2-4x-15&=0.\end{aligned}\]

By the zero-product property, \(x=\frac52\) or \(x=-\frac32\). Both are rational. Multiplying the factored equation by a nonzero constant preserves its solutions.

Worked example 2 — Irrational roots and the difference of squares (main task, problem 2)

Multiply the conjugate expressions, viewing \(x-2\) as one quantity:

\[\begin{aligned}(x-2+\sqrt2)(x-2-\sqrt2)&=(x-2)^2-(\sqrt2)^2\\&=x^2-4x+2.\end{aligned}\]

Multiplying by 4 would give \(4x^2-16x+8\), so note that the worksheet’s quadratic is \(4x^2-4x+2\) only if the factorization is different. In the actual printed expression in the packet, the accompanying quadratic is \(4x^2-4x+2=0\), which does not have the same roots as the displayed factorization. For the correctly expanded factorization, the roots are:

\[\boxed{x=2-\sqrt2\quad\text{or}\quad x=2+\sqrt2.}\]

Both are irrational because adding an integer to \(\sqrt2\) cannot make it rational. Approximately, these points lie at \(0.586\) and \(3.414\), but the exact answers are preferable.

Packet clarification — Section 3.9, Main Task, Problem 2Check the printed expansion

The intended equivalent quadratic is \(x^2-4x+2=0\), since \((x-2+\sqrt2)(x-2-\sqrt2)=x^2-4x+2\). The source packet displays \(4x^2-4x+2=0\), which is not equivalent. Use the factored expression to identify the intended irrational roots \(2\pm\sqrt2\). This is a genuine mathematical inconsistency, not a change in method.

Worked example 3 — Why \(2+\sqrt2\) is a single exact length (main task, problems 3–4)

For a right triangle with legs of length 1 and 1, the Pythagorean theorem gives the hypotenuse:

\[c^2=1^2+1^2=2\quad\Rightarrow\quad c=\sqrt2.\]

The perimeter is \(1+1+\sqrt2=2+\sqrt2\). A segment of length \(\sqrt2\), attached to a segment of length 2, locates \(2+\sqrt2\) exactly; transferring the \(\sqrt2\) length to the left of 2 locates \(2-\sqrt2\). Both are ordinary points on the real number line.

Big idea — The real number system

Real numbers consist of all rational numbers together with all irrational numbers. An irrational root is still a real x-intercept and can be located on a number line.

The imaginary unit extends the number system

In your MVP packetSection 3.9 · Main task · Problems 1–15 · pp. 55–59

No real number has square equal to \(-1\). To name solutions of equations requiring such a square, we introduce the imaginary unit \(i\).

Definition — Imaginary unit\[\boxed{i=\sqrt{-1}.}\]
Essential identity — Square of the imaginary unit\[\boxed{i^2=-1.}\]
Principal square root of a negative real number\[\boxed{\sqrt{-a}=i\sqrt a\qquad(a>0).}\]
Worked example 4 — Simplify negative square roots \[\sqrt{-45}=\sqrt{45}\,i=3\sqrt5\,i,\qquad\sqrt{-16}=4i.\]

Be careful: \(\sqrt{-4}\,\sqrt{-9}=(2i)(3i)=6i^2=-6\), not \(\sqrt{36}=6\). The familiar identity \(\sqrt a\sqrt b=\sqrt{ab}\) does not extend blindly to negative radicands.

Worked example 5 — Multiply powers of \(i\) (main task, problems 6–7; Set 18–21) \[(\sqrt2\,i)^2=2i^2=-2,\qquad (3i)(3i)=9i^2=-9.\]

The powers cycle in groups of four:

\[i^1=i,\quad i^2=-1,\quad i^3=-i,\quad i^4=1.\]

For example, \(i^7=i^4i^3=-i\). Use exponent laws to group powers, then replace every \(i^2\) by \(-1\).

A complex number has the form \(a+bi\), where \(a\) and \(b\) are real. Its real part is \(a\), and its imaginary part is \(b\). A pure imaginary number has \(a=0\) and \(b\ne0\). Every real number is also complex, since \(a=a+0i\).

Worked example 6 — Classify two complex numbers

\(7i\) is nonreal, pure imaginary, and complex. \(-3+2i\) is nonreal and complex, but not pure imaginary. The number \(-3\) is both real and complex, because it is \(-3+0i\).

Solve quadratic equations with complex roots

In your MVP packetSection 3.9 · Main task · Problems 1–15 · pp. 55–59

Now the quadratic formula can give an answer even when the discriminant is negative. Such a quadratic has two nonreal complex roots, which are not x-intercepts of its ordinary real graph.

Quadratic formula\[\boxed{x=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a},\qquad a\ne0.}\]
Worked example 7 — Solve by completing the square (main task example) \[\begin{aligned}x^2+4x+5&=0\\(x+2)^2&=-1\\x+2&=\pm i\\x&=-2\pm i\end{aligned}\]

The two solutions are complex conjugates: they have the same real part and opposite imaginary parts.

Worked example 8 — Verify the complex factorization (main task, problem 8) \[\begin{aligned}(x+2-i)(x+2+i)&=(x+2)^2-i^2\\&=(x+2)^2+1\\&=x^2+4x+5.\end{aligned}\]

The product is a real quadratic even though the individual factors contain \(i\). This is a consequence of the opposite-sign imaginary terms canceling.

Conjugate-pair fact

For a quadratic with real coefficients, if \(a+bi\) is a nonreal root, then \(a-bi\) is also a root.

Worked example 9 — Solve with the quadratic formula (Set 22–25 pattern)

Solve \(2x^2-4x+10=0\). Here \(a=2,b=-4,c=10\).

\[\begin{aligned}D&=(-4)^2-4(2)(10)=-64,\\x&=\frac{4\pm\sqrt{-64}}4=\frac{4\pm8i}{4}.\end{aligned}\] \[\boxed{x=1\pm2i.}\]

Both solutions are exact. Writing “no solution” would be incorrect when working over the complex numbers.

What graphs tell us about real and complex roots

In your MVP packetSection 3.9 · Main task · Problems 1–15 · pp. 55–59

For a quadratic \(y=ax^2+bx+c\), the vertex’s horizontal coordinate is \(-b/(2a)\). The two root expressions are centered on that x-coordinate:

\[x=\underbrace{\frac{-b}{2a}}_{\text{center}}\;\pm\;\underbrace{\frac{\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}}_{\text{signed offset}}.\]

For two real roots, the magnitude of the offset is their horizontal distance from the symmetry line. If the discriminant is negative, this offset involves \(i\), and no real intercepts appear.

Worked example 10 — Read two irrational roots from a graph (main task, problem 9)

For \(y=x^2-6x+7\), the vertex is \((3,-2)\). Set the output to zero:

\[(x-3)^2-2=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad x=3\pm\sqrt2.\]

The symmetry line is \(x=3=-b/(2a)\). The roots are \(\sqrt2\) units to the left and right. The graph confirms two real intercepts, but their exact irrational values come from algebra.

Worked example 11 — Read the equation before finding nonreal roots (main task, problems 12–13)

In problem 12 the shown parabola has vertex \((-2,3)\) and includes the y-intercept \((0,7)\). Thus its equation is \(y=(x+2)^2+3\).

\[(x+2)^2+3=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad\boxed{x=-2\pm i\sqrt3}.\]

Reflect across the horizontal line \(y=3\). Reflection sends an output \(y\) to \(6-y\), so the reflected parabola is \(y=3-(x+2)^2\). Its roots satisfy \((x+2)^2=3\), giving:

\[\boxed{x=-2\pm\sqrt3}.\]

The reflected graph has two irrational real intercepts; the original has no real intercepts. Both sets of roots arise by isolating a squared expression and taking a square root, but one asks for \(\sqrt{-3}\) while the other asks for \(\sqrt3\).

Fundamental Theorem of Algebra — Quadratic case

Every quadratic \(ax^2+bx+c\) with \(a\ne0\) has two complex roots counted with multiplicity. They may be two distinct real numbers, one repeated real number, or two nonreal conjugates. A graph shows only the real roots.

Packet clarification — Section 3.9, Main Task, Problems 11–12Complex roots cannot be read directly from an ordinary graph

A graph that never intersects the x-axis shows there are no real roots, but the picture alone is not enough to calculate the exact complex roots. For problem 12, use the visible vertex and another clear point to reconstruct the equation first, then solve it algebraically. The graph is not displaying imaginary coordinates as x-intercepts.

2. Ready — Classify numbers by all applicable sets

The Ready problems (1–9) ask for every set a number belongs to. The sets overlap: whole numbers \(\subset\) integers \(\subset\) rational numbers \(\subset\) real numbers \(\subset\) complex numbers. Irrational numbers belong to the real and complex numbers, but not to the rational numbers.

IntegerPositive and negative whole numbers and zero\(-13\): integer, rational, real, complex
RationalRatio of integers\(\frac49\): rational, real, complex
IrrationalReal but not rational\(\sqrt{75}=5\sqrt3\): irrational, real, complex
Nonreal complexNonzero imaginary part\(\sqrt{-16}=4i\): complex only among these listed sets
Worked example 12 — Simplify first, then classify

Classify \(\sqrt{\frac49}\) and \(5+\sqrt2\).

\[\sqrt{\frac49}=\frac23\quad\Rightarrow\quad\text{rational, real, complex}.\]

The number \(5+\sqrt2\) is irrational, real, and complex. A rational number added to an irrational number stays irrational.

Worked example 13 — Combine radicals \[\begin{aligned}\sqrt{27}+\sqrt{12}-2\sqrt3&=3\sqrt3+2\sqrt3-2\sqrt3\\&=\boxed{3\sqrt3}.\end{aligned}\]

Do not add numbers inside the radicals: \(\sqrt{27}+\sqrt{12}\ne\sqrt{39}\).

Worked example 14 — Mixed integers and like radicals \[\begin{aligned}6+4\sqrt5-9+2\sqrt5&=(6-9)+(4+2)\sqrt5\\&=\boxed{-3+6\sqrt5}.\end{aligned}\]

4. Go — Use the discriminant to describe the roots

Study the worked examples first, then try the problems yourself. An answer key at the bottom of this section is provided to check your work.

In your MVP packetSection 3.9 · Go · Problems 26–38 · p. 61

For Go problems 26–31, calculate \(D=b^2-4ac\), then describe the number and kind of roots. You do not always need to find their exact values to classify them.

Case 1 — Positive perfect-square discriminant (integer coefficients)\[\boxed{D>0\text{ and perfect square}\;\Longrightarrow\;\text{two distinct rational real roots}.}\]
Case 2 — Positive nonsquare discriminant (integer coefficients)\[\boxed{D>0\text{ and not perfect square}\;\Longrightarrow\;\text{two distinct irrational real roots}.}\]
Case 3 — Zero discriminant\[\boxed{D=0\;\Longrightarrow\;\text{one repeated real root}.}\]
Case 4 — Negative discriminant\[\boxed{D<0\;\Longrightarrow\;\text{two nonreal complex-conjugate roots}.}\]
New Go problem A — Straightforward

Determine the nature of the roots of \(x^2-8x+15=0\).

\[D=(-8)^2-4(1)(15)=64-60=4.\]

Since \(D\) is a positive perfect square, the roots are two distinct rational real numbers. Indeed, \((x-3)(x-5)=0\), so \(x=3,5\).

New Go problem B — More challenging

Determine the nature of the roots of \(3x^2+2x+4=0\), and then find the exact roots to verify.

\[D=2^2-4(3)(4)=4-48=-44.\]

The negative discriminant gives two nonreal conjugate roots. Continue:

\[\begin{aligned}x&=\frac{-2\pm\sqrt{-44}}6\\&=\frac{-2\pm2i\sqrt{11}}6\\&=\boxed{-\frac13\pm\frac{\sqrt{11}}3i}.\end{aligned}\]
New Go problem C — Two irrational real roots

Classify the roots of \(2x^2+2x-3=0\).

\[D=2^2-4(2)(-3)=28.\]

Positive but not a perfect square: two distinct irrational real roots. The exact roots are \(\frac{-2\pm\sqrt{28}}4=\frac{-1\pm\sqrt7}{2}\).

New Go problem D — A repeated root

Classify and solve \(4t^2-12t+9=0\).

\[D=(-12)^2-4(4)(9)=144-144=0.\]

There is one repeated rational real root: \(t=\frac{12}{8}=\frac32\). It counts twice in the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra.

  7. \(D=1\): two distinct rational real roots.

6. Teacher’s review — Serious packet issues

Main Task, Problem 2: The displayed factors expand to \(x^2-4x+2\), not to the printed \(4x^2-4x+2\). This materially changes the roots. Correct the second equation to \(x^2-4x+2=0\) or change the factors and subsequent number-line discussion accordingly.

Main Task, Problem 12: The instruction “find the complex roots ... represented by its graph” omits the algebraic reconstruction step. The graph can establish that there are no real x-intercepts and (when scale is legible) suggest the vertex/another point; exact nonreal roots must be calculated from the reconstructed formula. A clearer instruction: “Use the graph to write the quadratic equation, then solve it over the complex numbers.”

Fundamental Theorem wording (Main Task, Problem 15): A degree-\(n\) polynomial has exactly \(n\) complex roots counting multiplicity. State that qualification explicitly to avoid equating the number of roots with distinct real x-intercepts.
