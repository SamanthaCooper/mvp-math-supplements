You already know that exponential growth multiplies an amount by the same factor over equal time intervals. In the previous lessons, half-year and other fractional time intervals introduced roots and fractional exponents. Today we explain why the exponent rules still work for those smaller intervals.

1. In class: A Solidify Understanding Task

Revisit the ideas from class

This section explains some of the ideas you explored in class through the guided exercises. Use it alongside the “A Solidify Understanding Task” portion of Section 3.3 in your MVP packet.

One year's growth can happen in twelve equal steps

In your MVP packetSection 3.3 · Main task · Problems 1–11 · pp. 15–16

Suppose an account starts with $300 and grows by 5% over one year. A 5% increase means multiplying the current balance by 1.05. The annual model is

\[A(t)=300(1.05)^t,\qquad t\text{ in years}.\]

The balance after one year is \(300(1.05)=315\) dollars. How can we describe the same growth after one month, assuming growth follows the same exponential pattern throughout the year?

Let \(m\) be the exact monthly multiplication factor. Twelve months must reproduce one annual factor:

\[\underbrace{m\cdot m\cdots m}_{12\text{ factors}}=m^{12}=1.05.\]

Because \(m\) is positive, the unique positive solution is the positive twelfth root:

\[m=\sqrt[12]{1.05}=(1.05)^{1/12}\approx1.00407412.\]

The monthly increase is about \(0.4074\%\), not exactly \(5\%/12\) under this model. A monthly factor is approximately 1.004074; the corresponding percentage increase is approximately 0.4074%.

Worked example 1 — Write a monthly balance model

Let \(n\) count months since the deposit. Each month multiplies by \((1.05)^{1/12}\). Thus

\[A(n)=300\left((1.05)^{1/12}\right)^n=300(1.05)^{n/12}.\]

At six months: \(A(6)=300(1.05)^{6/12}=300\sqrt{1.05}\approx\$307.41.\) At twelve months: \(A(12)=300(1.05)^1=\$315.\) These values match the stated annual growth.

In the packet, time \(t\) remains measured in years, so the number of elapsed months is \(12t\). Substituting \(n=12t\) into our monthly model yields

\[A(t)=300\left((1.05)^{1/12}\right)^{12t}.\]
Worked example 2 — Show two models are identical \[\begin{aligned}300\left((1.05)^{1/12}\right)^{12t}&=300(1.05)^{(1/12)(12t)}\\&=300(1.05)^t.\end{aligned}\]

Using the power-of-a-power rule, the exponents multiply: \((1/12)(12t)=t\). Therefore these two exact equations predict precisely the same balance for every time \(t\) in their domain, not just after full years.

A rounded monthly multiplier is only an approximation

In your MVP packetSection 3.3 · Main task · Problems 1–11 · pp. 15–16

The packet also gives \(1.004\) as a convenient rounded monthly factor. Because \(1.004\neq(1.05)^{1/12}\), the expression \(300(1.004)^{12t}\) is not algebraically identical to \(300(1.05)^t\).

Worked example 3 — Check the rounding effect \[300(1.004)^{12}\approx\$314.72,\qquad 300(1.05)=\$315.00.\]

Rounding the monthly multiplier before repeatedly multiplying gives a balance about 28 cents smaller after one year. Use the exact twelfth root in calculations when accuracy matters; round the final balance only at the end.

Why exponent rules extend to fractional powers

In your MVP packetSection 3.3 · Main task · Problems 1–11 · pp. 15–16

With whole-number exponents, \(a^2a^3=a^5\) because the combined product contains five factors of \(a\). For positive bases, we want fractional powers to follow this same pattern.

Multiplying powers: add their exponents

In your MVP packetSection 3.3 · Main task · Problems 1–11 · pp. 15–16

Multiplying two square roots of a positive number must give that number:

\[a^{1/2}a^{1/2}=\sqrt a\,\sqrt a=a=a^1.\]

The rule \(a^r a^s=a^{r+s}\) agrees because \(\tfrac12+\tfrac12=1\). Thus a fractional exponent measures a proportional part of a whole exponent.

Worked example 4 — Combine pieces of a year

Combine three one-month growth factors:

\[(1.05)^{1/12}(1.05)^{1/12}(1.05)^{1/12}=(1.05)^{3/12}=(1.05)^{1/4}.\]

The resulting factor describes three months, or one-quarter of a year.

Raising a power to a power: multiply the exponents

In your MVP packetSection 3.3 · Main task · Problems 1–11 · pp. 15–16

Six monthly steps correspond to half a year:

\[\left((1.05)^{1/12}\right)^6=(1.05)^{6/12}=(1.05)^{1/2}.\]

In general, \((a^r)^s=a^{rs}\) for positive \(a\) and real exponents \(r,s\). It works here because taking six equal monthly steps is the same as taking half of the annual exponent.

Worked example 5 — A fourth root squared \[\left(16^{1/4}\right)^2=16^{(1/4)2}=16^{1/2}=4.\]

Indeed, \(16^{1/4}=2\), and \(2^2=4\). The two calculations confirm the same result.

Dividing powers: subtract the exponents

In your MVP packetSection 3.3 · Main task · Problems 1–11 · pp. 15–16

For a positive base \(a\), division reverses multiplication:

\[\frac{a^{3/4}}{a^{1/4}}=a^{3/4-1/4}=a^{1/2}.\]

A negative exponent expresses a reciprocal, not a negative output: \(a^{-r}=\frac{1}{a^r}\) for \(a>0\).

Worked example 6 — Rewind the account one month \[(1.05)^{-1/12}=\frac{1}{(1.05)^{1/12}}\approx0.995942.\]

This multiplier reverses one month of growth. It is not a factor for an ordinary month of forward growth.

Rules established so far (positive base \(a\)) \[\boxed{a^ra^s=a^{r+s},\quad \frac{a^r}{a^s}=a^{r-s},\quad (a^r)^s=a^{rs},\quad a^{-r}=\frac1{a^r}.}\]

For positive bases, these work for fractional exponents just as they do for integer exponents. Expressions with negative bases or even roots require additional care; today's account model uses positive bases throughout.

Use the exponent to interpret time

In your MVP packetSection 3.3 · Main task · Problems 1–11 · pp. 15–16

For \(1.05^t\), the exponent \(t\) counts years. Thus the difference between two exponents represents elapsed time, and adding exponents combines time intervals.

Worked example 7 — From roots to fractional exponents

What does \((1.05)^{1/12}\) mean? Let \(q=(1.05)^{1/12}\). The power rule gives \(q^{12}=1.05\). Therefore \(q\) is the positive twelfth root of 1.05:

\[(1.05)^{1/12}=\sqrt[12]{1.05}.\]

Similarly, \(7^{1/3}=\sqrt[3]{7}\): multiplying that positive number by itself three times gives 7. This is precisely why the denominator of a unit-fraction exponent specifies the root.

2. READY — Exponential forms and equivalent representations (1–11)

Study the worked examples first, then try the problems yourself. An answer key at the bottom of this section is provided to check your work.

In your MVP packetSection 3.3 · Ready · Problems 1–11 · p. 17

A positive whole-number exponent counts the number of identical factors. For example, \(4^3=4\cdot4\cdot4=64\). The exponential form, factor list, and standard numeral are three descriptions of the same value.

Worked example 8 — Convert between the three forms

Write the product of six 2s in exponential and standard forms.

\[2\cdot2\cdot2\cdot2\cdot2\cdot2=2^6=64.\]

Conversely, \(343=7\cdot7\cdot7=7^3\). For numbers such as 81 or 625, different bases may represent the same value: \(81=3^4=9^2\) and \(625=5^4=25^2\). The packet does not always specify a unique base, so more than one valid exponential representation can exist.

To write several equivalent expressions, use the product, quotient, and power rules. They are identities: each representation has the same value wherever it is defined.

Worked example 9 — Find three forms of one power

For \(3^8\), three equivalent expressions are

\[3^5\cdot3^3,\qquad \frac{3^{11}}{3^3},\qquad (3^2)^4.\]

The exponents check out: \(5+3=8\), \(11-3=8\), and \(2\cdot4=8\).

Worked example 10 — A negative or fractional exponent \[13^{-4}=\frac1{13^4}=\frac{13^2}{13^6}=(13^{-2})^2.\]

All three represent the same positive reciprocal. Similarly, \(7^{1/3}=\sqrt[3]7=(7^{1/6})^2=\frac{7^{4/3}}7\). Do not confuse \(7^{1/3}\) with \(\frac{7}{3}\).

3. SET — Decide whether expressions are equivalent (12–17)

Study the worked examples first, then try the problems yourself. An answer key at the bottom of this section is provided to check your work.

In your MVP packetSection 3.3 · Set · Problems 12–17 · p. 18

Equivalent expressions give the same output for every allowed input. To decide, simplify each expression independently using distributive and exponent rules. One matching numerical test does not prove equivalence for all inputs, although one counterexample can prove they are not equivalent.

Worked example 11 — Coefficients and exponent shifts

Compare \(6(2^{x+1})\), \(12(2^x)\), and \(3(2^{x+2})\).

\[6\cdot2^x\cdot2=12\cdot2^x,\qquad 3\cdot2^x\cdot4=12\cdot2^x.\]

All three are equivalent. The key is that changing \(x+1\) to \(x\) removes a factor of 2, which the outside coefficient must compensate for.

Worked example 12 — Negative exponents and reciprocal bases \[48\left(3^{-x}\right)=\frac{48}{3^x}=48\left(\frac13\right)^x.\]

They are equivalent for every real \(x\), because the base 3 is positive and nonzero.

Worked example 13 — Find one mismatch

Compare \(40(2^{x+2})\), \(20(2^{x+3})\), and \(40(4^x)\).

\[40(2^{x+2})=160\cdot2^x,\qquad 20(2^{x+3})=160\cdot2^x.\]

The first two agree. At \(x=0\), the third is \(40(4^0)=40\), while each of the first two is 160. Thus not all three are equivalent. Be especially careful: \(4^x=(2^2)^x=2^{2x}\), not \(2^{x+2}\).

Worked example 14 — Parentheses around an exponent

Compare \(10(1.04^x)\), \(10((1.04^{1/4})^{4x})\), and \(10((1.04^{x/2})^2)\).

\[\left(1.04^{1/4}\right)^{4x}=1.04^{(1/4)(4x)}=1.04^x,\] \[\left(1.04^{x/2}\right)^2=1.04^{(x/2)2}=1.04^x.\]

Therefore all three are equivalent. An important difference is where the exponent is placed: \((1.04^{1/4})^{4x}\) does not mean \(1.04^{1/(4\cdot4x)}\).

Worked example 15 — Linear expressions also require checking \[4(x-3)+7=4x-12+7=4x-5,\] \[4(x-2)-1=4x-8-1=4x-9.\]

Because the constant terms differ, these are not equivalent. Distribute the number outside the parentheses to every term inside.

Additional fully worked Go practice

In your MVP packetSection 3.3 · Go · Problems 18–29 · p. 18
Problem A — Straightforward: products and powers

Simplify \(4^2\cdot4^6\cdot4^3\), then simplify \((m^5)^3\).

\[4^2\cdot4^6\cdot4^3=4^{2+6+3}=\boxed{4^{11}}.\] \[(m^5)^3=m^{5\cdot3}=\boxed{m^{15}}.\]

All factors in the first expression use the same base. For the second, multiplication of exponents is required because there is a power outside parentheses.

Problem B — More challenging: several variables and a quotient

Assuming nonzero denominators, simplify \(\frac{x^7y^2z^4\cdot x^3y^5}{x^4y^3z^2}\).

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{x^7y^2z^4\cdot x^3y^5}{x^4y^3z^2}&=x^{7+3-4}y^{2+5-3}z^{4-2}\\&=\boxed{x^6y^4z^2}.\end{aligned}\]

Work with each variable separately. You may subtract exponents only between powers of the same base.

Problem C — A symbolic power inside a quotient

Simplify \(\frac{(a^4)^5a^2}{a^9}\), assuming \(a\neq0\).

\[\frac{(a^4)^5a^2}{a^9}=\frac{a^{20}a^2}{a^9}=a^{20+2-9}=\boxed{a^{13}}.\]

First multiply the exponents inside and outside the parentheses, then add and subtract as appropriate. Do not distribute the outside exponent by adding.

Problem D — Exponent variables and reciprocals

Simplify \(\frac{u^{r+5}u^{2r-1}}{u^{r+2}}\), for \(u\neq0\).

\[u^{(r+5)+(2r-1)-(r+2)}=\boxed{u^{2r+2}}.\]

Place the entire denominator exponent in parentheses before subtracting to avoid a sign error.

Packet clarifications

Packet clarificationSection 3.3, main task, problems 1–5

The account is described as earning 5% annually and the task asks for equal monthly steps. The intended model assumes a fixed monthly growth factor whose twelfth power is 1.05. This is an effective annual 5% model. A bank quoting a 5% nominal annual rate compounded monthly would instead use a factor of \(1+0.05/12\), giving a different effective yearly rate. The packet is asking for the former.

In problem 5, \(1.004\) is a rounded approximation, not an exact replacement for \(1.05^{1/12}\).

Packet clarificationSection 3.3, Ready, problems 3 and 6

Given only the standard values 81 and 625, the exponential form is not unique unless a base is specified. For example, \(81=3^4=9^2\) and \(625=5^4=25^2\). Choose a correct repeated-factor form; either is mathematically valid.

Teacher's review — Mathematics and wording

Section 3.3 main task, problems 1–5: The phrase “earns 5% annually” is potentially ambiguous without specifying whether 5% is an effective annual yield or a nominal annual rate. The task expects twelve monthly growth factors to multiply to exactly 1.05, which corresponds to an effective annual rate of 5%. A clearer statement would be: “The balance grows by exactly 5% over one year, and we model growth with a constant monthly multiplier.”

Section 3.3 main task, problem 5: It is important to describe \(1.004\) as an approximation, not an equivalent multiplier. The true monthly multiplier is about \(1.00407412\). After one year, use of \(1.004\) produces roughly $314.72 rather than $315. A clearer instruction would explicitly ask students to interpret this rounded factor.

Section 3.3 Ready, problems 3 and 6: Specifying standard form alone permits multiple exponent bases (for example \(81=3^4=9^2\)); accept mathematically correct alternatives or add the intended base.
