Section 7.3 · Main · 1–8 · pages 14–19

Two centers with different jobs

A cyclic polygon has every vertex on one circle. To find a triangle’s circumcenter, construct the perpendicular bisectors of two sides. Their intersection is equally far from all three vertices: equality to the first two vertices and to the last two forces all three distances to agree. Draw the circle with that center through any vertex.

Main 1: do this separately for triangles ABC, MNO, and QRS. Extend the bisectors if needed: the center may be outside an obtuse triangle. The constructions are not drawn onto your packet here. Main 2: the three arcs between consecutive vertices, each opposite one triangle angle, partition the circle, so their measures sum to 360°. The three interior angles sum to 180°.

Why an inscribed angle is half its arc — Main 3

An inscribed angle has its vertex on the circle and its sides along two chords. Its intercepted arc is the arc between the other endpoints that does not contain the vertex. Arc measure is an angle in degrees; arc length is a distance.

Measure the angles in your three constructed circles. Your measurements will vary slightly, but each intercepted arc should measure twice its inscribed angle. Use a protractor at the center to measure the arc via its central angle; for a major arc subtract the minor central angle from 360°.

Case 1 (ABC): call the center O. AB is a diameter. Let ∠BAC = x. Since OA = OC, triangle AOC has equal base angles x, so ∠AOC = 180° − 2x. The adjacent central angle BOC is therefore 2x, and arc BC measures 2x.

Case 2 (MNO): name the far endpoint of the diameter from M “D.” Split ∠NMO into ∠NMD and ∠DMO. Each is Case 1. Their intercepted arcs ND and DO add to arc NO, so adding the two half-arc equations proves the result.

Case 3 (QRS): name the far endpoint of the diameter from Q “D.” Here ∠SQR = ∠SQD − ∠RQD. Each angle on the right is Case 1. Likewise, arc SD through R minus arc RD is arc SR. Subtracting gives the same half-arc rule.

\[m\angle ACB=\frac12m\widehat{AB}\]

In particular, an angle intercepting a semicircle measures 90°, because half of 180° is 90°.

Which quadrilaterals are cyclic? — Main 4–5

For vertices in order around a circle, opposite angles intercept arcs that together make the whole circle. Their sum is half of 360°, so opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral are supplementary.

4a–e: all squares; some rhombuses (exactly the squares); some trapezoids (the isosceles trapezoids); all rectangles; some parallelograms (exactly the rectangles). Here squares count as rhombuses and rectangles. A parallelogram has equal opposite angles, so if it is cyclic each must be 90°. A rectangle’s diagonal intersection is equally far from its vertices, which supplies its circle.

Main 5 proof: in the packet’s ABCD diagram, angle A intercepts arc BCD and angle C intercepts arc DAB. These arcs total 360°, hence A + C = 180°. The same reasoning proves B + D = 180°. To experiment, draw a circle and move four points around it, measuring these sums after each move.

Incircle and tangent lines — Main 6–8

The incenter is the intersection of a triangle’s angle bisectors. A point on an angle bisector is equally far from its two sides, so the incenter is equally far from all three sides. For Main 6, bisect two angles in each of ABC, MNO, and QRS. Drop a perpendicular from the incenter to a side. Its length is the incircle’s radius, and its foot is the tangency point. Repeat for the other sides to mark all three contacts. These packet markings are not drawn here.

Main 7: let O be the center and let BA and BC be tangents at A and C. The radii OA and OC are perpendicular to the tangents: the contact point is the closest point of the tangent line to the center. The quadrilateral OABC has two right angles, so ∠ABC + ∠AOC = 180°. Thus the angle between the tangents is 180° minus the minor arc AC, equivalently half the difference of the major and minor arcs.

This also uses inscribed angles as requested: O,A,B,C lie on the circle with diameter OB, since the angles at A and C are right angles. Opposite angles of that cyclic quadrilateral are supplementary.

Main 8 construction: join an exterior point P to the circle’s center O. Bisect OP and draw the circle with diameter OP. Its intersections T and U with the original circle give two tangent lines PT and PU: each angle OTP or OUP is inscribed in a semicircle and therefore right. The construction needs P outside the circle.

Symmetry and right triangles

A regular n-gon matches itself after each multiple of 360°/n and has n reflection axes. Include 360° as the full-turn match. For the nonsquare rectangle and rhombus shown, the half-turn is the smallest rotation. A rectangle’s reflection axes go through side midpoints; a rhombus’s lie along its diagonals.

For a right triangle, identify opposite, adjacent, and hypotenuse relative to the known angle. Use sine = opposite/hypotenuse, cosine = adjacent/hypotenuse, tangent = opposite/adjacent. In 8, the side 2 is opposite 18°, so the hypotenuse is 2/sin 18° ≈ 6.472 and the other leg is 2/tan 18° ≈ 6.155. The other acute angle is 72°.

Section 7.3 · Set · 11–15 · page 21

Read the vertex before choosing a rule

A central angle equals its arc measure; an inscribed angle equals half its intercepted arc. Subtract known arcs from 360° to find a remaining arc. Three letters on an arc tell you which route to take: the middle point lies along that arc.

Worked example — 11: the 110° central angle LMN gives minor arc LN = 110°. Arc ON = 130°, so ∠OLN = 65°. The remaining arc OL is 360° − 110° − 130° = 120°.

14: NK is a diameter, so triangle NLK is right at L. Its legs are 12 and 5, hence NK = √(12² + 5²) = 13. An angle at N intercepts arc KL: sin ∠KNL = 5/13, so the minor arc KL measures twice arcsin(5/13).

Check your work — 11–15
  1. (a) 110°; (b) 65°; (c) 120°.
  2. (a) 130°; (b) 65°; (c) 100°.
  3. (a) 155°; (b) 205°; ∠GEH = 25°; ∠GFH = 50°.
  4. (a) 13; (b) arc NLK = 180°; (c) arc NJK = 180°; (d) 90°; (e) arc KL ≈ 45.240°; (f) arc NL ≈ 134.760°.
  5. Draw the diameter from the angle’s vertex and use the isosceles-triangle proof in Main 3; add or subtract if needed. Triangle angles sum to 180°; the full circle measures 360°. Those sums alone do not prove the half-arc rule for each individual angle.

Section 7.3 · Go · 16–17 · page 22

Take the same fraction of the circumference or area

A quarter circle has one quarter of the full circumference and one quarter of the full area. A sixth works the same way. For radius 6, the quarter-circle arc is (1/4)(2π·6) = 3π, while its sector area is (1/4)(π·6²) = 9π. Distances have length units; sector areas have square units.

Clarify the routes in 16c and 17c, page 22

The student packet says “go to” each point without specifying every straight segment or boundary arc. The checking key treats center-to-circle portions as radii and the remaining portions as the shorter boundary arcs. State that choice on your work; straight chords give different answers. In 16c the named point after R is Q, not O.
