Section 7.11 · Main · 1–6 · pages 65–67

Volume comes from base area and perpendicular height

A prism has congruent parallel bases. Think of it as equal cross-sectional layers stacked through a perpendicular height h. If the base area is B, each thin layer contributes base area times its thickness; all thicknesses total h. Thus V = Bh. B is an area, not a side length.

Main 1: an equilateral triangular footprint

Chau’s base has sides 2 inches. Its altitude bisects the bottom side, making a right triangle with hypotenuse 2 and one leg 1. By the Pythagorean theorem, the altitude is √(2² − 1²) = √3 inches.

The triangle’s area is (1/2)(2)(√3) = √3 in². Extruding through the prism’s 10-inch height gives V = 10√3 in³, about 17.321 in³. Using the packet’s rectangle gives the same reasoning: its rectangular prism has volume 20√3 in³, and the triangular prism occupies half of it at every level.

Main 2: a regular hexagonal footprint

Join the hexagon’s center to all six vertices. Each central angle is 60°, so the six triangles are equilateral with side 2. Each has area √3 in². The hexagon’s base area is therefore 6√3 in². With height 10 inches, V = 60√3 in³, about 103.923 in³.

Main 3: a general procedure

Find the area B of one base using its dimensions. For a complicated polygon, split the base into triangles or rectangles and add their areas. Then multiply B by the perpendicular distance h between the parallel bases. Use square units for B and cubic units for V. A slanted side edge is not the perpendicular height of an oblique prism.

Main 4–6: six pyramids fill one cube

Join the cube’s center to its eight vertices. Each of the six square faces is the base of a pyramid whose apex is the cube center. Cube symmetry makes those six pyramids congruent, and they fill the cube without overlapping interiors.

4: a cube of side x inches has volume x³ in³. Each pyramid therefore has x³/6 in³. Its base area is x² and its perpendicular height is x/2, the distance from the cube center to a face.

5: a prism with that same square base and height has volume x²(x/2) = x³/2 in³. 6: comparing the two gives:

\[\frac{V_{\text{pyramid}}}{V_{\text{matching prism}}}=\frac{x^3/6}{x^3/2}=\frac13\]

So for this family of pyramids, V = Bh/3. The one-sixth fraction compares with the full cube; the one-third fraction compares with a prism of half the cube’s height. This dissection establishes a special case. Extending it to other base shapes and heights needs the cross-section reasoning in the next section.

The distance formula is the Pythagorean theorem

Between (x₁,y₁) and (x₂,y₂), the horizontal and vertical changes are the legs of a right triangle. Hence d = √[(x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²]. Subtract first, then square; negative changes become positive squares.

1: A(3,−7), B(−9,−2) give Δx = −12, Δy = 5, so d = √(144 + 25) = 13. In 5–6, the coordinates contain square roots: the second coordinates are √2 and √6, respectively.

For 6, d² = 5² + (√6 − 7)² = 25 + 6 − 14√6 + 49 = 80 − 14√6. Leave the outer square root for the exact distance.

Section 7.11 · Set · 7–12 · pages 68–69

Height and slant height do different jobs

Pyramid volume is Bh/3, using perpendicular height h. Surface area adds the base and all triangular faces. Each face uses its own altitude, called a slant height. For a centered apex over a 3-by-4 rectangular base with h = 2, the faces with base 3 have slant height √(2² + 2²) = 2√2; the faces with base 4 have slant height √(2² + 1.5²) = 2.5. Thus SA = 12 + 3(2√2) + 4(2.5) = 22 + 6√2 ft².

Set 7–10, pages 68–69: specify the apex position

The student packet gives dimensions and perspective drawings without explicitly stating that the apex is above the base center. Surface area depends on this position. The main numerical key uses centered right pyramids. If the dashed apex-to-base segment in 7 is instead read as a perpendicular over the rear base vertex, its face heights and surface area differ; that alternative is included below. Volume in 7 remains 8 ft³ when the perpendicular height is 2 ft.

9: for a centered square pyramid, side 10 m and face slant height 12 m give h = √(12² − 5²) = √119 m. Then V = 100√119/3 m³ and SA = 100 + 4(1/2·10·12) = 340 m².

A right circular cone has slant height ℓ = √(r² + h²), volume πr²h/3, and complete surface area πr² + πrℓ. In 11, r = 5 ft, h = 15 ft, and ℓ = 5√10 ft. Similarity multiplies surface area by k² and volume by k³.

Recognize half an equilateral triangle

Cut an equilateral triangle along an altitude. It makes two 30°–60°–90° triangles. If the short leg is a, the hypotenuse is 2a and the long leg is a√3 (from the Pythagorean theorem). Always locate the side opposite each angle before using this pattern.

13: AC = 8 cm is the hypotenuse, so AB = 4 cm and BC = 4√3 cm. 16: all three outer sides are 14 cm, so the triangle is equilateral. Its altitude from D bisects the base into 7 cm pieces and splits its 60° apex angle into 30° and 30°. Thus DG = √(14² − 7²) = 7√3 cm.

Teacher/parent review of the source packet

The student task and teacher notes distinguish the prism formula V = Bh from the special cube dissection for pyramids. The dissection alone does not prove the result for arbitrary heights or base shapes. Ready 5–6 contain radical coordinates that are easy to lose in text extraction. Set’s surface-area calculations require an apex-position assumption, made explicit above; all total surface areas include the base.
