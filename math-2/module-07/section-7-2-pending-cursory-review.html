Why every circle is similar to every other circle

Two figures are similar when a dilation, possibly combined with translations, rotations or reflections, carries one onto the other. A dilation multiplies every distance from its center by one positive scale factor.

Suppose circle A has radius \(r_1\) and circle B has radius \(r_2\). Translate A until its center coincides with B's center. Then dilate about that common center by \(k=\frac{r_2}{r_1}\). Every point formerly \(r_1\) units from the center moves to \(kr_1=r_2\) units from it, exactly the points of circle B. This maps the entire circle, not just a few sample points.

Main 1: The enlargement from A to B has factor \(\frac{r_2}{r_1}\); the reverse reduction has factor \(\frac{r_1}{r_2}\). No numerical radii are supplied in the packet, so these are the appropriate answers.

Main 2 — A single dilation for the packet's tangent circles

Extend the line through centers A and B until it meets the common tangent line through R and S. Call this intersection O. The right triangles OAR and OBS have the same angle at O, so they are similar. Consequently \(\frac{OB}{OA}=\frac{BS}{AR}=\frac{r_2}{r_1}\). A dilation centered at O with that factor sends A to B and the radius \(r_1\) to \(r_2\), so it maps the circles.

This single-center construction works for the unequal circles drawn in the packet. For equal-radius circles with different centers, use the translation argument instead; their center line is parallel to the common tangent, so there is no finite intersection O of those two lines.

Main 3–4 — Map inscribed figures with the same dilation

Sample construction: Choose three distinct points P, Q, T on circle A. Draw rays OP, OQ, OT and mark their dilated images P′, Q′, T′ on circle B. Join the points in each circle. Since one dilation maps the whole triangle, the triangles are similar.

\[\frac{P'Q'}{PQ}=\frac{Q'T'}{QT}=\frac{T'P'}{TP}=k.\]

Ratios within each triangle agree as well, for example \(\frac{PQ}{QT}=\frac{P'Q'}{Q'T'}\). Use corresponding vertices in the same order. More generally, corresponding chords and arcs have the same length scale factor.

Main 5–6 — Why circumference divided by diameter is constant

Under a scale factor \(k\), diameter and circumference both multiply by \(k\). Therefore their ratio stays unchanged:

\[\frac{C_B}{d_B}=\frac{kC_A}{kd_A}=\frac{C_A}{d_A}.\]

That common ratio is the number \(\pi\), so \(C=\pi d=2\pi r\). The circumference of B is \(\frac{r_2}{r_1}\) times A's circumference. If the question is read as asking for a difference rather than a factor, the difference is \(2\pi(r_2-r_1)\).

Main 7 — Areas have a different scale factor

The proposed equality between the area ratio and the circumference ratio is false for the two different-sized circles shown. Lengths multiply by \(k\), but areas multiply by \(k^2\). A dilation stretches both dimensions of a region; for a rectangle, multiplying width and height by \(k\) multiplies their product by \(k^2\). The same area rule applies to similar regions, including disks.

\[\frac{A_B}{A_A}=k^2,\qquad \frac{C_B}{C_A}=k.\]

Original example: If a radius grows from 1 to 2, circumference grows from \(2\pi\) to \(4\pi\), a factor of 2. Area grows from \(\pi\) to \(4\pi\), a factor of 4. The two ratios agree only when \(k=1\) for positive scale factors.
