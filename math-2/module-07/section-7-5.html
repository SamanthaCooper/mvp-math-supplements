Section 7.5 · Main · 1–12 · pages 28–31

More sides bring the boundary closer to a circle

For a regular n-gon inscribed in a circle of radius r, half a central triangle has angle θ = 180°/n, hypotenuse r, half-side x = r sin θ, and apothem h = r cos θ. Use degree mode for these angles. Here n is an integer at least 3.

Main 1: the boundary contains 2n half-sides, so P = 2nx = 2nr sin(180°/n). With r fixed, only the bracketed factor below changes:

\[P=2r\left[n\sin(180^\circ/n)\right]\]

Main 2–3: calculate the factor for each requested n. For n = 12, it is 12 sin 15° ≈ 3.105829. As n grows the chords follow the circle more closely; the perimeter approaches the circumference, and the factor approaches π. Thus C = 2πr. A finite row remains an approximation; rounding to π does not turn a polygon into a circle.

Checking values for n = 6, 12, 24, 48, 96, 100, 1,000, 10,000, in that order: 3.000000, 3.105829, 3.132629, 3.139350, 3.141032, 3.141076, 3.141587, 3.141593.

Area approach 1 — Main 4–6

One full central triangle has base 2r sin θ and height r cos θ. Its area is half their product. Adding n triangles cancels the factor 2:

\[A=nr^2\sin(180^\circ/n)\cos(180^\circ/n)\]

For n = 12, the factor n sin θ cos θ equals 3 exactly. The area is therefore 3r², still below the circle’s area. As n increases, the cosine factor approaches 1 and n sin θ approaches π. The area approaches πr².

Main 5 checking values in the same n order: 2.598076, 3.000000, 3.105829, 3.132629, 3.139350, 3.139526, 3.141572, 3.141592. Main 6: A = πr².

Area approach 2: unroll thin rings — Main 7–9

Imagine cutting the disk into many narrow concentric rings. Cut each ring open and straighten it approximately into a thin strip. Strips nearer the center are shorter; those farther out are longer, because their lengths are approximately their circumferences.

7: stacking the strips makes a shape whose height approaches r, the sum of the rings’ thicknesses. 8: its longest base approaches the outer circumference 2πr. 9: as the strips become thinner, this approaches a triangle of area (1/2)(2πr)r = πr². Finite rings have curved edges; the limiting argument explains why the approximation improves.

Area approach 3: alternate sectors — Main 10–12

Cut an even number of equal sectors and place them in a row, alternating points up and down as in your packet. Half of the original boundary forms the top and half forms the bottom. Increasing the number of sectors flattens the scalloped edges.

10: the height approaches r. 11: the base approaches half the circumference, πr. 12: the area approaches base times height = (πr)r = πr². Both rearrangements preserve the disk’s pieces; the limiting shapes make their areas easier to calculate.

2. Ready

Section 7.5 · Ready · 1–7 · pages 32–33

Use arcs to organize angle information

In the first diagram K is the center. The two given 45° inscribed angles intercept arcs LH and GJ, so each of those arcs measures 90°. The remaining arcs GH and JL must together measure 180°. Every triangle formed by two radii and the chord joining their endpoints is isosceles.

In the second diagram G is the center; AE and BF are diameters. An angle intercepting AF has the same measure wherever its vertex lies on the other arc. Thus ∠ABF, ∠ACF, ∠ADF, and ∠AEF agree. Radius triangles supply equal base angles, and diameter triangles supply right angles.

Worked example — 6: GE = GF, so ∠GEF = ∠GFE = 70°. Therefore ∠EGF = 40° and arc EF = 40°. The opposite rays GA and GE show ∠AGF = 140°. Hence arc AF = 140° and any inscribed angle intercepting it measures 70°. The positions of C and D are not fixed by these data.

Keep correspondence consistent in 7

The matching vertices are B ↔ G, C ↔ K, D ↔ H, E ↔ J, F ↔ L. Corresponding lengths have one common scale factor. For example, BD/BC = GH/GK: both ratios compare corresponding pairs within each figure. A cross-figure ratio must use the same size order on both sides.

Check your work — 1–7
  1. Six examples: arc LH = 90°, arc GJ = 90°, ∠LKH = 90°, ∠GKJ = 90°, ∠LJH = 45°, ∠GLJ = 45°.
  2. GKH is isosceles. Other drawn radius triangles: GKL, JKL, HKL. JKL and HKL are also right isosceles using the givens in 1.
  3. Minor arc JL = 66.8°.
  4. Equal-angle groups: ∠ABF = ∠ACF = ∠ADF = ∠AEF (same arc AF); ∠BAF = ∠AFE = 90° (diameters); ∠GAB = ∠ABG, ∠GAF = ∠AFG, ∠GEF = ∠GFE (radius triangles). Also ∠BGA = ∠EGF and ∠BGE = ∠AGF (vertical angles). Names using a point on the same ray represent the same angle.
  5. Drawn inscribed triangles: ABF, ACF, ADF, AEF. ABF and AEF are right. Other triangles with named vertices: ABG, AFG, EFG, all isosceles. ACF and ADF have no fixed extra classification from the givens.
  6. Arcs AB = EF = 40°; arcs AF = BE = 140°; AE and BF are semicircles (180°). Central angles AGB = EGF = 40°, AGF = BGE = 140°. ∠GAB = ∠ABG = ∠GEF = ∠GFE = 70°; ∠GAF = ∠AFG = 20°; ∠ABF = ∠ACF = ∠ADF = ∠AEF = 70°; ∠BAF = ∠AFE = 90°. Other individual arcs involving C and D are not numerically determined. Write arc BC = u, CD = v, DE = w with u + v + w = 140°; add these pieces to find any other arc and halve the intercepted arc for any drawn inscribed angle.
  7. (a) BD/BC = GH/GK; (b) JL/EF = KL/CF; (c) examples: (i) DE/HJ = BC/GK; (ii) CF/KL = EF/JL; (iii) GH/GK = BD/BC; (iv) BC/BD = GK/GH; (v) HJ/GK = DE/BC. All are possible; other consistent completions work.

3. Set

In your MVP packet

Section 7.5 · Set · 8–12 · pages 33–34

Compare the circle with familiar pieces

8: each radius-side square has area r². The disk contains about 3.14 of these square areas, because its area is πr². Cut and rearrange the squares to estimate this; intact squares need not tile the disk.

9: the octagon, the polygon with the most sides among the five shown, is closest in both area and perimeter. Its shorter chords leave smaller gaps.

Worked example — 10, pentagon: r = 10 ft, n = 5. The interior angle is 108°. Its side is 20 sin 36° ≈ 11.756 ft, and its area is 500 sin 36° cos 36° ≈ 237.764 ft². For every row, keep full calculator precision until the end.

11–12: make the sector and ring sketches described in Main’s two rearrangements. Label the sector arrangement’s limiting base πr and height r; label the ring arrangement’s limiting base 2πr and height r. Your packet sketches are not filled in here.

Check your work — 8–12

8: about π ≈ 3.14 square areas. 9: octagon.

10: each entry gives interior angle; side length in feet; area in square feet.

  • Triangle: 60°; 10√3 ≈ 17.321; 75√3 ≈ 129.904.
  • Square: 90°; 10√2 ≈ 14.142 (supplied); 200.
  • Pentagon: 108°; ≈ 11.756; ≈ 237.764.
  • Hexagon: 120°; 10; 150√3 ≈ 259.808.
  • Octagon: 135°; ≈ 7.654; 200√2 ≈ 282.843.
  • Circle: no polygon side or interior angle; area 100π ≈ 314.159.

11: alternating sectors approach a parallelogram, area (πr)r. 12: unrolled thin rings approach a triangle, area (1/2)(2πr)r. Both give πr².

4. Go

In your MVP packet

Section 7.5 · Go · 13–16 · page 35

Arc length is a fraction of circumference

For θ degrees, arc length s = (θ/360)(2πr). In the supplied radius-3, 80° example on page 34, s = (80/360)(6π) = 4π/3 inches, about 4.19 inches. For 13, the 151° arc has length (151/360)(2π·5) = 151π/36 cm.

Go example, page 34: this is arc length

The student packet says “the area of the sector with a central angle of 80°” before calculating (80)(6π/360). That expression is an arc length in inches. The sector’s area would instead be (80/360)(9π) = 2π square inches. Use the circumference for 13–16.

  3. 232π/45 yd ≈ 16.197 yd.
