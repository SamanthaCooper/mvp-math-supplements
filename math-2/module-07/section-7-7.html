Section 7.7 · Main · 1–10 · pages 40–42

The same fraction controls arc length and sector area

If a sector’s central angle is θ degrees, it uses θ/360 of a full turn. It therefore contains that same fraction of the circle’s area and circumference:

\[A_{\text{sector}}=\frac{\theta}{360}\pi r^2\]\[s=\frac{\theta}{360}\cdot2\pi r\]

The curved edge s is the arc length. It excludes the two straight radii. Use the radius, half the pie’s stated diameter, in both formulas. To compare amounts of pie here, compare areas, assuming equal thickness.

Main 1: compare the four slices

The 8-inch round pie has r = 4 and five slices, so each area is 16π/5 ≈ 10.053 in². The 9-inch pie has r = 4.5 and six slices, so each area is 27π/8 ≈ 10.603 in². The 2-by-4 rectangle has area 8 in². The 3-by-3¼ rectangle has area 39/4 = 9.75 in². The 9-inch round slice is largest; the 2-by-4 rectangle is smallest.

Main 2–3: allow four degrees either way

The ideal 8-inch slice angle is 360°/5 = 72°. Accepting deviations of at most 4° gives 68° through 76°. Multiply each endpoint’s fraction by 16π:

\[A_{\min}=\frac{136\pi}{45}\approx9.495\text{ in}^2\]\[A_{\max}=\frac{152\pi}{45}\approx10.612\text{ in}^2\]

For the 9-inch pie, the ideal angle is 60°, so the range is 56° through 64°. Areas range from 63π/20 ≈ 9.896 in² to 18π/5 ≈ 11.310 in². A fixed degree error changes more area in the larger-radius pie.

Main 4–5: measure the curved crust

For the five equal 8-inch slices, use one fifth of the circumference: string length 8π/5 ≈ 5.027 inches. For the six equal 9-inch slices, use one sixth: 9π/6 = 3π/2 ≈ 4.712 inches. The string follows the crust, not the straight chord joining the cuts.

Main 6–9: work backward from 15 square inches

Area increases with angle at a fixed radius. The smallest satisfactory slice therefore has area exactly 15 in². For radius 4:

\[15=\frac{\theta}{360}\cdot16\pi\]\[\theta=\frac{675}{2\pi}\approx107.430^\circ\]

This is 6. For 7, use the same fraction in the circumference formula, or divide the two sector formulas to get A = rs/2. Thus s = 2A/r = 30/4 = 7.5 inches.

For 8, r = 4.5 gives θ = 800/(3π) ≈ 84.883°. For 9, s = 30/4.5 = 20/3 inches. The larger pie needs a smaller angle for the same area.

Main 10: use a variable angle

For the radius-4.5 pie with angle n degrees:

\[A=\frac{9\pi n}{160}\text{ in}^2\]\[s=\frac{\pi n}{40}\text{ in}\]

For a single sector, 0 ≤ n ≤ 360. The formulas use n as its numerical degree measure; substituting a radian value directly would change the meaning.

Section 7.7 · Ready · 1–6 · page 43

Length scales once; area scales twice

For radii 1, 2, 3, 4, apply C = 2πr and A = πr². Doubling r doubles circumference and quadruples area. The ratio C/r is always 2π; A/r² is always π.

For 2–6, preserve the order of the words. If a class has 12 boys and 15 girls, boys:girls is 12:15 = 4:5. Use your actual counts for your responses. A ratio such as 0:3 can be written, but division by a zero second quantity is undefined; explain that case rather than assigning a finite quotient.

Check your work — 1–6

1: circumferences 2π, 4π, 6π, 8π; areas π, 4π, 9π, 16π, in matching radius order. Appropriate length and square units apply.

2: boys:girls in your class. 3: girls:boys in your family. 4: bathrooms:bedrooms. 5: televisions:people. 6: people:cell phones. Responses depend on your counts; simplify by dividing both counts by the same common factor.

Undo the circle fraction before solving for radius

9: 72° is one fifth of a circle. If its sector area is 20π ft², the whole area is 100π ft², so r² = 100 and r = 10 ft.

10: a 120° arc is one third of the circumference. Thus C = 3·16π = 48π cm, r = 24 cm, and area = 576π cm². In 11, use 100/360 of the circumference, not of the area: 30 = (100/360)C, giving C = 108 m and r = 54/π m.

Section 7.7 · Go · 13–16 · page 45

Subtract the light regions from the disk

Circle-minus-polygon area gives the dark curved pieces. For a regular n-gon with circumradius r, polygon area is (n/2)r² sin(360°/n). In 14, interpreting the shown octagon as regular gives area 200√2 yd², so the dark region has area 100π − 200√2 yd².

Go 13–16, page 45: identify the shape assumptions

The student directions only say “Find the area of the darkest shaded region.” They do not state that the polygons in 13–14 are regular, mark the quadrilateral in 15 as a rectangle, or give enough information to fix triangle FGH’s area in 16. The numerical checks below explicitly state those additional assumptions. A drawing alone does not establish equal sides or angles.

For 15, if the quadrilateral is a rectangle, its diagonal is the circle’s diameter 10 in. A side is 5 in, so the other side is √(100 − 25) = 5√3 in. The dark area is 25π − 25√3 in².

For 16, triangle CDE has two radii 5 cm with included angle 60°, giving area 25√3/4 cm². FH is a diameter through D, so triangle FGH is right at G, but its leg lengths are not determined. In general, subtract (FG·GH)/2 as well. If FGH is additionally right isosceles, its legs are 5√2 and its area is 25 cm².

Check your work — 13–16
  1. If the pentagon is regular: 64π − 160 sin 72° m² ≈ 48.893 m².
  2. If the octagon is regular: 100π − 200√2 yd² ≈ 31.317 yd².
  3. If the quadrilateral is a rectangle: 25π − 25√3 in² ≈ 35.239 in².
  4. From the labeled data: 25π − 25√3/4 − (FG·GH)/2 cm². If FGH is right isosceles: 25π − 25 − 25√3/4 cm² ≈ 42.714 cm². The extra isosceles condition is needed for that numerical value.

Teacher/parent review of the source packet

The teacher notes use proportional reasoning before general formulas. Treat “8-inch” and “9-inch” pans as diameters and compare equal-thickness pie areas. The student wording about rejecting deviations on page 40 is awkward; Main 2–3 use an inclusive ±4° tolerance, matching the teacher notes’ 76° example. Go 13–16 need the extra shape conditions identified above; numerical values are conditional rather than inferred from appearance.
