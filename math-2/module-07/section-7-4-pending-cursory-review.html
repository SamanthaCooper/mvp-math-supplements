Section 7.4 · Main · 1–5 · pages 23–25

Break the polygon into triangles

A regular polygon has equal sides and equal interior angles. Join its center to every vertex. This divides a regular n-gon into n congruent isosceles triangles: each has two radii r and central angle 360°/n. Their equality follows from side–angle–side.

Draw the perpendicular from the center to one side. This segment is the apothem, a. It bisects the side and the central angle. The resulting right triangle has hypotenuse r, opposite leg s/2, adjacent leg a, and angle θ = 180°/n at the center. Here s is the full polygon side length.

Main 1: check all four claims

Agree with each statement. Rotational symmetry makes the six central angles equal, so each is 60°. The six radius triangles are congruent isosceles triangles. In half of one triangle, the center angle is 30°: cosine gives the altitude a = 2 cos 30° = √3, and sine gives half a chord s/2 = 2 sin 30° = 1. Thus trigonometry finds both the altitude and the polygon side.

Main 2–3: radius-2 hexagon

Each side is 2, so P = 6·2 = 12 inches. One radius triangle has base 2 and height √3, hence area √3. Six such triangles give 6√3 square inches, about 10.392. This is the area of the hexagon, which is smaller than the enclosing circle’s area 4π.

Main 4: change six sides to eight

The central angle becomes 45° and its half is 22.5°. With radius still 2, s = 4 sin 22.5° and a = 2 cos 22.5°. Therefore:

\[P=32\sin22.5^\circ\approx12.246\text{ in}\]\[A=\tfrac12aP=8\sqrt2\approx11.314\text{ in}^2\]

The octagon uses eight triangles instead of six; the strategy is unchanged.

Main 5: generalize the method

For an integer n ≥ 3 and radius r > 0, use degree mode:

\[s=2r\sin(180^\circ/n)\]\[a=r\cos(180^\circ/n)\]\[P=ns=2nr\sin(180^\circ/n)\]\[A=\tfrac12aP=nr^2\sin(180^\circ/n)\cos(180^\circ/n)\]

Why A = aP/2? Add n triangle areas: n(as/2) = a(ns)/2. The same apothem serves as every triangle’s height. If a side length s is given instead of radius, tangent gives a = s/[2 tan(180°/n)], so A = ns²/[4 tan(180°/n)].

2. Ready

In your MVP packet

Section 7.4 · Ready · 1–6 · page 26

Recover the radius first

Use A = πr² and C = 2πr. Given area, divide by π before taking a square root: r = √(A/π). Given circumference, divide by 2π. For example, in 2, r² = 9π/π = 9, so r = 3 feet and C = 6π feet. A length is positive; discard a negative square root.

Ready 4, page 26: distinguish 3.14 from π

The student packet gives “A = 3.14 m².” Taken exactly, r = √(3.14/π) m ≈ 0.999746 m. Using π ≈ 3.14 gives r ≈ 1 m and C ≈ 6.28 m. Label the approximation.

  3. r = 4 yd; A = 16π yd².

  5. A = 49π mi²; C = 14π mi.
  6. r = 81/2 in; A = 6561π/4 in².

Section 7.4 · Set · 7–12 · pages 26–27

Choose the formula for the given measurement

The interior angle is 180° − 360°/n. With side s given, P = ns and a = s/[2 tan(180°/n)]. In 8, a pentagon has side 6 cm. Its interior angle is 108°, P = 30 cm, a = 3/tan 36°, and A = 45/tan 36° ≈ 61.937 cm².

In 9, the perpendicular segment labeled 10 inches is the apothem, not the circumradius. Use half-side = 10 tan 22.5°, so P = 160 tan 22.5° and A = 800 tan 22.5°.

Section 7.4 · Go · 13–16 · page 27

Sector area is a fraction of circle area

A sector with central angle θ degrees occupies θ/360 of its circle. Therefore Asector = (θ/360)πr². The supplied example uses r = 3 inches and θ = 80°: (80/360)π·9 = 2π square inches. The right-angle marker in Set 9 has a different role from a sector’s central angle.

For 13, use the labeled 160° region: (160/360)π·5² = 100π/9 square inches. Do not use the complementary 200° sector.

  1. 100π/9 in².
  2. 25π/3 cm².
  3. 931π/72 m².
  4. 17π/9 m².
