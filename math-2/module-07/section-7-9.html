Section 7.9 · Main · 1–7 · pages 52–54

One radian is one radius-length of arc

Radian measure x is the ratio s/r, with arc length s and radius r in the same unit. Main 5: if s = r, then x = 1. Main 2 and 6: a full turn has s = 2πr, so x = 2πr/r = 2π radians, about 6.283. A larger circle changes both lengths by the same factor, leaving the angle unchanged.

A half-turn is therefore π radians and a quarter-turn is π/2 radians. These are exact measures; decimal values are approximations.

Main 1, 3–4: derive the conversion factors

If an angle measures d degrees, its arc is (d/360)2πr. Dividing by radius cancels r:

\[x=\frac{d}{360}\cdot2\pi=\frac{d\pi}{180}\]

Thus Main 3 converts degrees to radians by multiplying by π/180. Solving for d gives Main 4: multiply radians by 180/π to get degrees.

\[d=\frac{180x}{\pi}\]

For Main 1, x = 1 gives d = 180/π ≈ 57.296°. This lies between 50° and 60°, as the given decimal radian values suggest. It is not exactly 57.3°.

Worked conversion: 140° × π/180° = 7π/9 radians. Backward: (7π/9) × 180°/π = 140°. Choose the factor that cancels the unit you start with.

Main 7: label every 10° tick

Each 10° adds π/18 radians. Start at the right-hand 0° tick and move counterclockwise to 180° on the left. Reduce each fraction. For example, 40° = 4π/18 = 2π/9. The labels are:

  • 0°, 10°, 20°, 30°, 40°, 50°: 0, π/18, π/9, π/6, 2π/9, 5π/18.
  • 60°, 70°, 80°, 90°, 100°, 110°: π/3, 7π/18, 4π/9, π/2, 5π/9, 11π/18.
  • 120°, 130°, 140°, 150°, 160°, 170°, 180°: 2π/3, 13π/18, 7π/9, 5π/6, 8π/9, 17π/18, π.

Your packet’s protractor is not relabeled here; transfer these labels to its ticks. Two rays’ labels give their measures from the zero ray. The angle between two other rays is the difference between their labels.

Keep three circle quantities distinct

Arc degree measure equals its central angle; an inscribed angle is half its intercepted arc. Arc length is a distance, found by multiplying the radian measure by radius. Sector area is half of radius squared times the radian measure.

2: each marked 30° inscribed angle intercepts a 60° arc. The upper semicircle has 180°, so subtracting the two end arcs EF and HG leaves arc FH = 60° as well. With radius 7 cm, its length is (60/360)(14π) = 7π/3 cm.

4: convert 40° to 2π/9 radians. With r = 13 m, arc length is 26π/9 m; sector area is (1/2)(169)(2π/9) = 169π/9 m².

  3. Arc JL measures 125° and has length 125π/18 m; its angle is 25π/36 radians.
  4. Sector area 169π/9 m²; arc length 26π/9 m.

Leave π exact when it simplifies

Section 7.9 · Go · 14–15 · page 56

Find the rotation center using perpendicular bisectors

A rotation keeps each point and its image equally far from the center. Connect two corresponding pairs of vertices and construct each connecting segment’s perpendicular bisector. Their intersection is the center. Check that the third pair has the same distance and the same turning angle. Then draw one circle for each distinct vertex radius; both members of each pair lie on their circle.

14: read the left triangle’s vertices as (−4,2), (−1,1), (−1,5), and match them to (3,9), (2,6), (6,6). The center is (3,2). For example, (−4,2) is 7 units left of the center; a 90° clockwise turn sends it 7 units up to (3,9).

15: using A(7,2), B(6,6), C(4,6), the bisectors meet near (3,−1). A 60° counterclockwise rotation about that center predicts A′ ≈ (2.402,3.964), B′ ≈ (−1.562,5.098), C′ ≈ (−2.562,3.366), consistent with the noninteger image points. Do not round the image points to grid intersections before constructing the bisectors.

Check your work — 14–15

14: center (3,2), 90° clockwise. Draw concentric circles of radii 7, √17, and 5 through the respective vertex pairs.

15: center approximately (3,−1), 60° counterclockwise. Vertex radii: OA = 5, OB = √58, OC = √50. The compass circles are not drawn on the packet here.

Teacher/parent review of the source packet

The student task and teacher notes develop radians from s/r on any circle before unit-circle trigonometry. Ready 2–3 use the word “measure” without consistently specifying angular measure versus length; both are identified in the key. Go 15 image coordinates are noninteger and its center is read approximately from the construction.

The teacher guide’s 7.9 Explore paragraph says the circumference and radius cannot both be rational “since their ratio is π.” The ratio of circumference to radius is 2π; circumference to diameter is π. Its irrationality conclusion remains true for a nonzero circle, but the named ratio needs that correction.
