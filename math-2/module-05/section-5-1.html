You may have memorized that a triangle’s three angles add to 180°. This lesson is about the next question: why must this be true for every triangle? Geometry uses diagrams, established facts, and logical explanations—not just measurements—to answer questions like this.

1. In class: A Develop Understanding Task

Revisit the ideas from class

This section explains some of the ideas you explored in class through the guided exercises, in a more formal, step-by-step way. Use it alongside the “A Develop Understanding Task” portion of Section 5.1 in your MVP packet.

From an observation to a mathematical explanation

In your MVP packetSection 5.1 · Main task · Problems 1–6 · pp. 1–3

If you cut three corners off a paper triangle and put them together, they appear to form a straight angle. Repeating the experiment with different triangles supplies evidence for the statement. But it does not establish the statement for every imaginable triangle: we have tested only finitely many triangles, and cutting and measuring introduce small errors.

A conjecture is a mathematical statement believed to be true based on patterns or evidence. A proof is a logical argument that shows a statement must be true, using accepted definitions and mathematical facts. A result established by proof is called a theorem. Today we develop the reasoning behind an important theorem without requiring a formal two-column proof.

Worked example 1 — Is this convincing for every triangle?

Statement: “I measured the angles of five triangles, and each total was approximately 180°. Therefore every triangle has angle sum 180°.”

What works: the five measurements provide a good reason to investigate the claim.

What is missing: the examples do not cover every possible triangle. To establish a universal rule, we need reasoning that applies to an arbitrary triangle.

The interior angles of a triangle

In your MVP packetSection 5.1 · Main task · Problems 1–6 · pp. 1–3

An interior angle lies inside a figure at one of its vertices. Label the three angles of a triangle \(\alpha\), \(\beta\), and \(\gamma\).

Triangle interior angle-sum theorem\[\boxed{\alpha+\beta+\gamma=180^\circ}\]

A triangle can be tall, flat, acute, right, or obtuse; its three interior angles still have the same sum.

Worked example 2 — Find a missing triangle angle

Two angles of a triangle are 48° and 67°. Find the third angle.

\[\begin{aligned}48^\circ+67^\circ+x&=180^\circ\\\115^\circ+x&=180^\circ\\x&=65^\circ.\end{aligned}\]

Check: \(48+67+65=180\).

A convincing angle-sum argument

In your MVP packetSection 5.1 · Main task · Problems 1–6 · pp. 1–3

Imagine placing three congruent copies of a triangle so the three different interior angles meet at one point along a straight line. If those copies really are congruent and their rays line up, the angles fill exactly one straight angle, or 180°. The paper-corner experiment suggests this arrangement; the half-turn construction below explains why matching angles and straight edges appear.

Half-turns explain the picture

In your MVP packetSection 5.1 · Main task · Problems 1–6 · pp. 1–3

A rotation turns a figure around a fixed point. A 180° rotation, or half-turn, places every point on the opposite side of the center at the same distance. It preserves lengths and angle measures. The midpoint of a segment lies halfway between its endpoints.

Begin with arbitrary triangle \(ABC\). Locate the midpoint of side \(BC\) and rotate the whole triangle 180° about that point. Points \(B\) and \(C\) exchange places; point \(A\) moves to \(A'\). Next, rotate the new triangle 180° about the midpoint of side \(CA'\). This sends \(B\) to \(B'\).

First half-turn: The segments \(AB\) and \(CA'\) are parallel and equal in length; \(AC\) and \(BA'\) are also parallel and equal in length. Thus \(ABA'C\) is a parallelogram.
Second half-turn: The segments \(BA'\) and \(CB'\) are parallel; because \(AC\) is parallel to \(BA'\) from the first turn, \(A,C,B'\) lie on a single straight line. The three angles of the original triangle appear next to each other around point \(C\).
Conclude: Rotations preserve angles, so the three adjacent angles at \(C\) correspond to the original angles at \(C\), \(B\), and \(A\). They form a straight angle. Therefore, \[\boxed{\alpha+\beta+\gamma=180^\circ}\]

More precisely, at \(C\), the adjacent angles are the original angles at \(C\), \(B\), and \(A\), in that order. This reasoning works regardless of the starting triangle’s side lengths or angles. The construction itself is a way to justify the general claim rather than testing one measurement.

Why rotate about the midpoint? A half-turn centered at a side’s midpoint exchanges that side’s endpoints. Turning around a different point would not create the same edge-to-edge arrangement.

What figures do the rotations make?

In your MVP packetSection 5.1 · Main task · Problems 1–6 · pp. 1–3

Parallel lines lie in one plane and do not meet. A parallelogram is a quadrilateral with two pairs of opposite sides parallel. A trapezoid is often defined in school geometry as a quadrilateral with at least one pair of opposite sides parallel, although some textbooks require exactly one pair.

Worked example 3 — Why is the middle figure a parallelogram?

The first rotation takes \(A\) to \(A'\) and swaps \(B\) and \(C\). Its image of side \(AB\) is side \(A'C\), and its image of side \(AC\) is side \(A'B\).

A 180° rotation makes the image of each line parallel to the original line. Therefore \(AB\) is parallel to \(A'C\), and \(AC\) is parallel to \(BA'\). Both pairs of opposite sides of \(ABA'C\) are parallel: it is a parallelogram.

Worked example 4 — Why is the final outline a trapezoid?

In the final diagram, the upper segment \(BA'\) is parallel to the lower segment \(AB'\). The lower edge is a straight segment made from \(AC\) and \(CB'\). Thus the outer quadrilateral \(ABA'B'\) has a pair of parallel opposite sides, so it is a trapezoid.

For an ordinary nondegenerate triangle, the other two outer sides are not parallel, so it also satisfies the definition requiring exactly one pair.

A line continues forever in both directions; a line segment has two endpoints; a ray has one endpoint and continues forever in one direction. Real-world edges are finite segments, even if we model them with ideal geometric lines.

3. Set — creases, intersections, straight angles, and linear pairs (problems 2, 3, packet “14,” and 5)

Study the worked examples first, then try the problems yourself. An answer key at the bottom of this section is provided to check your work.

In your MVP packetSection 5.1 · Set · Problems 2, 3, “14,” and 5 · pp. 4–5

A fold in a flat piece of paper forms a straight crease segment when the fold is made along a straight line. It is not literally an infinite geometric line because the paper has edges; extending its path would model a line. When a flat wall meets a flat ceiling, their intersection is modeled by a line (the visible seam is a line segment).

A straight angle

In your MVP packetSection 5.1 · Set · Problems 2, 3, “14,” and 5 · pp. 4–5

An angle is formed by two rays with a common endpoint, the vertex. A straight angle consists of two opposite rays and measures exactly \( 180^\circ\). A diameter across a circle is a segment, not itself an angle; its two halves can be viewed as opposite rays starting at the center, and those rays form a straight angle.

Straight angle\[\boxed{180^\circ}\]

Half a full turn (360°) is 180°. That is why opposite rays make a straight angle.

Adjacent angles and linear pairs

In your MVP packetSection 5.1 · Set · Problems 2, 3, “14,” and 5 · pp. 4–5

Adjacent angles share a vertex and one side, with nonoverlapping interiors. A linear pair is a pair of adjacent angles whose two nonshared sides are opposite rays. The combined angle is straight.

Linear-pair rule\[\boxed{m_1+m_2=180^\circ}\]

The angles are supplementary because their measures add to 180°. All linear pairs are supplementary, but supplementary angles do not have to be adjacent.

Worked example 5 — Sketch a real-life linear pair

Imagine a straight shelf with a thin upright bracket rising from a point on the shelf. Draw the shelf as a horizontal line and the bracket as a ray upward from the shared point.

The angles on the left and right share the bracket and together occupy the straight angle above the shelf. They form a linear pair. A second example is a straight road edge with a slanted road marking extending from a point on it.

Drawing check: show the shared vertex, one common ray, and two opposite outer rays.

  2. The intersection of the ideal flat wall and ceiling is a line; the visible portion is a segment.

4. Go — algebra of linear pairs (problems 6–14)

Study the worked examples first, then try the problems yourself. An answer key at the bottom of this section is provided to check your work.

In your MVP packetSection 5.1 · Go · Problems 6–14 · p. 6

To find the missing angle in a linear pair, subtract the known angle from 180°. If an angle measures \(x^\circ\), its supplement is \((180-x)^\circ\). Supplementary angles may be drawn separately; they are a linear pair only if they have the required positions.

Worked example 6 — Find the missing angle

One angle of a linear pair is 60°. Find the other.

\[x+60^\circ=180^\circ\]\[\boxed{x=120^\circ}\]

The two angles share a straight line: \( 60+120=180 \).

Worked example 7 — Draw a supplement

Given \(m\angle ABC=72^\circ\), determine the supplement and describe how to draw a linear pair, with B as vertex.

\[\boxed{180^\circ-72^\circ=108^\circ}\]

Draw opposite rays \(BA\) and \(BD\) with B between A and D. Draw ray \(BC\) above the line so \(\angle ABC=72^\circ\); the adjoining \(\angle CBD=108^\circ\). The letters identify the ray endpoints; the center letter is the vertex.

Worked example 8 — Supplementary does not always mean linear pair

Two separate angles measure 25° and 155°. Are they supplementary? Must they form a linear pair?

Yes, they are supplementary because \( 25+155=180 \). No, they need not form a linear pair: unless they share one vertex and side with their other sides forming opposite rays, they do not satisfy the definition.

Additional fully worked Go practice

In your MVP packetSection 5.1 · Go · Problems 6–14 · p. 6
Problem A — Straightforward

Angles \(PQR\) and \(RQS\) form a linear pair. If \(m\angle PQR=137^\circ\), find \(m\angle RQS\).

Write the sum, then subtract:

\( 137^\circ+x=180^\circ\)\[\boxed{x=43^\circ}\]

Check: \( 137+43=180 \). The angle at Q has measure 43°.

Problem B — More challenging

A linear pair has angle measures \((3x+12)^\circ\) and \((2x+18)^\circ\). Find \(x\) and both angle measures.

\[\begin{aligned}(3x+12)+(2x+18)&=180\\5x+30&=180\\5x&=150\\x&=30\\3(30)+12&=102^\circ\\2(30)+18&=78^\circ.\end{aligned}\]

Check: \(102+78=180\). Common mistake: \(x=30\) is not one of the angle measures; substitute back into both expressions.

Problem C — Drawing from angle notation

Draw a linear pair containing \(\angle XYZ=24^\circ\), where Y is the vertex.

Step 1: Draw line segment X–Y–W, with rays YX and YW in opposite directions. Step 2: Draw ray YZ on one side of the line making a 24° angle with YX. Step 3: Mark the adjacent angle \(\angle ZYW\) as \( 180^\circ-24^\circ=156^\circ\). The pair meets all three requirements: one vertex, one common side, and opposite nonshared rays.

Packet clarification — Section 5.1, Set, straight-angle description (page 4)A line segment is not itself an angle.

The packet refers to a diameter as “a special kind of angle.” More precisely, a diameter is a line segment. The two opposite rays at its center determine the 180° straight angle.
