Four special lines and segments in a triangle

In your MVP packetSection 5.3 · Main task · Problems 1–9 · pp. 15–19

A triangle has several useful constructions. They can coincide in a highly symmetric triangle, but their definitions are different. Learn what each definition requires before beginning a proof.

ObjectDefinitionWhat must be shown
AltitudeA segment from a vertex perpendicular to the line containing the opposite sideStarts at the vertex and forms a right angle
MedianA segment from a vertex to the midpoint of the opposite sideThe opposite side is divided into two congruent segments
Angle bisectorA ray or segment from a vertex dividing its angle into two equal anglesThe two smaller angles are congruent
Perpendicular bisectorA line perpendicular to a segment at its midpointBoth a right angle and two congruent half-segments
Worked example 1 — Which names apply?

In triangle \(PQR\), point \(M\) lies on \(\overline{QR}\). Suppose \(QM=MR\) and \(PM\perp QR\). Since \(M\) is the midpoint of \(QR\), \(\overline{PM}\) is a median. Since it makes a right angle with \(QR\), it is also an altitude. The line \(PM\) is the perpendicular bisector of \(QR\).

Do these facts alone show \(PM\) bisects \(\angle QPR\)? Yes: right triangles \(PMQ\) and \(PMR\) have \(PM\) in common, \(QM=MR\), and equal included right angles. They are congruent by SAS, so \(\angle QPM\cong\angle MPR\).

Common confusion: A median need not be perpendicular, and an altitude need not land at a midpoint. Also, a perpendicular bisector of a side need not pass through the opposite vertex.

Turn equality of parts into triangle congruence

In your MVP packetSection 5.3 · Main task · Problems 1–9 · pp. 15–19

Two triangles are congruent when corresponding sides and angles agree. The order of vertex names in a congruence statement tells the reader which vertices correspond: \(\triangle ABC\cong\triangle DEF\) means \(A\leftrightarrow D\), \(B\leftrightarrow E\), \(C\leftrightarrow F\).

Congruence criterionInformation required
SSSThree pairs of corresponding sides
SASTwo pairs of corresponding sides and their included angle
ASATwo pairs of corresponding angles and the included side

After establishing triangle congruence, you may use corresponding parts of congruent triangles are congruent (often abbreviated CPCTC) to prove an angle or side equality.

Worked example 2 — Match vertices carefully

Given \(JL\cong QS\), \(LM\cong SR\), and \(\angle L\cong\angle S\). The correspondence is \(J\leftrightarrow Q\), \(L\leftrightarrow S\), \(M\leftrightarrow R\). The angle \(L\) is included between \(JL\) and \(LM\), so

\[\boxed{\triangle JLM\cong\triangle QSR\quad\text{by SAS}}.\]

Writing \(\triangle JLM\cong\triangle QRS\) would be wrong: it would incorrectly pair \(L\) with \(R\).

Use a flow diagram to organize reasons (main task, problems 1–8)

In your MVP packetSection 5.3 · Main task · Problems 1–9 · pp. 15–19

The packet starts with an equilateral triangle \(ABC\), and a fold that reflects the triangle onto itself through the line containing \(C\) and \(D\), where \(D\) lies on side \(AB\). A reflection fixes points on its mirror line and sends \(A\) to \(B\). Therefore that mirror line crosses \(\overline{AB}\) at its midpoint and at a right angle.

Flow diagrams are statements connected by reasons.

Begin with the givens. Follow arrows to results justified by definitions or known properties. When two facts are needed together (such as a midpoint and perpendicularity), show both feeding the next result.

Worked example 3 — Four conclusions from one fold

Givens: \(\triangle ABC\) is equilateral; the line containing \(CD\) is a reflection line of the triangle, and \(D\) lies on \(AB\).

FactJustification
\(AD\cong DB\)Reflection interchanges \(A\) and \(B\) and fixes \(D\); corresponding distances are equal
\(D\) is the midpoint of \(AB\)Definition of midpoint
\(CD\perp AB\)A reflection axis that interchanges \(A\) and \(B\) is their perpendicular bisector
\(\angle ACD\cong\angle BCD\)The reflection maps \(CA\) to \(CB\) while fixing \(CD\)

Now apply the definitions: \(CD\) is a median because \(D\) is a midpoint; an altitude because \(CD\perp AB\); an angle bisector because the two angles at \(C\) are congruent; and the line \(CD\) is the perpendicular bisector of \(AB\) because it passes through its midpoint at right angles.

Worked example 4 — A second reasoning route using congruent triangles

Equilateral means \(AC\cong BC\). Reflection provides \(AD\cong DB\), and \(CD\cong CD\) by the reflexive property (a segment equals itself). Consequently,

\[\triangle ACD\cong\triangle BCD\qquad(\text{SSS}).\]

By corresponding parts, \(\angle ACD\cong\angle BCD\) and \(\angle ADC\cong\angle BDC\). Since \(A,D,B\) lie on a straight line, the latter two angles form a linear pair and add to \(180^\circ\); equal supplementary angles must each be \(90^\circ\). Thus \(CD\perp AB\). This is a proof using triangle congruence rather than relying only on the picture.

Which reasons actually belong in the flow? The packet provides a long choice bank, including rotations and translations. For this diagram, the useful reasons include equilateral-triangle properties, reflection, midpoint, perpendicularity, SSS, reflexive property, CPCTC, and the definitions of the four special segments. A listed reason is not automatically relevant.

Convert a flow diagram to a two-column proof (main task, problem 9)

In your MVP packetSection 5.3 · Main task · Problems 1–9 · pp. 15–19

A two-column proof makes the same logical chain more linear. Put the claim in the left column and the reason beside it. Start from the givens, and never use a fact before it is established.

Worked example 5 — Prove \(CD\) is a median
StatementReason
1. \(\triangle ABC\) is equilateral; \(CD\) is a line of reflection, with \(D\in\overline{AB}\).Given
2. \(AD\cong DB\)Reflection swaps \(A\) and \(B\), fixes \(D\), and preserves distance
3. \(D\) is the midpoint of \(AB\)Definition of midpoint
4. \(CD\) is a median of \(\triangle ABC\)Definition of median

The purpose of the table is not to make the reasoning more complicated; it lets the reader test each connection independently.

Worked example 6 — Prove the perpendicular-bisector claim
StatementReason
1. Reflection across \(CD\) interchanges \(A\) and \(B\).Given reflection symmetry
2. \(D\) is the midpoint of \(AB\).Reflection axis between a point and its image bisects their connecting segment
3. \(CD\perp AB\).Reflection axis between a point and its image is perpendicular to their connecting segment
4. The line \(CD\) is the perpendicular bisector of \(AB\).Definition of perpendicular bisector

For Tehani's angle-bisector claim, use the reflected rays to show \(\angle ACD\cong\angle BCD\), then cite the angle-bisector definition. For Clarita's altitude claim, use \(CD\perp AB\) and the altitude definition.

Worked example 7 — Included-angle SAS

Suppose \(AB\cong DE\), \(BC\cong EF\), and \(\angle B\cong\angle E\). The pairings are \(A\leftrightarrow D\), \(B\leftrightarrow E\), and \(C\leftrightarrow F\). Thus \(\boxed{\triangle ABC\cong\triangle DEF\text{ by SAS}}\).

Worked example 8 — All three sides (SSS)

Suppose \(PQ\cong XY\), \(QR\cong YZ\), and \(PR\cong XZ\). Then \(P\leftrightarrow X\), \(Q\leftrightarrow Y\), \(R\leftrightarrow Z\), so \(\boxed{\triangle PQR\cong\triangle XYZ\text{ by SSS}}\).

Read the markings, not the appearance (7–10)

In your MVP packetSection 5.3 · Set · Problems 7–14 · pp. 21–22

The Set diagram of \(\triangle GHM\) contains \(E\) on \(HM\) and \(D\) on \(GM\). The right-angle markings show \(GH\perp HM\) and \(ED\perp GM\), so \(\overline{GH}\) and \(\overline{ED}\) are altitudes of \(\triangle GHM\) (the latter from a point \(E\) on the opposite side, not a vertex of triangle \(GHM\), so it is not itself an altitude of \(\triangle GHM\)). The altitude from vertex \(H\) would have to meet line \(GM\) at a right angle. The angle arcs at \(G\) indicate \(GE\) bisects \(\angle HGM\).

For problem 10, a segment becomes a perpendicular bisector only when you mark its intersection with a side as the midpoint in addition to the shown right angle. In this diagram, mark \(GD\cong DM\), making line \(ED\) the perpendicular bisector of \(GM\) (even though \(ED\) is not an altitude of the large triangle).

Worked example 9 — Why a median is not necessarily an altitude

Take \(D=(0,4)\), \(E=(-2,0)\), \(F=(4,0)\). The midpoint is \(M=(1,0)\), so \(DM\) is a median. But \(DM\) is slanted, while \(EF\) is horizontal: they are not perpendicular. The altitude from \(D\) is the vertical segment to \((0,0)\). The perpendicular bisector of \(EF\) is the vertical line \(x=1\).

Organize the rectangle proof (14)

In your MVP packetSection 5.3 · Set · Problems 7–14 · pp. 21–22

In the diagram, \(ADEG\) is a rectangle, with \(A,D,C\) collinear, \(B,G,A\) collinear, and \(B,E,C\) collinear. The intended additional given is that \(\overline{ED}\) bisects \(\overline{BC}\) at \(E\), so \(BE=EC\). We can then prove \(\triangle BGE\cong\triangle EDC\).

Worked example 10 — A complete two-column ASA proof for Set 14

Given: \(ADEG\) is a rectangle; \(ED\) bisects \(BC\) at \(E\); points \(A,D,C\), \(A,G,B\), and \(B,E,C\) are collinear as shown. Prove: \(\triangle BGE\cong\triangle EDC\).

StatementsReasons
1. \(ADEG\) is a rectangle, and \(ED\) bisects \(BC\) at \(E\).Given (using corrected segment \(BC\))
2. \(BE\cong EC\)Definition of segment bisector
3. \(GE\parallel AD\) and \(AG\parallel ED\)Opposite sides of a rectangle are parallel
4. \(GE\parallel DC\) and \(BG\parallel ED\)\(DC\) lies on line \(AD\), and \(BG\) lies on line \(AG\)
5. \(\angle BEG\cong\angle ECD\)Corresponding angles formed by transversal \(BC\) cutting \(GE\parallel DC\)
6. \(\angle GBE\cong\angle DEC\)Corresponding angles formed by transversal \(BC\) cutting \(BG\parallel ED\)
7. \(\triangle BGE\cong\triangle EDC\)ASA (angle–included side–angle)

Order check: \(B\leftrightarrow E\), \(G\leftrightarrow D\), and \(E\leftrightarrow C\). The side \(BE\) is between the two stated angles of the first triangle, just as \(EC\) is between the two stated angles of the second.

Packet clarification — Section 5.3, Set, problem 14Conflicting givens: ED bisects BC versus ED bisects AC

The prose and “I know” statements say \(\overline{ED}\) bisects \(\overline{BC}\), which implies \(BE\cong EC\). However, the formal “Given” line instead says it bisects \(\overline{AC}\). These are not interchangeable, and the written proof requires \(BE\cong EC\). For the intended proof, use “ED bisects BC at E.” If your teacher instructs you to use the printed \(AC\) given literally, ask for a correction; the stated ASA argument does not follow from it.

4. Go — Analyze transformations on a coordinate plane (homework 15–23)

Study the worked examples first, then try the problems yourself. An answer key at the bottom of this section is provided to check your work.

In your MVP packetSection 5.3 · Go · Problems 15–23 · p. 23

In the packet, the original triangle is \(ABC\) and the marked line through \(L\) and \(K\) is the mirror line for the first transformation. Later questions ask you to compare each image with the original. Use exact coordinate rules where a mirror line or translation has been specified.

TransformationWhat to doHow original–image segments behave
Reflect across a line (15–17)Place each image point at the same perpendicular distance on the opposite sideThe mirror line perpendicularly bisects each nonzero point-to-image segment; side lengths are preserved but orientation reverses
Translate right 10, down 8 (18–20)\((x,y)\mapsto(x+10,y-8)\)Point-to-image segments are parallel, congruent, and directed the same way; all side lengths are preserved
Translate down 10, then reflect over y-axis (21–23)\((x,y)\mapsto(-x,y-10)\)Point-to-image segments generally have different directions and lengths; corresponding triangle sides still have equal lengths

A rigid motion preserves distances and angle measures. A reflection reverses orientation; a translation does not. A translation followed by reflection is also rigid, although the segments joining a point to its image need not be parallel.

Worked example 11 — Reflection across a vertical mirror

Reflect \(P(-3,2)\), \(Q(1,5)\), and \(R(3,-1)\) across the y-axis. The rule is \((x,y)\mapsto(-x,y)\), giving \(P'(3,2)\), \(Q'(-1,5)\), \(R'(-3,-1)\). Each segment joining an original point to its image is horizontal, so it is perpendicular to the y-axis and has its midpoint on that axis. For example, the midpoint of \(P\) and \(P'\) is \((0,2)\).

Worked example 12 — Translation and slope of corresponding-point segments

Translate \(P(-3,2)\) right 10 and down 8: \(P''=(7,-6)\). The segment \(PP''\) has horizontal change \(10\), vertical change \(-8\), and slope \(\boxed{-8/10=-4/5}\). Every other original–image segment under this translation has the same slope and length \(\sqrt{10^2+(-8)^2}=\sqrt{164}=2\sqrt{41}\).

Additional fully worked Go problems

In your MVP packetSection 5.3 · Go · Problems 15–23 · p. 23
Problem A — Straightforward

Translate triangle \(U(-2,3)\), \(V(1,-1)\), \(W(4,2)\) left 4 and up 2. List the images and compare \(UV\) to \(U'V'\).

Use \((x,y)\mapsto(x-4,y+2)\): \(U'(-6,5)\), \(V'(-3,1)\), \(W'(0,4)\). Vector \(\overrightarrow{UV}=(3,-4)\) equals \(\overrightarrow{U'V'}=(3,-4)\); the two sides are parallel and each has length \(\sqrt{3^2+(-4)^2}=5\).

Problem B — More challenging, a composition

Take \(X(-4,3)\), \(Y(2,-1)\), \(Z(1,5)\). Translate down 10, then reflect across the y-axis. Find the images and explain whether \(XX'\), \(YY'\), and \(ZZ'\) must be parallel.

The combined rule is \((x,y)\mapsto(-x,y-10)\), giving \(X'(4,-7)\), \(Y'(-2,-11)\), \(Z'(-1,-5)\). The joining vectors are \((8,-10)\), \((-4,-10)\), and \((-2,-10)\). They are not scalar multiples, so the joining segments are not all parallel. Yet every side length of triangle \(XYZ\) is preserved because both stages are rigid motions.

Problem C — Compare lengths after reflection

Reflect segment with endpoints \(M(-5,1)\), \(N(-1,4)\) across \(x=2\). Determine its new endpoints and verify its length is unchanged.

For reflection in \(x=2\), use \(x'=4-x\). Thus \(M'(9,1)\) and \(N'(5,4)\). Original length: \(MN=\sqrt{4^2+3^2}=5\). Image length: \(M'N'=\sqrt{(-4)^2+3^2}=5\). Hence \(\boxed{MN=M'N'}\).
