In Section 5.4 you investigated how rigid motions preserve geometric relationships. Section 5.5 uses a tessellation—a repeating arrangement of figures—to recognize angle patterns and make conjectures. We will state the relationships clearly, explain why they make sense, and practice identifying them. Section 5.6 will develop more formal proofs.

1. What is a mathematical conjecture?

A conjecture is a statement you think is true based on patterns and known properties. Looking at several examples can suggest a conjecture, but appearance alone does not prove it for every possible figure.

The packet's tessellation is built by repeatedly rotating congruent triangles 180° around side midpoints. These rigid motions preserve lengths and angles, producing repeated straight lines and angle relationships. Instead of copying the busy tessellation, our small diagrams isolate the exact relationships you are supposed to notice.

Worked example 1 — Observation versus justification

Observation: Several pairs of opposite angles at line intersections look equal.

Conjecture: When two lines intersect, each pair of vertical angles has equal measure.

Justification to prepare: Each angle in the pair forms a linear pair with the same neighboring angle. Because both sums are 180°, the vertical angles must be equal.

2. Vertical angles

Vertical angles are the nonadjacent, opposite angles made when two lines intersect. They are congruent, meaning their measures are equal. Adjacent angles at the same intersection form linear pairs and add to 180°.

Worked example 2 — Calculate all four angles

If angle 1 measures 64°, its vertical angle 3 also measures 64°. An adjacent angle 2 forms a straight angle with 1, so it is 180° − 64° = 116°. Angle 4 is vertical to angle 2 and also measures 116°.

\[m\angle1=m\angle3=64^\circ,\qquad m\angle2=m\angle4=116^\circ.\]

Why it works: if angles 1 and 2 total 180°, and angles 2 and 3 total 180°, subtracting the shared angle 2 gives angle 1 = angle 3.

Do not confuse adjacent and vertical. Vertical angles share only a vertex, not a side. Two vertical angles are congruent; a linear pair is supplementary. Congruent adjacent angles are possible only when both are 90°.

3. Exterior angles of a triangle

Extend one side of a triangle past a vertex. The angle outside the triangle, between the extension and the other side, is an exterior angle. The two interior angles not touching that exterior angle are its remote interior angles.

Exterior Angle Theorem\[\boxed{\text{exterior angle}=\text{remote interior angle 1}+\text{remote interior angle 2}}\]

Why? The triangle's three interior angles total 180°. The exterior angle and its adjacent interior angle also total 180°. Subtract the adjacent interior angle from both totals.

Worked example 3 — Find an exterior angle

A triangle has remote interior angles of 43° and 68°. The exterior angle equals their sum:

\[43^\circ+68^\circ=\boxed{111^\circ}.\]

The adjacent interior angle is 180° − 111° = 69°. Check: 43° + 68° + 69° = 180°.

Worked example 4 — Recover missing remote angles

An exterior angle is 128°, and one remote interior angle is 52°. The other remote interior angle must be

\[128^\circ-52^\circ=\boxed{76^\circ}.\]

The adjacent interior angle is 52° because it is supplementary to 128°. Do not confuse this adjacent angle with a remote one.

4. Parallel lines cut by a transversal

A transversal is a line crossing two or more other lines at different points. When it crosses parallel lines, the eight angles occur in predictable pairs. Corresponding angles occupy matching positions at the two intersections; alternate interior angles lie between the parallel lines on opposite sides of the transversal; same-side interior angles lie between the parallels on the same side of the transversal.

Worked example 5 — Use an angle of 115°

Suppose angle 3 measures 115°. Alternate interior angle 5 is 115°. Same-side interior angle 6 is 180° − 115° = 65°. Corresponding angle 7 is 115°, and its vertical angle 5 agrees.

Always identify which relationship you are using before calculating.

Worked example 6 — Why corresponding angles agree (preparation for proofs)

A translation taking the upper parallel line to the lower parallel line preserves directions and angle measures. The angle made by the transversal and one parallel line therefore matches the angle in the corresponding position at the other crossing. This supports the conjecture; a full formal proof will be developed in the next lesson.

Parallelism matters. Vertical angles are always congruent. The corresponding/alternate-interior/same-side-interior rules above require the two crossed lines to be parallel. Never rely solely on the appearance of a drawing to assume parallelism.

5. Ready — Quadrilaterals and coordinate-line graphs (1–7)

The interior angles of a quadrilateral add to 360° (problems 1–2)

Draw one diagonal from a vertex of any simple quadrilateral to the opposite vertex, provided it lies inside the figure (for a concave quadrilateral, choose the interior diagonal). It divides the quadrilateral into two triangles. Because each triangle has 180° of interior angles, the quadrilateral has a total of 180° + 180° = 360°.

Worked example 7 — Quadrilateral angle calculation

A quadrilateral has three interior angles of 82°, 107° and 94°. Let its fourth angle be x.

\[x+82^\circ+107^\circ+94^\circ=360^\circ\]\[x=360^\circ-283^\circ=\boxed{77^\circ}.\]

In packet Ready 2, the four angles are labeled \(4x+23\), \(7x-3\), \(16x+15\), and \(6x-5\). Their sum is 360°, so \[(4x+23)+(7x-3)+(16x+15)+(6x-5)=360\] \[33x+30=360\implies\boxed{x=10}.\] The angle measures are 63°, 67°, 175° and 55°, and they total 360°.

Recognize lines from slope and intercept (problems 3–7)

In slope-intercept form \(y=mx+b\), \(m\) is the slope (rise divided by run) and \(b\) is the y-intercept. A positive slope rises as x increases; a negative slope falls. Two nonvertical lines with equal slopes are parallel. Changing only b shifts a line up or down.

Worked example 8 — Match four lines to equations

Imagine a graph with two rising lines of slope \(\tfrac34\) and two falling lines of slope \(-\tfrac34\). The rising line passing through (0,4) is \(y=\tfrac34 x+4\), while the one through (0,2) is \(y=\tfrac34 x+2\). The falling lines with those intercepts are \(y=-\tfrac34x+4\) and \(y=-\tfrac34x+2\).

For each packet equation, read its printed fraction carefully: choose a line by slope first, then by y-intercept. Pairwise intersections of two slopes and two intercepts may form a parallelogram. Look for parallel opposite edges.

6. Set — Recognize and calculate angle relationships (8–25)

Problems 8–16: search each drawing for three possible features: (a) an intersection of two straight lines producing vertical angles, (b) a triangle with one side extended creating an exterior angle, and (c) two explicitly parallel lines crossed by another line. A single complicated picture can contain all three features, but do not assume an unlabeled pair of lines is parallel just because they look parallel.

Worked example 9 — Classify without guessing

A picture shows two crossing lines. Mark the opposite pair of angles to identify vertical angles. If a triangle is also present and one side is extended, mark the outside angle at that vertex. To label a transversal angle relationship, identify two confirmed parallel lines and the line crossing both of them.

Problems 17–19: when an exterior angle and one remote interior angle are given, find the second remote interior angle by subtraction. If instead the adjacent interior angle is given, subtract it from 180° first to obtain the exterior angle.

Worked example 10 — Two-stage exterior-angle problem

The interior angle adjacent to an exterior angle is 57°, and one remote interior angle is 49°. The exterior angle is 180° − 57° = 123°. The remaining remote angle is 123° − 49° = \(\boxed{74^\circ}\).

Problems 20–25: given p ∥ q, decide whether each angle pair is congruent or supplementary. The appropriate relationship—not how the angle appears—is the justification. Start by identifying corresponding, alternate interior, same-side interior, or vertical positions; if necessary, combine relationships (such as a linear pair followed by corresponding angles).

Packet clarification — Section 5.5, Set 20–25Do not rely on visual measurement alone.

The worksheet tells students to classify pairs “by trusting how they look,” even though the mathematical reason must use the given parallel lines and the relevant angle relationships. A more reliable instruction is: “Use the given fact that p and q are parallel and known angle relationships to decide whether each pair is congruent or supplementary.”

7. Go — Complementary and supplementary angles (26–31)

Complementary angles have measures adding to 90°. Supplementary angles have measures adding to 180°. For an angle of size \(\theta\):

\[\boxed{\text{complement}=90^\circ-\theta\qquad\text{supplement}=180^\circ-\theta}\]

A nonnegative complement exists only for \(0^\circ\leq\theta\leq90^\circ\); a nonnegative supplement exists only for \(0^\circ\leq\theta\leq180^\circ\). At 90°, the complement is a zero-degree angle by the nonnegative-measure convention; some classrooms require positive angles and say “none.” Follow your class convention.

Additional fully worked Go problems

Problem A — Straightforward

Find the complement and supplement of 26°.

\[90^\circ-26^\circ=\boxed{64^\circ},\qquad180^\circ-26^\circ=\boxed{154^\circ}.\]

Check: 26°+64°=90°, and 26°+154°=180°.

Problem B — More challenging

An angle is 38° larger than its complement. Find both angles and the supplement of the original angle.

Let the original angle be \(x\). Its complement is \(90-x\). Then

\[x=(90-x)+38\implies 2x=128\implies x=64^\circ.\]

The complement is 26° and its supplement is \(180^\circ-64^\circ=\boxed{116^\circ}\).

Problem C — Determine existence

Find the complement and supplement of a 138° angle.

Because 138° is greater than 90°, it has no nonnegative complement. Its supplement is \(180^\circ-138^\circ=\boxed{42^\circ}\). A negative result from subtracting 138° from 90° is not a physical angle measure for this task.

9. Teacher's review (teachers and parents)

Set 20–25, “by trusting how they look.” The wording explicitly encourages visual guessing instead of deduction from the given parallelism. It risks reinforcing a non-rigorous approach to angle relationships. Revise it to: “Using p ∥ q, identify the relationships that show each angle pair is congruent or supplementary.”

Go 31, complement of 90°. The answer is convention-dependent if the word “angle” is intended to require positive measure. A zero-degree angle is mathematically meaningful in common geometric conventions; if zero angles are excluded, state that convention explicitly.
