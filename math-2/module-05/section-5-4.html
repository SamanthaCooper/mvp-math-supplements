In earlier lessons you used rigid motions to reason about geometric figures. Today the focus is on one subtle question: when does a line segment point in the same direction as its transformed image? The answer depends on the transformation.

1. Parallelism within a figure versus parallelism across two figures

In your MVP packetSection 5.4 · Main task · pp. 24–26

A rigid motion moves a figure without changing lengths or angle measures. A translation slides, a rotation turns, and a reflection flips. Each maps a pair of parallel lines to another pair of parallel lines. For example, if the opposite sides of a parallelogram are parallel before a rigid motion, their images are still parallel.

But the statement “segment \(AB\) is parallel to its own image \(A'B'\)” is different. It compares the original figure with the image, not two lines within the same figure. It need not hold for a rotation or reflection.

Vocabulary for reasoning. A postulate is a statement accepted as a starting point without proof. A theorem is a statement justified by reasoning from accepted facts. The packet uses rigid-motion properties as accepted foundations for investigating parallelism.

Here, “parallel or on the same line” is the most precise wording: a transformed segment may overlap its original supporting line. Some definitions reserve parallel for distinct coplanar lines.

2. Translation: corresponding segments keep their direction

In your MVP packetSection 5.4 · Main task · pp. 24–26

A translation moves every point by the same displacement. In coordinates, translating right \(p\) and up \(q\) gives \((x,y)\mapsto(x+p,y+q)\). Every segment keeps its length and direction.

Worked example 1 — Prove direction is preserved

Take \(A=(-3,1)\), \(B=(1,3)\). Translate by \((5,-2)\) to obtain \(A'=(2,-1)\), \(B'=(6,1)\). The directional change of \(AB\) is \((4,2)\). The directional change of \(A'B'\) is also \((4,2)\). Therefore their containing lines are parallel or coincide.

\[\boxed{\text{Translation: always parallel or collinear.}}\]

3. Rotation: turning can change a segment’s direction

In your MVP packetSection 5.4 · Main task · pp. 24–26

A rotation turns each segment through the same angle about a fixed center. A quarter-turn changes a horizontal segment into a vertical segment, so corresponding segments need not be parallel. A half-turn (180°) reverses every direction, which produces parallel (or collinear) segments. A full turn (360°) returns each segment to its original direction.

Worked example 2 — The half-turn argument

Rotate \(A=(-4,1)\) and \(B=(-1,3)\) through 180° about the origin. Then \(A'=(4,-1)\), \(B'=(1,-3)\). The first direction vector is \((3,2)\); the second is \((-3,-2)\). Since one vector is the negative of the other, the segments have the same line direction.

More precisely: for a nonzero segment in the plane, its rotated image is parallel or collinear with the original when the rotation angle is a multiple of 180°. Other rotation angles do not preserve the segment's direction.

\[\boxed{\text{Rotation: sometimes parallel or collinear.}}\]

4. Reflection: two special directions remain parallel

In your MVP packetSection 5.4 · Main task · pp. 24–26

A reflection flips a figure across a mirror line. Imagine reflecting across a horizontal mirror. A horizontal segment remains horizontal; a vertical segment remains vertical, although its direction reverses. A slanted segment usually changes to a different slope.

A segment in either of the following directions is parallel or collinear with its reflection: parallel to the mirror or perpendicular to the mirror. These directions are special because reflecting reverses one component of motion while leaving the other unchanged.

Worked example 3 — Two cases that remain parallel

Reflect across the x-axis. Segment \(AB\) from \((1,2)\) to \((5,2)\) is horizontal and becomes \(A'B'\) from \((1,-2)\) to \((5,-2)\), also horizontal. Segment \(CD\) from \((3,1)\) to \((3,4)\) is vertical and becomes \(C'D'\) from \((3,-1)\) to \((3,-4)\), also vertical. A diagonal segment like \((1,1)\) to \((3,3)\) changes slope from 1 to −1, so it is not parallel to its image.

\[\boxed{\text{Reflection: sometimes parallel or collinear.}}\]
Important distinction for Set 7(c): the homework highlights segments parallel to the mirror line. That statement is correct but does not list every way a segment can remain parallel to its reflected image. Perpendicular segments also retain their line direction.

5. Ready — Classify special quadrilaterals (problems 1–6)

In your MVP packetSection 5.4 · Ready · Problems 1–6 · p. 27

Classify a quadrilateral using its stated properties and markings, not how closely the drawing resembles a familiar shape.

ShapeDefining property
ParallelogramBoth pairs of opposite sides parallel
RectangleA parallelogram with four right angles
RhombusA parallelogram with four congruent sides
SquareA rectangle with four congruent sides; also a rhombus with right angles
TrapezoidIn this lesson, a quadrilateral with exactly one pair of parallel sides (exclusive definition)

A figure can have multiple correct classifications. For instance, every square is a rectangle, rhombus, and parallelogram.

Worked example 4 — Classify using markings

A quadrilateral has all four sides marked congruent and one right angle marked. It is a rhombus by equal sides. A rhombus with one right angle has four right angles, so it is a square. Thus it is also a rectangle and parallelogram.

Answer: square, rectangle, rhombus, parallelogram.

Worked example 5 — A kite is not necessarily a rhombus

If a quadrilateral has two pairs of adjacent equal sides, but the two pairs have different lengths, we cannot conclude all four sides are equal or that opposite sides are parallel. The packet's Ready problem 5 shows this kind of kite, so none of the named classifications is forced.

Packet clarification — Section 5.4, Ready, problems 1–6“List ALL” depends on the definition of trapezoid.

Some textbooks define a trapezoid as having at least one pair of parallel sides, which would include parallelograms. Others require exactly one pair; under that convention, parallelograms are not trapezoids. The packet does not explicitly choose one. This supplement uses the exclusive definition, but your teacher's convention takes precedence. With the inclusive definition, also write “trapezoid” for parallelograms, rectangles, rhombi and squares.

Ready answer-pattern guide (use the printed markings):

1. Rectangle and parallelogram (adjacent sides have different tick marks). 2. Rhombus and parallelogram (all four sides marked equal; no right angle). 3. Trapezoid (one marked parallel pair). 4. Square, rectangle, rhombus and parallelogram. 5. None of these (kite). 6. Parallelogram (two opposite parallel pairs).

6. Set — Read transformation diagrams and name parallel segments (problem 7)

In your MVP packetSection 5.4 · Set · Problem 7 · p. 27

Use notation such as \(\overline{FG}\parallel\overline{F'G'}\). Each endpoint in the pre-image must match the image with the same letter and a prime. The packet's drawings contain several crossing segments: crossings do not change which vertices correspond.

Worked example 6 — A translated triangle

Translate \(\triangle PQR\) so its image is \(\triangle P'Q'R'\). The three corresponding side pairs are

\[\overline{PQ}\parallel\overline{P'Q'},\quad\overline{QR}\parallel\overline{Q'R'},\quad\overline{RP}\parallel\overline{R'P'}.\]

If an original and image side lie on the same supporting line, say they are collinear instead of claiming two distinct parallel lines.

Worked example 7 — A half-turn of a multi-segment star

For a 180° rotation, each segment between labeled points remains parallel or collinear to the corresponding segment joining the primed points. If the diagram draws \(\overline{FH}\), \(\overline{HJ}\), and \(\overline{GJ}\), list respectively \(\overline{F'H'}\), \(\overline{H'J'}\), and \(\overline{G'J'}\) as their pairs. The fact that segments cross at other points is irrelevant.

Worked example 8 — Reflection in a slanted line

In Set 7(c), locate the drawn segments whose direction matches that of the mirror. Each such segment is parallel to its own reflected counterpart. Name each pair using its endpoints and corresponding primed endpoints. If a segment is perpendicular to the mirror, it too has a parallel or collinear reflected counterpart, even though that broader observation is not the one emphasized in the packet statement.

7. Go — Triangle congruence and matching vertices (problems 8–11)

In your MVP packetSection 5.4 · Go · Problems 8–11 · p. 28

First use the marks supplied in the drawing. Equal-length tick marks and equal-angle arcs are mathematical information, not decoration. Then write a congruence statement with matching letters in corresponding order. Only after that should you draw segments connecting corresponding vertices.

PatternWhat must be markedCaution
SSSAll three pairs of corresponding sidesDo not match vertices by picture orientation alone
SASTwo sides and the angle between themSSA is not a general triangle-congruence criterion
ASATwo angles and the side between themMatch corresponding angle vertices first
Worked example 9 — SSS correspondence, like Go 8

Suppose triangle \(ABC\) has one, two and three tick marks on sides \(AB, BC, CA\), and triangle \(DEF\) has corresponding marks on \(DE, EF, FD\). Then \(A\leftrightarrow D\), \(B\leftrightarrow E\), \(C\leftrightarrow F\), so \(\triangle ABC\cong\triangle DEF\) by SSS. The correspondence controls the order in the statement.

Worked example 10 — SAS, like Go 9 and 11

Suppose \(PQ\cong ST\), \(PR\cong SU\), and \(\angle QPR\cong\angle TSU\). The congruent angle is included between the congruent sides, so \(\triangle PQR\cong\triangle STU\) by SAS.

Worked example 11 — ASA, like Go 10

Suppose \(\angle M\cong\angle X\), \(\angle N\cong\angle Y\), and \(MN\cong XY\). The equal side lies between the two equal angles, so \(\triangle MNO\cong\triangle XYZ\) by ASA.

Connecting matching vertices: draw \(AA'\), \(BB'\), and \(CC'\). For a translation these connectors are parallel, congruent and point in the same direction; for a reflection they are perpendicular to one common mirror line and bisected by it; for a 180° rotation they pass through and are bisected by a common center. Triangle congruence alone does not force one of these connector patterns—the relative placement of the two drawn triangles matters.

Additional fully worked Go problems

In your MVP packetSection 5.4 · Go · Problems 8–11 · p. 28
Problem A — Straightforward

In triangles \(JKL\) and \(RST\), side markings show \(JK=RS=6\), \(KL=ST=8\) and \(JL=RT=10\). State a congruence in matching order and justify it.

The sides at \(J\) have lengths 6 and 10, matching the sides at \(R\), so \(J\leftrightarrow R\). Similarly \(K\leftrightarrow S\) and \(L\leftrightarrow T\). Thus

\[\boxed{\triangle JKL\cong\triangle RST\text{ by SSS.}}\]
Problem B — More challenging

Triangle \(PQR\) has \(PQ=7\), \(PR=9\), and \(\angle QPR=58^\circ\). Triangle \(XYZ\) has \(YX=7\), \(YZ=9\), and \(\angle XYZ=58^\circ\). Determine the correspondence, criterion, and the matching connector segments.

The equal included angles occur at \(P\) and \(Y\), so \(P\leftrightarrow Y\). The length-7 side pairs \(PQ\) and \(YX\), so \(Q\leftrightarrow X\); the remaining pair is \(R\leftrightarrow Z\). Therefore \(\triangle PQR\cong\triangle YXZ\) by SAS. Connect \(P\) to \(Y\), \(Q\) to \(X\), and \(R\) to \(Z\). Whether these connectors are parallel, concurrent or perpendicular to a common line depends on how the triangles are placed.

Problem C — Congruence is not a transformation description

Two triangles have three corresponding equal sides but appear rotated in a drawing. Can you conclude that one is a 180° rotation of the other? No. SSS proves triangle congruence, but a 180° rotation is a more specific placement relationship. To establish it, the three corresponding-point connectors must have one common midpoint (the proposed center).

9. Teacher's review (teachers and parents)

Section 5.4, Ready 1–6: unspecified trapezoid convention. The packet asks students to list all classes but does not say whether “trapezoid” means exactly one or at least one pair of parallel sides. This changes answers for most parallelograms, including rectangles, rhombi and squares. Specify the convention before assigning the classification questions.

Section 5.4, Go 8–11: connector question can be overgeneralized. Triangle congruence markings alone do not determine a unique motion or the pattern of segments joining corresponding vertices. The illustrated placements may suggest a transformation, but a student should justify any connector conclusion using the drawn placement or an explicitly named rigid motion, not merely SSS/SAS/ASA.

Section 5.4, Set 7(c): accurate but incomplete reflection case. A segment perpendicular to a reflection axis also has a parallel or collinear reflected image. The packet states only the parallel-to-axis sufficient condition, not an exhaustive characterization. Its claim remains true; make clear that it is a sufficient condition rather than the only case.
