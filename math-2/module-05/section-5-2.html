1. In class: A Develop Understanding Task

Revisit the ideas from class

This section explains some of the ideas you explored in class through the guided exercises, in a more formal, step-by-step way. Use it alongside the “A Develop Understanding Task” portion of Section 5.2 in your MVP packet.

What does a diagram really tell you?

In your MVP packetSection 5.2 · Main task · Problems 1–10 · pp. 7–10

A diagram can help you formulate a conjecture—a claim that appears true. But lengths and angles that merely look equal are not established facts. You can rely on a stated given, construction steps, explicitly marked congruent segments or angles, and geometric results you already know.

Build a convincing geometric argument

Identify what is given → state the claim → connect the two using definitions, established facts, and justified construction steps → explain why the conclusion follows.

Worked example 1 — Equal-looking sides

A sketch shows a triangle with two sides that appear the same length. May we immediately conclude that it is isosceles?

No. The drawing alone is not a measurement or a given. If the two sides are explicitly marked congruent, or both are radii of the same circle, then we can justify their equality. Only then may we call the triangle isosceles.

Reconstruct the story behind intersecting circles (main task, problems 1–3)

In your MVP packetSection 5.2 · Main task · Problems 1–10 · pp. 7–10

Start with points \(A\) and \(B\). Draw one circle centered at \(A\) passing through \(B\), and another centered at \(B\) passing through \(A\). Let \(C\) be an intersection of the circles.

Worked example 2 — Why is the triangle equilateral?

Because \(C\) is on the circle centered at \(A\), \(AC=AB\). Because \(C\) is on the circle centered at \(B\), \(BC=BA\). Therefore \(AC=AB=BC\). All three sides are equal, so \(\triangle ABC\) is equilateral; consequently, its three angles each measure \(60^\circ\).

Construction sequence: draw \(\overline{AB}\), draw the circles with radius \(AB\), mark their intersection \(C\), and connect \(A\), \(B\), and \(C\). Notice that the equality comes from the circle definitions—not from how the printed picture looks.

Reason about the quadrilateral formed by two circles (main task, problems 4–6)

In your MVP packetSection 5.2 · Main task · Problems 1–10 · pp. 7–10

Now consider congruent circles centered at \(A\) and \(B\), intersecting at \(C\) (above) and \(D\) (below). In the packet, \(P\) is the intersection of the diagonals \(\overline{AB}\) and \(\overline{CD}\). The packet explicitly gives that the two circles are congruent.

Worked example 3 — Establish properties of the quadrilateral

All four segments \(AC,BC,AD,BD\) are radii of two congruent circles, so \(AC=BC=AD=BD\). Hence quadrilateral \(ACBD\) has four equal sides: it is a rhombus, which is also a parallelogram. Its opposite sides are parallel.

Since \(C\) and \(D\) each have equal distance from \(A\) and \(B\), both lie on the perpendicular bisector of \(AB\). Thus \(CD\perp AB\) and the diagonals cross at the midpoint \(P\) of \(AB\). Symmetry also shows that \(P\) is the midpoint of \(CD\).

As an alternative proof of one diagonal property: triangles \(ACB\) and \(ADB\) are congruent by SSS (\(AC=AD\), \(BC=BD\), and common side \(AB\)); reflection across \(AB\) interchanges \(C\) and \(D\).

A key restriction: the quadrilateral is guaranteed to be a rhombus because the circles are congruent. Two intersecting circles of different radii can produce a kite without giving four equal sides.
Worked example 4 — Write a justification paragraph

Claim: \(ACBD\) is a rhombus.

“The circles centered at \(A\) and \(B\) are congruent, so their radii have equal length. Each of the segments \(AC\), \(AD\), \(BC\), and \(BD\) is a radius of one of the circles. Therefore all four sides of \(ACBD\) are congruent. A quadrilateral with four congruent sides is a rhombus.”

This paragraph contains the given, the relevant fact, and the conclusion in a readable order. For a second conjecture, you could justify why the diagonals are perpendicular.

Why does a perpendicular bisector create equal distances? (main task, problems 7–10)

In your MVP packetSection 5.2 · Main task · Problems 1–10 · pp. 7–10

The midpoint \(M\) of \(\overline{AB}\) satisfies \(AM=MB\). The perpendicular bisector of \(\overline{AB}\) is the line through \(M\) that forms right angles with \(AB\). Any point \(C\) on this line is called equidistant from \(A\) and \(B\) when \(CA=CB\).

Worked example 5 — Explain the perpendicular-bisector theorem step by step

Given: \(M\) is the midpoint of \(\overline{AB}\), line \(\ell\) is perpendicular to \(AB\) at \(M\), and \(C\) is any point on \(\ell\).

Goal: prove \(AC=BC\).

1. Midpoint definition gives \(AM=MB\).

2. Perpendicular lines make right angles, so \(\angle AMC\cong\angle BMC\) (both measure \(90^\circ\)).

3. Segment \(CM\) is shared by both triangles, so \(CM=CM\).

4. The triangles have side–included angle–side congruent; therefore \(\triangle AMC\cong\triangle BMC\) by SAS.

5. Corresponding sides of congruent triangles are congruent. Therefore \(AC=BC\): point \(C\) is equidistant from the endpoints.

We did not choose a special position for \(C\). The same argument works above or below \(AB\), and at \(C=M\) the equality follows immediately from \(AM=BM\).

Perpendicular-bisector distance theorem\[\boxed{C\text{ on the perpendicular bisector of }\overline{AB}\ \Longrightarrow\ CA=CB}\]

Do not reverse the direction of this statement without giving a separate justification; today the task is to establish the forward direction.

3. Set — compass constructions and angle algebra (homework problems 15–23)

In your MVP packetSection 5.2 · Set · Problems 15–23 · pp. 12–13

Construct a perpendicular bisector (15–16)

In your MVP packetSection 5.2 · Set · Problems 15–23 · pp. 12–13

With a compass, use the same opening (greater than half the segment length) to draw arcs centered at each endpoint, one arc above the segment and one below. The two pairs of arcs intersect at points equidistant from both endpoints. Connect the two intersection points with a straightedge. This is the perpendicular bisector; mark the right angle with a small square and mark the two halves of the original segment with matching tick marks.

Worked example 6 — Perpendicular bisector of a segment

Given \(\overline{PQ}\), construct line \(\ell\) by the equal-radius arc method. If \(M=\ell\cap PQ\), then \(PM=MQ\) and \(\ell\perp PQ\). These are two separate facts to mark: congruent halves and the right angle.

Construct an angle bisector (17–19)

In your MVP packetSection 5.2 · Set · Problems 15–23 · pp. 12–13

An angle bisector is a ray that divides an angle into two congruent angles. Put the compass point at the vertex and draw an arc cutting both sides at points \(P\) and \(Q\). Keeping the same compass width, draw intersecting arcs centered at \(P\) and \(Q\) inside the angle. Draw the ray from the original vertex through that intersection. Mark the two equal angles with matching arcs.

Worked example 7 — Angle bisector with algebra

Ray \(CF\) bisects \(\angle ECD\), and \(m\angle ECF=2x+10\), \(m\angle FCD=3x-18\). Find \(m\angle FCE\).

\[2x+10=3x-18\]\[x=28\]\[m\angle FCE=2(28)+10=\boxed{66^\circ}.\]

The requested angle is \(FCE\), not the whole \(ECD\); the whole angle is \(132^\circ\).

Use stated right angles to find missing measures (20–22)

In your MVP packetSection 5.2 · Set · Problems 15–23 · pp. 12–13

For a right angle split into parts, the parts add to \(90^\circ\). For a triangle, all three interior angles add to \(180^\circ\). For a right triangle, its other two angles therefore add to \(90^\circ\). Use the figure’s labels to build an equation; do not assume a right angle solely from the way a segment is drawn.

Worked example 8 — Two acute angles of a right triangle

A right triangle has acute angles \((3x+2)^\circ\) and \((2x+8)^\circ\). Find \(x\) and both angle measures.

\[(3x+2)+(2x+8)=90\]\[5x+10=90\Longrightarrow x=16.\]

Then the angles are \(50^\circ\) and \(40^\circ\). Check that \(90+50+40=180\).

4. Go — translations, reflections, and rotations (homework problems 24–28)

In your MVP packetSection 5.2 · Go · Problems 24–28 · p. 14

These transformations were introduced earlier. Today you apply coordinate rules and notice invariant properties: translations preserve slope of corresponding-point connections, reflections cross their mirror line perpendicularly, and half-turns preserve distances.

TransformationPoint ruleWhat stays true
Right 6 and down 4\((x,y)\mapsto(x+6,y-4)\)Every point moves by the same vector
Reflect across \(x=0\) (y-axis)\((x,y)\mapsto(-x,y)\)Connecting segments to images are horizontal
Rotate \(180^\circ\) about \((-2,0)\)\((x,y)\mapsto(-4-x,-y)\)Center is midpoint of original/image pair
Worked example 9 — Apply all three transformations to the packet triangle

Original points: \(C(-4,0)\), \(E(-3,3)\), \(D(0,4)\).

TransformationC imageE imageD image
Right 6, down 4 (primed)\(C'(2,-4)\)\(E'(3,-1)\)\(D'(6,0)\)
Reflect across \(x=0\) (double-primed)\(C''(4,0)\)\(E''(3,3)\)\(D''(0,4)\)
Rotate 180° about \((-2,0)\) (triple-primed)\(C′′′(0,0)\)\(E′′′(-1,-3)\)\(D′′′(-4,-4)\)

For the translation, the slopes of \(\overline{CC'}\), \(\overline{EE'}\), and \(\overline{DD'}\) are all \(\boxed{-4/6=-2/3}\). For the reflection, \(D\) lies on the mirror line, so \(D''=D\) and no distinct connecting segment is needed. The segments \(\overline{CC''}\) and \(\overline{EE''}\) are perpendicular to \(x=0\) and bisected by it.

Additional fully worked Go problems

In your MVP packetSection 5.2 · Go · Problems 24–28 · p. 14
Problem A — Straightforward translation

Translate triangle with vertices \(P(-2,1)\), \(Q(1,4)\), \(R(3,-1)\) left 3 and up 2. Determine the images and slope of \(\overline{PP'}\).

The rule is \((x,y)\mapsto(x-3,y+2)\). Thus \(P'(-5,3)\), \(Q'(-2,6)\), and \(R'(0,1)\). Every connecting segment has slope \(\Delta y/\Delta x=2/(-3)=\boxed{-2/3}\).

Problem B — More challenging half-turn

Rotate triangle \(J(1,-2)\), \(K(-4,3)\), \(L(0,5)\) through 180° about \((2,1)\). Find the image and check one midpoint.

The half-turn rule about \((h,k)\) is \((x,y)\mapsto(2h-x,2k-y)\). Here it becomes \((4-x,2-y)\). Therefore \(J'(3,4)\), \(K'(8,-1)\), and \(L'(4,-3)\).

The midpoint of \(J(1,-2)\) and \(J'(3,4)\) is \(\bigl((1+3)/2,(-2+4)/2\bigr)=(2,1)\), exactly the given rotation center.

Problem C — Reflection over a vertical line

Reflect \(T(-1,4)\) and \(U(5,-2)\) over \(x=2\), and explain the relationship of each point-to-image segment to the mirror line.

The reflected x-coordinate is \(x'=2(2)-x=4-x\); the y-coordinate stays the same. Therefore \(T'(5,4)\) and \(U'(-1,-2)\). Segments \(TT'\) and \(UU'\) are horizontal (perpendicular to the vertical line \(x=2\)), and each is bisected by the mirror line.

Packet clarification — Section 5.2, main task, problem 4The circle congruence is an essential given.

The printed diagram says the circles centered at A and B are congruent. That is why radii from both circles can be treated as equal, supporting the rhombus and perpendicular-diagonal conclusions. Mere visual similarity of the circles would not be enough.

Packet clarification — Section 5.2, main task, problems 7–10The point on the perpendicular bisector is arbitrary.

The construction shows a particular point C in the picture, but the SAS argument applies to any C on the perpendicular bisector. The special case C=M is handled directly by the midpoint property rather than by a nondegenerate triangle congruence argument.

Packet clarification — Section 5.2, Go, problem 27The reflection is a separate transformation of the original triangle.

Use the original C, E, D positions for the double-primed reflection, not the already translated C′, E′, D′ points. Similarly the triple-primed 180° rotation in problem 28 starts from the original triangle. The point D lies on the reflection line, so its image coincides with D.

5. Teacher’s review (teachers and parents)

Section 5.2, main task, problem 7, statement immediately following: The perpendicular-bisector theorem is written with a question mark despite being the claim students are to prove. Suggested correction: State it declaratively: “Every point on the perpendicular bisector of a segment is equidistant from the segment’s endpoints.”

Section 5.2, main task, problems 8–10: SAS with triangles AMC and BMC assumes C is not the midpoint M. If C=M the triangles degenerate, although the conclusion remains true. Suggested correction: State that C is on the perpendicular bisector and C≠M for the SAS step, then handle C=M directly using AM=BM.

Section 5.2, Go, problems 27–28: The notation distinguishes successive image labels but does not explicitly say each transformation is performed on the original diagram. Suggested correction: Specify “Return to the original triangle CED before each transformation” if that is the intended independent practice.
