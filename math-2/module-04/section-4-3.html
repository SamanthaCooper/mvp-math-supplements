1. In class: A Solidify Understanding Task

Revisit the ideas from class

This section explains some of the ideas you explored in class through the guided exercises, in a more formal, step-by-step way. Use it alongside the “A Solidify Understanding Task” portion of Section 4.3 in your MVP packet.

Absolute value: distance from zero

In your MVP packetSection 4.3 · Main task · Problems 1–15 · pp. 11–14

The absolute value of a number is its distance from zero on the number line. Distance cannot be negative, so \(|4|=4\) and \(|-4|=4\). The bars mean “take the nonnegative magnitude,” not “erase any minus sign anywhere in the expression.”

Worked example — evaluating the inside first

\(|2-7|=|-5|=5\), while \(-|2-7|=-5\). The negative sign outside the bars changes the sign of the answer.

A useful mathematical rule is that \(|u|=-u\) when \(u<0\), and \(|u|=u\) when \(u\ge0\). This is the foundation of today’s two-piece graphs.

\[|u|=\begin{cases}-u,&u<0,\\u,&u\ge0.\end{cases}\]

Michelle’s trip: two straight segments, one V

In your MVP packetSection 4.3 · Main task · Problems 1–15 · pp. 11–14

In the packet’s Part I graph, \(t\) is time in minutes and \(D(t)\) is Michelle’s distance from the lake in blocks. She starts six blocks away at \(t=0\), reaches the lake at \((3,0)\), then ends six blocks away at \(t=6\). The slopes are \(-2\) blocks per minute on the way toward the lake and \(+2\) blocks per minute leaving it.

The point-slope form of a line through \((t_1,D_1)\) is \(D-D_1=m(t-t_1)\). Both pieces pass through \((3,0)\):

Worked example — write both rules in point-slope form

Before the lake: \(D-0=-2(t-3)\), or \(D=-2(t-3)\), for \(0\le t<3\).

After reaching the lake: \(D-0=2(t-3)\), or \(D=2(t-3)\), for \(3\le t\le6\).

Therefore

\[D(t)=\begin{cases}-2(t-3),&0\le t<3,\\2(t-3),&3\le t\le6.\end{cases}\]

Because the left piece reverses the negative difference \(t-3\) and the right piece keeps the positive difference, the same ride can be expressed concisely as \(D(t)=2|t-3|\) for \(0\le t\le6\).

The vertex \((3,0)\) is the turning point. The graph is symmetric about \(t=3\). Equal slopes in magnitude on either side make a symmetric V. Notice that distance from the lake increases on the return; it is not the total distance Michelle has biked.

Building the parent absolute-value graph

In your MVP packetSection 4.3 · Main task · Problems 1–15 · pp. 11–14

Compare the line \(f(x)=x\) with \(g(x)=|x|\). For nonnegative inputs, they are equal. For negative inputs, the absolute-value output has the opposite sign: for example, \(f(-3)=-3\) but \(g(-3)=3\). The part of the line below the x-axis reflects above it; the other part stays in place.

\(x\)−4−3−2−101234
\(f(x)=x\)−4−3−2−101234
\(g(x)=|x|\)432101234
\[g(x)=|x|=\begin{cases}-x,&x<0,\\x,&x\ge0.\end{cases}\]

Every input has exactly one output. The left and right pieces meet at the included vertex \((0,0)\). Domain: all real numbers. Range: \([0,\infty)\).

Moving a V and rewriting it in two pieces

Horizontal and vertical shifts

In your MVP packetSection 4.3 · Main task · Problems 1–15 · pp. 11–14

Starting with \(y=|x|\), replacing \(x\) by \(x-h\) moves the vertex to the right by \(h\), and adding \(k\) moves it up by \(k\). Thus \(y=|x-1|+2\) has vertex \((1,2)\). To write its pieces, ask where the inside \(x-1\) changes sign: at \(x=1\).

Worked example — convert \(|x-1|+2\) to piecewise form (main task 10)\[|x-1|+2=\begin{cases}-(x-1)+2,&x<1,\\ (x-1)+2,&x\ge1.\end{cases}\]

On the left, this simplifies to \(-x+3\); on the right, to \(x+1\). Check the meeting point: either gives \(2\) when \(x=1\). The vertex is \((1,2)\), not \((1,0)\).

Vertical stretches and reflections

In your MVP packetSection 4.3 · Main task · Problems 1–15 · pp. 11–14

Multiplying the absolute-value expression by \(a\) changes the slopes to \(-a\) and \(a\) (in left-to-right order) when \(a>0\). For example, \(3|x+2|+1\) has vertex \((-2,1)\) and rises three units for each horizontal unit away from the vertex. A negative multiplier reflects the V across a horizontal line through its vertex, making an upside-down V.

Worked example — derive the rule in main task 14

Given \(f(x)=-3(x+2)+1\) for \(x<-2\), and \(f(x)=3(x+2)+1\) for \(x\ge-2\), the common sign-switch occurs at \(x=-2\). Thus

\[\boxed{f(x)=3|x+2|+1}.\]

Its vertex is \((-2,1)\), and its values include \(f(-3)=4\), \(f(-2)=1\), and \(f(-1)=4\).

Worked example — an upside-down V

For \(q(x)=-2|x-4|+3\), the vertex is \((4,3)\). When \(x<4\), \(|x-4|=-(x-4)\), so \(q(x)=2(x-4)+3\). When \(x\ge4\), \(q(x)=-2(x-4)+3\). Both pieces decrease in height as you move away from the vertex; the range is \(( -\infty,3]\).

Putting the ideas together

In your MVP packetSection 4.3 · Main task · Problems 1–15 · pp. 11–14

After studying the individual changes, we can summarize a linear absolute-value function as

\[y=a|x-h|+k.\]

Its vertex is \((h,k)\), its left arm has slope \(-a\), and its right arm has slope \(a\). If \(a>0\), it opens upward; if \(a<0\), downward. The piecewise form is

\[y=\begin{cases}-a(x-h)+k,&x<h,\\a(x-h)+k,&x\ge h.\end{cases}\]
Common mistakes. The boundary is \(x=h\), not \(x=k\). Simplify the sign carefully: when \(x<h\), the whole difference \(x-h\) is negative. Keep endpoint ownership consistent: \(x<h\) for one piece and \(x\ge h\) for the other.

2. Ready homework (1–6): quadratic x-intercepts

Study the worked examples first, then try the problems yourself. An answer key at the bottom of this section is provided to check your work.

In your MVP packetSection 4.3 · Ready · Problems 1–6 · p. 15

An x-intercept is where a graph crosses or touches the x-axis, so its y-value is zero. To find x-intercepts algebraically, set \(y=0\) and solve for \(x\). This is review of quadratic algebra, not a new type of absolute-value function.

Factoring when the equation is expanded

In your MVP packetSection 4.3 · Ready · Problems 1–6 · p. 15
Worked example — factor a quadratic

Find the x-intercepts of \(y=x^2+5x-14\). First set \(y=0\):

\[0=x^2+5x-14=(x+7)(x-2).\]

By the zero-product property, \(x=-7\) or \(x=2\). The intercepts are \((-7,0)\) and \((2,0)\). Substitution checks both.

Worked example — factor with a leading coefficient

Find the zeros of \(y=6x^2+x-2\). Because \(6(-2)=-12\) and \(4+(-3)=1\), split the middle term:

\[6x^2+4x-3x-2=2x(3x+2)-(3x+2)=(2x-1)(3x+2).\]

Hence \(x=\frac12\) or \(x=-\frac23\).

When the quadratic is in vertex form

In your MVP packetSection 4.3 · Ready · Problems 1–6 · p. 15
Worked example — isolate the square

For \(y=2(x-3)^2-8\), set \(y=0\):

\[2(x-3)^2=8\Rightarrow (x-3)^2=4\Rightarrow x-3=\pm2.\]

Thus \(x=1\) or \(x=5\). Do not forget both the positive and negative square roots.

Worked example — a fractional coefficient

For \(y=\frac12(x+2)^2-\frac92\), set \(y=0\). Multiply by 2: \((x+2)^2=9\). Taking square roots yields \(x+2=\pm3\), so \(x=1\) and \(x=-5\).

When there is no real intercept: \((x-2)^2+5=0\) would require a square to equal \(-5\), which is impossible for real \(x\). In that case there are no x-intercepts.

4. Go homework (11–20): negatives inside and outside bars

Study the worked examples first, then try the problems yourself. An answer key at the bottom of this section is provided to check your work.

In your MVP packetSection 4.3 · Go · Problems 11–20 · p. 16

The packet’s Go expressions combine unary minus signs and absolute-value bars. The order matters: evaluate the innermost expression first, apply absolute value bars, and then apply any negative sign outside.

Expression when \(x=-7\)WorkValue
\(-x\)\(-(-7)\)\(7\)
\(|x|\)\(|-7|\)\(7\)
\(-|x|\)\(-|-7|\)\(-7\)
\(|-x|\)\(|-(-7)|=|7|\)\(7\)
\(-|-x|\)\(-|-(-7)|=-|7|\)\(-7\)
Problem A — straightforward original Go-style problem

Evaluate \(-|x|\) when \(x=12\).

Substitute: \(-|12|\). The absolute value is \(12\), and the leading negative stays outside, so the answer is \(-12\).

Problem B — more challenging original Go-style problem

Evaluate \(-|-(-x)|\) when \(x=-9\).

Start inside: \(-x=-(-9)=9\). The next negative gives \(-(-x)=-9\). Now apply the bars: \(|-9|=9\). Finally apply the outer negative: \(-9\).

Problem C — multiple bars and grouping

Evaluate \(-|\,-|x-3|+2\,|\) when \(x=-1\).

First \(x-3=-4\), so \(|x-3|=4\). Then \(-4+2=-2\). The outer bars give \(2\). The initial negative makes the result \(-2\).

Common sign mistake. \(-|x|\) and \(|-x|\) are different expressions. The first is never positive; the second is never negative. Do not cancel a negative outside absolute-value bars with a negative inside them.
