Start with a square whose side length is made from two pieces. We will count the pieces of its area, connect them to multiplication, and then reverse that reasoning to discover the missing piece of a perfect-square trinomial. Work the examples before attempting the corresponding packet exercises.

1. In class: A Develop Understanding Task

Revisit the ideas from class

This section explains some of the ideas you explored in class through the guided exercises. Use it alongside the “A Develop Understanding Task” portion of Section 2.3 in your MVP packet.

A square built from four areas

In your MVP packetSection 2.3 · Main task · Problems 1–4 · pp. 13–14

The area of a square of side length \(x\) is \(x^2\). If each side becomes \(x+3\) long, the new area is \((x+3)^2\), not \(x^2+9\). Both dimensions change, creating two rectangular strips as well as the corner square.

Worked example 1 — Read the area model.

Add the four pieces:

\[A=x^2+3x+3x+9=x^2+6x+9.\]

The outer square has side \(x+3\), so \(A=(x+3)^2\). Thus

\[(x+3)^2=x^2+6x+9.\]

The two expressions are equivalent: they give the same area for every allowed \(x\).

Using lengths \(x+2\) and \(x+1\) gives the same four-piece arrangement. The particular numbers change, but the structure does not.

Worked example 2 — Two smaller squares.

For side \(x+2\), the four pieces have areas \(x^2,2x,2x,4\), giving

\[(x+2)^2=x^2+4x+4.\]

For side \(x+1\), they have areas \(x^2,x,x,1\), giving

\[(x+1)^2=x^2+2x+1.\]

These model the same skills as the main task's problems 3–5.

The algebraic pattern behind a perfect square

In your MVP packetSection 2.3 · Main task · Problems 1–7 · pp. 13–15

A binomial has two terms. Squaring a binomial means multiplying it by itself; it does not mean squaring each term separately. A trinomial has three terms. A perfect-square trinomial is a trinomial that equals a binomial multiplied by itself.

Worked example 3 — Multiply rather than guess.\[\begin{aligned}(x+5)^2 &=(x+5)(x+5)\\&=x^2+5x+5x+25\\&=x^2+10x+25.\end{aligned}\]

The middle term is \(10x\) because there are two products of \(5x\). The constant term is \(25=5^2\).

If the number inside the parentheses is negative, the two matching middle products are negative.

Worked example 4 — A negative number.\[\begin{aligned}(x-4)^2 &=(x-4)(x-4)\\&=x^2-4x-4x+16\\&=x^2-8x+16.\end{aligned}\]

The last term is still positive because \((-4)(-4)=16\).

General pattern, developed from the area model:\[(x+q)^2=x^2+2qx+q^2.\]

To expand: square the first term, add twice its product with the second term, then square the second term. The rule works for negative, fractional, and other real values of \(q\).

Common error. \((x+5)^2\ne x^2+25\). That incorrect expression leaves out both rectangles, whose combined area is \(10x\).

Finding the missing piece of a perfect square

In your MVP packetSection 2.3 · Main task · Problems 6–7 · pp. 14–15

Suppose you already know the \(x^2\) part and the middle term. You want a constant that makes the expression a square of \(x+q\). Since the middle term must be \(2qx\), find \(q\) by halving its coefficient. Then square \(q\) to find the constant.

Worked example 6 — Find a missing constant.

Complete \(x^2+14x+\square\).

Step 1: The middle coefficient is 14. Half of 14 is 7.

Step 2: Square 7 to get 49.

\[x^2+14x+49=(x+7)^2.\]

Check by multiplying: \((x+7)(x+7)=x^2+14x+49\).

Worked example 7 — Negative and odd coefficients.

Complete \(x^2-9x+\square\). Half of \(-9\) is \(-\frac92\); squaring gives \(\frac{81}{4}\):

\[x^2-9x+\frac{81}{4}=\left(x-\frac92\right)^2.\]

An odd middle coefficient is no problem. The missing constant can be a fraction.

Rule for the missing constant: for \(x^2+bx+c\) to equal \((x+q)^2\), the needed value is \(c=(b/2)^2\). This is the specific meaning of completing the square in today's lesson: adding the missing constant to make a perfect-square trinomial.
Today's boundary. Here you are making an expression into a perfect-square trinomial by supplying a missing term. Rewriting a general trinomial while preserving its value, especially when the coefficient of \(x^2\) differs from 1, is developed in the following lessons.

2. Ready review — Graph a line from its intercepts

Study the worked examples first, then try the problems yourself. An answer key at the bottom of this section is provided to check your work.

In your MVP packetSection 2.3 · Ready · Problems 1–6 · p. 16

The x-intercept is a point where a graph meets the horizontal x-axis. Its y-coordinate is 0. The y-intercept meets the vertical y-axis. Its x-coordinate is 0.

Intercept to findWhat to substituteHow to record it
x-interceptSet \(y=0\); solve for \(x\)\((x,0)\)
y-interceptSet \(x=0\); solve for \(y\)\((0,y)\)
Worked example 10 — Graph with intercepts.

Graph \(4x+3y=24\).

Set \(y=0\): \(4x=24\), so the x-intercept is \((6,0)\).

Set \(x=0\): \(3y=24\), so the y-intercept is \((0,8)\).

Plot the two points and draw the unique straight line through them. Substitute each into the original equation to check.

Worked example 11 — Signs matter.

Find the intercepts of \(5x-2y=10\).

\[y=0:\ 5x=10\Rightarrow(2,0).\]\[x=0:\ -2y=10\Rightarrow(0,-5).\]

A negative y-intercept goes below the x-axis.

Sometimes both intercepts are the origin \((0,0)\). Then you need a second point to draw the line: choose another convenient \(x\)-value and solve for \(y\).

Square a binomial with a multiple of x

The Set homework also squares expressions beginning with a multiple of \(x\). Here the two rectangular products are \(ax\cdot q\) rather than \(x\cdot q\):

Worked example 5 — Squaring \((3x+2)\).\[\begin{aligned}(3x+2)^2&=(3x+2)(3x+2)\\&=9x^2+6x+6x+4\\&=9x^2+12x+4.\end{aligned}\]

In general, \((ax+q)^2=a^2x^2+2aqx+q^2\). The coefficient of the middle \(x\)-term is \(B=2aq\).

Find a missing middle coefficient

You may instead know the final constant but not the middle coefficient. A perfect square ending in \(q^2\) has middle term \(2qx\) if the binomial is \(x+q\), or \(-2qx\) if it is \(x-q\).

Worked example 8 — Find a missing middle coefficient.

Complete \(x^2+Bx+64\).

Since \(64=8^2\), there are two possible perfect squares:

\[(x+8)^2=x^2+16x+64,\]\[(x-8)^2=x^2-16x+64.\]

Therefore \(B=16\) or \(B=-16\). Both work unless the question places a restriction on the sign.

Worked example 9 — Fractional last term.

Complete \(x^2+Bx+\frac9{16}\). The square root of \(\frac9{16}\) is \(\frac34\). Double it to obtain \(\frac32\).

\[B=\pm\frac32,\qquad x^2\pm\frac32x+\frac9{16}=\left(x\pm\frac34\right)^2.\]
Packet clarification — 2.3 Set, problems 18–26.

These problems ask for the value of \(B\) when the constant term is given. Each positive constant shown in these exercises allows two values, positive and negative, because \((x+q)^2\) and \((x-q)^2\) have the same final term. Unless the instructor specifies \(B>0\), report both possibilities with their corresponding factorizations.

4. Go review — Horizontal and vertical lines

Study the worked examples first, then try the problems yourself. An answer key at the bottom of this section is provided to check your work.

In your MVP packetSection 2.3 · Go · Problems 27–30 · p. 17

In an equation \(y=c\), every point has the same y-coordinate; its graph is a horizontal line. If \(c\ne0\), it has the y-intercept \((0,c)\) but no x-intercept. In \(x=c\), every point has the same x-coordinate; its graph is vertical. If \(c\ne0\), it has the x-intercept \((c,0)\) but no y-intercept.

Go Problem A — Straightforward.

Identify and classify all intercepts of \(y=7\).

All points have y-coordinate 7, so the line is horizontal and crosses the y-axis at \((0,7)\). Because \(y\) never equals 0, there is no x-intercept.

Go Problem B — More challenging.

Find and classify the intercepts of \(x=-\frac73\). Then compare with \(y=-\frac73\).

The equation \(x=-\frac73\) describes a vertical line, crossing the x-axis at \((-\frac73,0)\); it has no y-intercept. The equation \(y=-\frac73\) describes a horizontal line, crossing the y-axis at \((0,-\frac73)\); it has no x-intercept. Swapping \(x\) and \(y\) changes both line orientation and intercept type.

Go Problem C — Special case at zero.

Find the intercepts of \(x=0\) and \(y=0\).

\(x=0\) is the entire y-axis, so every point on it is a y-intercept in the geometric sense and the origin is its only x-intercept. Likewise, \(y=0\) is the entire x-axis, so every point on it lies on the x-axis and the origin is its only y-intercept. They do not have one unique intercept of the coincident-axis type. The packet's problems 27–30 avoid these zero cases.
