2.10 — I've Got a Fill-in

Supplementary instruction for Secondary Math II students using the Mathematics Vision Project (MVP) curriculum · Module 2, Section 2.10
Independently created to support students using MVP Math. Not official publisher material and not affiliated with or endorsed by the Mathematics Vision Project.

Today's goal. Choose the most useful representation of a quadratic function, move correctly among standard, vertex, and factored forms, construct tables and graphs, interpret first and second differences, and review factoring. This lesson uses the skills developed in Sections 2.1–2.9.
Contents: Reading forms · Converting forms · Graphs and tables · Ready · Set · Go · Resources · Teacher review · Coverage

A quadratic function can be described by several equivalent equations, a table, or a graph. Each representation emphasizes different information. The most useful first step is to decide what the problem is asking you to find.

1. Three forms reveal different features

Consider the same quadratic written three ways:

\[f(x)=x^2+2x-15=(x+5)(x-3)=(x+1)^2-16.\]

Expanding either of the last two expressions produces the first. They are equivalent functions: they give the same output for every real input.

FormWhat it reveals quicklyExample
Standard
\(ax^2+bx+c\)		\(y\)-intercept \((0,c)\); convenient for evaluating at a given input\(x^2+2x-15\): \((0,-15)\)
Factored
\(a(x-r)(x-s)\)		Real zeros and x-intercepts when real factors exist\((x+5)(x-3)\): x-intercepts \((-5,0),(3,0)\)
Vertex
\(a(x-h)^2+k\)		Vertex \((h,k)\), axis \(x=h\), maximum/minimum value\((x+1)^2-16\): vertex \((-1,-16)\)

The coefficient \(a\) is the same in every form; its sign gives the opening direction. A parabola opening upward has a minimum, and one opening downward has a maximum. A quadratic may have zero, one, or two real x-intercepts; in particular, not every quadratic has a factored form using real linear factors.

Worked example 1 — Match questions to forms.

Given \(f(x)=2(x-4)^2-18\), find the vertex immediately: \((4,-18)\). The axis is \(x=4\). To find zeros, solve \(2(x-4)^2-18=0\): \((x-4)^2=9\), so \(x=1,7\). Thus factored form is \(2(x-1)(x-7)\). To find the y-intercept, evaluate at zero: \(f(0)=2(16)-18=14\). The expanded standard form is \(2x^2-16x+14\).

2. Move between forms without changing the function

A. Factored to standard: multiply

Worked example 2.

\[y=-2(x-3)(x+1).\]

Multiply the binomials first: \((x-3)(x+1)=x^2-2x-3\). Then distribute \(-2\): \[\boxed{y=-2x^2+4x+6}.\]

Quick check: both expressions give \(y=6\) at \(x=0\).

B. Standard to factored: reverse multiplication

Worked example 3.

Factor \(y=x^2-2x-24\). Find two integers whose product is \(-24\) and whose sum is \(-2\): \(-6\) and \(4\). Hence \(\boxed{y=(x-6)(x+4)}\), giving zeros \(6\) and \(-4\).

C. Standard to vertex: complete the square

Worked example 4 — Leading coefficient not 1.

Start with \(y=2x^2+12x+13\). Factor 2 from the x-terms: \(2(x^2+6x)+13\). Half of 6 is 3, so add and subtract 9 inside the parentheses:

\[\begin{aligned}y&=2\bigl[(x+3)^2-9\bigr]+13\\&=2(x+3)^2-18+13\\&=\boxed{2(x+3)^2-5}.\end{aligned}\]

The vertex is \((-3,-5)\). Notice that the 9 added inside parentheses contributes \(2\cdot9=18\), so we must subtract 18—not 9—from the outside result.

D. Vertex to standard and, when possible, to factored

Worked example 5.

\[y=-(x-5)^2=-[x^2-10x+25]=\boxed{-x^2+10x-25}.\]

Factored form is \(\boxed{-(x-5)(x-5)}\): a repeated root at \(x=5\). The parabola touches, rather than crosses, the x-axis at its vertex.

A reliable conversion checklist. (1) Identify which form you have and which feature you need. (2) Use multiplication to expand, integer factoring when possible, or completing the square to recover the desired form. (3) Check that the resulting forms give the same y-intercept or expand to identical standard expressions. (4) If real linear factors are not available, do not invent them.

3. Work with graphs and tables

A quadratic's vertex is its turning point. In vertex form \(a(x-h)^2+k\), plot the vertex first. Then choose inputs equally spaced on either side of \(h\). Symmetric x-values give identical y-values. For example, with \(y=-3(x-1)^2+3\):

\(x\)−10123
\(y\)−9030−9
First differences\(+9,\ +3,\ -3,\ -9\)
Second differences\(-6,\ -6,\ -6\)

A first difference is the change between two consecutive outputs. A second difference is the difference between consecutive first differences. For a quadratic \(y=ax^2+bx+c\) sampled at equally spaced x-values of step \(d\), the second differences equal \(2ad^2\). In particular, when \(d=1\), they equal \(2a\).

Worked example 6 — Recover an equation from a table.

Suppose the outputs at x = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 are \(7,2,-1,-2,-1\). First differences are \(-5,-3,-1,1\). Second differences are \(2,2,2\), so if these data are generated by a quadratic and the input step is 1, \(a=1\). The smallest table value is \(-2\) at \(x=3\), with symmetric values at x=2 and x=4. The vertex form is \(\boxed{y=(x-3)^2-2}\). Expansion gives \(y=x^2-6x+7\). Check x=0: \(9-2=7\).

Packet clarification — Section 2.10, Set 8–9Interpreting a graph or a finite table

When the worksheet asks you to recover a quadratic from a graph or table, assume the representation is intended to describe a quadratic function. Read the grid scale carefully when estimating coordinates. Exact equations require exact graph information; a small printed graph may make some measurements approximate. A finite set of table entries, even with constant second differences, does not prove the unknown function is quadratic for all x.

4. Ready — Which form best answers a real-world question?

Suppose a ball's height in meters after t seconds is given equivalently by

\[H(t)=-4.9t^2+19.6t+58.8\] \[=-4.9(t-6)(t+2)\] \[=-4.9(t-2)^2+78.4.\]

Use the factored form to find when the ball reaches the water: the physically meaningful time is \(t=6\) seconds, not \(t=-2\), because the experiment starts at \(t=0\). Use the vertex form to read its maximum height, \(78.4\) meters, attained at \(t=2\) seconds. Use the standard form to see the cliff height at \(t=0\), which is \(58.8\) meters. To find height at an arbitrary specified time, any form works by substitution; standard form is often the most straightforward numerically.

Worked example 7 — A specified time.

At \(t=3.5\) seconds, vertex form gives \[H(3.5)=-4.9(3.5-2)^2+78.4=-4.9(2.25)+78.4=\boxed{67.375\text{ m}}.\] This substitution is equally valid in the other forms. The height is above the lake, not above the cliff.

5. Set — Assemble the full set of representations

Worked example 8 — Begin with factored form.

Given \(y=(x+4)(x-2)\), expand to standard: \(y=x^2+2x-8\). Complete the square: \(y=(x+1)^2-9\). The vertex is \((-1,-9)\); the x-intercepts are \((-4,0)\) and \((2,0)\). Use x-values \(-3,-2,-1,0,1\) around the vertex to get outputs \(-5,-8,-9,-8,-5\). First differences \(-3,-1,1,3\); second differences \(2,2,2\). A graph should be symmetric around \(x=-1\).

Worked example 9 — Begin with vertex form.

Given \(y=-2(x-3)^2+8\), expand: \(y=-2x^2+12x-10\). To factor, set up a difference of squares: \(-2[(x-3)^2-4]=-2(x-5)(x-1)\). Thus the zeros are 1 and 5. The vertex is \((3,8)\), and sample x-values 1,2,3,4,5 yield outputs \(0,6,8,6,0\). First differences \(6,2,-2,-6\); second differences \(-4,-4,-4\).

Worked example 10 — A missing real factorization.

Take \(y=x^2+2x+4=(x+1)^2+3\). The vertex is \((-1,3)\), above the x-axis, and the parabola opens upward. There are no real x-intercepts, so there is no factorization into real linear factors. Still, it has a perfectly valid standard form, vertex form, graph, and table.

6. Go review — Factoring and verifying

When you verify a factorization, multiply the proposed factors and combine like terms. For monic quadratics \(x^2+bx+c\), integer factoring seeks integers \(p,q\) with \(p+q=b\) and \(pq=c\). Special patterns include \(u^2-v^2=(u-v)(u+v)\) and \(u^2\pm2uv+v^2=(u\pm v)^2\).

New Go Problem A — Straightforward.

Verify \(x^2+9x+14=(x+7)(x+2)\). Multiply: \[(x+7)(x+2)=x^2+2x+7x+14=x^2+9x+14.\] Every coefficient matches, so the factorization is correct.

New Go Problem B — More challenging.

Factor \(z^2-4z-45\). Look for integers that multiply to −45 and add to −4. They are −9 and 5, so \(\boxed{(z-9)(z+5)}\). Verify: \(z^2+5z-9z-45=z^2-4z-45\).

New Go Problem C — A special pattern.

Factor \(16p^2-81\). This is the difference of squares \((4p)^2-9^2\), so \(\boxed{(4p-9)(4p+9)}\). The cross terms cancel when multiplied.

New Go Problem D — Not factorable over integers.

Investigate \(q^2+5q+1\). The only integer factor pair of 1 is 1 and 1 (or −1 and −1); neither gives middle coefficient 5. Thus the expression is not factorable using integer-coefficient linear factors. It does have real roots \(\frac{-5\pm\sqrt{21}}2\), so “not factorable” must always specify the intended number system.

New Go Problem E — Square-area reasoning.

The expression \(x^2+10x+25=(x+5)^2\) represents the area of a square of side \(x+5\), provided the side length is nonnegative. Compare \(x^2+10x+24=(x+4)(x+6)\), generally a rectangle rather than a square. A symbolic rectangle description also requires dimensions that are nonnegative for the x-values in the physical context.

Packet clarification — Section 2.10, Go 14–24Meaning of “will not factor” and physical side lengths

For problems 14–22, interpret “factor, if possible” as factor over the integers unless instructed otherwise. For problem 24, expressions are areas rather than literal side lengths; factors can represent rectangle side lengths when both are nonnegative on the chosen domain. Failing to factor over the integers does not prove that a real rectangle model is impossible.

Teacher's review — Material issues only

Set 8 and Set 9: The representations are provided without an explicit assumption that the relationship is quadratic. The tasks intend a quadratic model; state this assumption. An unlabeled or tightly scaled graph may also limit the precision with which a coefficient can be recovered.

Go 14–24: “Some will not factor” does not specify the coefficient number system. Several examples are irreducible over the integers but factor over the reals. Problem 24 also conflates a quadratic area expression with possible rectangle side lengths; require nonnegative lengths on a stated input domain.
