2.7 — The x Factor

In Section 2.6 you learned to express the area of a rectangle as a product or as an expanded polynomial. Today we extend that method to rectangles with dimensions smaller as well as larger than the original side length. This makes the signs in quadratic trinomials predictable and makes factoring easier.

1. Multiply binomials by thinking about rectangular areas

Begin with a square of side length \(x\). If one side stays at \(x\) and the other becomes \(x+2\), the area is \(x(x+2)=x^2+2x\). If the other side becomes \(x-2\), the area is \(x(x-2)=x^2-2x\). We require \(x>2\) for the second expression to represent an ordinary physical rectangle.

Worked example 1 — One side shortened and the other lengthened

A rectangle has dimensions \(x-2\) and \(x+3\). Write its area in two equivalent ways.

\[\begin{aligned}A&=(x-2)(x+3)\\&=x^2+3x-2x-6\\&=x^2+x-6.\end{aligned}\]

The four products are \(x\cdot x\), \(x\cdot3\), \((-2)\cdot x\), and \((-2)\cdot3\). The rectangle is physically meaningful when \(x>2\). The algebraic identity holds for every real \(x\).

In general, multiplying \((x+m)(x+n)\) gives four pieces:

\[(x+m)(x+n)=x^2+mx+nx+mn=x^2+(m+n)x+mn.\]

The coefficient of \(x\) is the sum of the side changes; the constant term is their product. This remains true for negative numbers.

Worked example 2 — Both side lengths reduced

Suppose the dimensions are \(x-3\) and \(x-4\). Then

\[\begin{aligned}(x-3)(x-4)&=x^2-4x-3x+12\\&=x^2-7x+12.\end{aligned}\]

The negative side changes add to \(-7\), but their product is positive \(12\). Physically require \(x>4\).

2. Predict the signs of a trinomial

For a monic quadratic \(x^2+bx+c\), the word monic means the coefficient of \(x^2\) is 1. If the expression comes from \((x+m)(x+n)\), then \(b=m+n\) and \(c=mn\).

Signs of \(m,n\)Sign of constant \(c=mn\)Sign of middle coefficient \(b=m+n\)
Both positivePositivePositive
Both negativePositiveNegative
Opposite signsNegativeDetermined by which magnitude is greater; possibly zero
Worked example 3 — Two opposite-sign pairs

Compare these products:

\[\begin{aligned}(x+6)(x-2)&=x^2+4x-12,\\(x+2)(x-6)&=x^2-4x-12.\end{aligned}\]

In each, \(c=-12\). The sign of \(b\) changes because the larger-magnitude side change switches from positive to negative.

Common mistake. When both changes are negative, the middle term is negative but the last term is positive: \((x-5)(x-2)=x^2-7x+10\), not \(x^2-7x-10\).

3. Equal increases and decreases: the difference of squares

Suppose one side of an \(x\)-by-\(x\) square grows by 4, and the other shrinks by 4. The rectangle's new area is not unchanged:

Worked example 4 — An increase does not cancel a decrease in area\[(x+4)(x-4)=x^2-4x+4x-16=x^2-16.\]

The two rectangular strip terms cancel, but the corner correction \(-16\) remains. For positive dimensions, \(x>4\).

Difference-of-squares identity.\[(u+v)(u-v)=u^2-v^2.\]

To factor a difference of squares, reverse this identity: \(u^2-v^2=(u-v)(u+v)\). It works because the two cross terms cancel.

Worked example 5 — Factor without a middle term\[x^2-81=x^2-9^2=(x-9)(x+9).\]

Check: \((x-9)(x+9)=x^2+9x-9x-81=x^2-81\).

Recognize also the special square patterns from earlier lessons: \(x^2+10x+25=(x+5)^2\) and \(x^2-10x+25=(x-5)^2\). These are different from a difference of two squares.

4. Reverse the multiplication: factor a trinomial

Factoring means rewriting an expanded expression as a product. For \(x^2+bx+c\), find two numbers \(m,n\) for which both conditions hold:

\[m+n=b\qquad\text{and}\qquad mn=c.\]

Then \(x^2+bx+c=(x+m)(x+n)\). In the assigned tasks, use integer pairs; check every answer by multiplying.

Worked example 6 — Positive middle, negative constant

Factor \(x^2+5x-24\). Since the constant is negative, the two numbers have opposite signs. Consider the factor pairs of 24: \(1,24\), \(2,12\), \(3,8\), \(4,6\). The pair \(8\) and \(-3\) has product \(-24\) and sum \(5\).

\[x^2+5x-24=(x+8)(x-3).\]

Verify: \(x^2-3x+8x-24=x^2+5x-24\).

Worked example 7 — Negative middle, positive constant

Factor \(x^2-13x+36\). Since the product is positive and the sum is negative, both numbers must be negative. The factor pair \(4\) and \(9\) of 36 gives the needed sum 13, so use \(-4,-9\):

\[x^2-13x+36=(x-4)(x-9).\]
Worked example 8 — A repeated factor

Factor \(x^2-12x+36\). The numbers \(-6,-6\) multiply to \(36\) and sum to \(-12\):

\[x^2-12x+36=(x-6)^2.\]

This is a perfect-square trinomial; always check for matching factors.

Worked example 9 — What if there is no integer pair?

Try \(x^2+4x+7\). Integer factor pairs of 7 are \(1,7\) and \(-1,-7\), with sums \(8\) and \(-8\), not 4. Therefore it cannot be written as \((x+m)(x+n)\) with integer \(m,n\). That is a statement about the specified factoring method, not a claim that every quadratic factors over the integers.

Rectangles and factoring are not identical statements. Algebraic factors can be negative for some \(x\), whereas physical rectangle lengths must be positive. For \((x-8)(x+3)\), require \(x>8\) when interpreting both factors as side lengths.

5. Ready review — Finding numbers between two real numbers

The density property of real numbers says that whenever \(a<b\), there is another real number strictly between them. In fact, there are infinitely many. One is the midpoint \(\frac{a+b}{2}\). By repeatedly taking midpoints, we can find three or more distinct numbers between any two values.

Worked example 10 — Between mixed numbers

Find three values strictly between \(2\frac14=2.25\) and \(2\frac34=2.75\).

Choose \(2.3\), \(2.5\), and \(2.7\). All obey \(2.25<2.3<2.5<2.7<2.75\).

Worked example 11 — Between negative numbers

Find three values between \(-3.2\) and \(-2.7\). In increasing order, use \(-3.1\), \(-3.0\), and \(-2.9\). Remember: \(-3.1\) is greater than \(-3.2\).

Worked example 12 — Between fractions with different denominators

Find three numbers between \(\frac13\) and \(\frac12\). Convert to a common denominator 60: \(\frac13=\frac{20}{60}\) and \(\frac12=\frac{30}{60}\). Choose \(\frac{21}{60},\frac{25}{60},\frac{29}{60}\); all are strictly between the endpoints.

Square roots and irrational endpoints

Square-root endpoints can be handled with careful decimal bounds or with squares. Since \(1.7^2=2.89<3\) and \(1.8^2=3.24>3\), \(1.7<\sqrt3<1.8\). Likewise \(2.2^2=4.84<5\) and \(2.3^2=5.29>5\), so \(2.2<\sqrt5<2.3\).

Worked example 13 — Between two irrational values

Find three numbers between \(\sqrt3\approx1.732\) and \(\sqrt5\approx2.236\). The choices \(1.8,2,2.2\) satisfy \(\sqrt3<1.8<2<2.2<\sqrt5\).

Worked example 14 — One endpoint is a square root

Find three numbers between 4 and \(\sqrt{23}\approx4.796\). Take \(4.2,4.4,4.6\). To justify the largest choice without relying solely on a calculator, \(4.6^2=21.16<23\), so \(4.6<\sqrt{23}\).

Worked example 15 — Roots of fractions

Between \(\sqrt{\frac14}=\frac12=0.5\) and \(\sqrt{\frac49}=\frac23\approx0.667\), pick \(0.55,0.60,0.65\). All lie strictly inside the interval.

Packet clarification — Section 2.7 Ready, problems 1–8What counts as three numbers “between” endpoints?

The endpoints themselves are excluded. Use any three distinct real numbers strictly greater than the smaller endpoint and strictly less than the larger endpoint. The answers need not share the same form as the given numbers: decimals are acceptable between fractions or square roots, provided the comparisons are justified.

6. Set practice — Recognize which factor pattern to use

The 20 Set exercises include many variations on \(x^2+bx+c\): positive or negative constants, positive or negative middle terms, repeated factors, and differences of squares. The same two-number test handles all of them.

Worked example 16 — A larger factor pair

Factor \(x^2-19x+60\). The positive factor pairs of 60 are \((1,60),(2,30),(3,20),(4,15),(5,12),(6,10)\). The pair 4 and 15 sums to 19. Since the needed sum is negative, choose both negative:

\[x^2-19x+60=(x-4)(x-15).\]

Check: \(-4-15=-19\) and \((-4)(-15)=60\).

7. Go review — Graphing parabolas and describing transformations

The Go assignment reviews material from Sections 2.1–2.2. Start with the parent parabola \(y=x^2\), whose vertex is \((0,0)\). The axis of symmetry is the vertical line through the vertex. A horizontal shift changes the vertex's x-coordinate, a vertical shift changes its y-coordinate, and a negative sign outside the square reflects the graph across the x-axis.

Graphing procedure. From \(y=a(x-h)^2+k\), mark the vertex \((h,k)\), draw the symmetry line \(x=h\), then evaluate \(x=h\pm1,h\pm2,h\pm3\) to get three accurately placed pairs of points. Connect with a smooth parabola.
Worked Go problem A — A vertical shift

Graph \(g(x)=x^2+2\). Its vertex is \((0,2)\), axis of symmetry is \(x=0\), and it opens upward. Plot seven points:

\(x\)−3−2−10123
\(g(x)\)116323611

Description: the graph of \(y=x^2\) moves up 2 units.

Worked Go problem B — A horizontal shift and reflection

Graph \(h(x)=-(x+2)^2+3\). The vertex is \((-2,3)\) because \(x+2=0\) at \(x=-2\). The axis is \(x=-2\). The negative coefficient makes the graph open downward.

\(x\)−5−4−3−2−101
\(h(x)\)−6−1232−1−6

Description: reflect \(y=x^2\) across the x-axis, shift 2 left, then shift 3 up.

Worked Go problem C — Horizontal shift only

Graph \(p(x)=(x-3)^2\). Vertex \((3,0)\), axis \(x=3\); opens up. Three pairs of points are \((2,1),(4,1)\), \((1,4),(5,4)\), and \((0,9),(6,9)\). Description: shift right by 3 units.

Common mistakes. \((x+2)^2\) shifts left, not right; a minus sign outside the parentheses reflects vertically, while a minus sign inside the square changes horizontal position. Always give three points on each side of the axis, as the packet requires.

9. Teacher / Parent Review — Source Packet

No critical mathematical error was found in the selected 2.7 task. The central sign patterns and factoring questions are consistent.

Modeling qualification — Main task problems 2–7 and 12–14. Subtracted side lengths \(x-k\) must be positive to represent physical rectangular sides. The packet does not explicitly state the relevant restrictions, so a student could mistakenly treat a negative numerical side length as a real rectangle. Suggested note: “Assume \(x\) is larger than every amount subtracted from it when using a physical area model; the algebraic identities themselves are valid for all real \(x\).”

Ready problem wording — numbers between roots/fractions. “Between” should be understood as strictly between; answers may use exact fractions or carefully justified decimals. This is an interpretation note, not a defect requiring correction.
