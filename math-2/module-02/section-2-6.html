2.6 — Factor Fixin’

Supplementary instruction for Secondary Math II students using the Mathematics Vision Project (MVP) curriculum · Module 2, Section 2.6
Independently created to support students using MVP Math. Not official publisher material and not affiliated with or endorsed by the Mathematics Vision Project.

Today's goal. Translate a rectangular area between a product of side lengths and an expanded quadratic; reverse that process by factoring trinomials of the form \(x^2+bx+c\); recognize common factors and difference-of-squares patterns; and review square roots of perfect-square expressions.

Earlier lessons used squares to understand quadratic expressions. A rectangle whose sides are different lengths gives another important pattern. For today's new factoring method, we begin with rectangles whose sides are \(x+m\) and \(x+n\), where the added lengths are integers. The order is deliberate: build the area first, then learn to recover the side lengths from the area.

1. A rectangular area has two equivalent expressions

Start with an \(x\)-by-\(x\) square. Extend one dimension by 4 units and the other by 2 units. Its new dimensions are \(x+4\) and \(x+2\), so its area is \((x+4)(x+2)\).

Worked example 1 — Build and expand a rectangle

Multiply each part of \((x+4)(x+2)\):

\[\begin{aligned}(x+4)(x+2)&=x\cdot x+x\cdot2+4\cdot x+4\cdot2\\&=x^2+2x+4x+8\\&=x^2+6x+8.\end{aligned}\]

Thus \(A(x)=(x+4)(x+2)=x^2+6x+8\). The product tells us the side lengths; the expanded expression shows the extra area beyond the original \(x^2\): \(6x+8\).

Area rule, developed from the diagram. If the side extensions are \(m\) and \(n\), then\[(x+m)(x+n)=x^2+(m+n)x+mn.\]

There are two strips with combined area \((m+n)x\) and a corner with area \(mn\). This identity works for any real \(m,n\); positive extensions are the natural context for today's rectangle models.

Worked example 2 — Different and matching extensions

If one side is unchanged and the other gains 7, then \(A=x(x+7)=x^2+7x\): there is no constant corner area. If both sides gain 5, then \(A=(x+5)^2=x^2+10x+25\); this is the perfect-square pattern from Section 2.3.

2. Reverse multiplication: find the side lengths

Factoring means expressing a polynomial as a product. Suppose a rectangle has area \(x^2+9x+20\). To find the side additions, look for two numbers that have sum 9 and product 20. The pair is 4 and 5.

Worked example 3 — Recover dimensions from an expanded area\[x^2+9x+20=(x+4)(x+5).\]

Check: \(4+5=9\) creates the middle coefficient, while \(4\cdot5=20\) creates the constant term. The rectangle's sides are \(x+4\) and \(x+5\).

Factoring procedure for \(x^2+bx+c\). Find numbers \(m,n\) such that \(m+n=b\) and \(mn=c\). Then write \((x+m)(x+n)\), and multiply to check. In today's homework, the intended numbers are integers. If none exist, say that the expression does not factor into integer binomials of this kind.

A systematic search rather than guessing

List the factor pairs of \(c\), then check their sums. For \(x^2+13x+36\), the positive factor pairs of 36 are \((1,36),(2,18),(3,12),(4,9),(6,6)\). Their sums are 37, 20, 15, 13, and 12. The successful pair is 4 and 9.

Worked example 4 — A trinomial with a negative constant

Factor \(x^2-7x-30\). Since the constant is negative, one factor must be positive and the other negative. The numbers \(-10\) and \(3\) multiply to \(-30\) and sum to \(-7\).

\[x^2-7x-30=(x-10)(x+3).\]

Check: \(x^2+3x-10x-30=x^2-7x-30\). Algebraically this is valid for all real \(x\), although the geometric side length \(x-10\) is positive only for \(x>10\).

Worked example 5 — Negative middle and positive constant

Factor \(x^2-11x+24\). Two negative numbers must multiply to positive 24 and add to negative 11: \(-3\) and \(-8\).

\[x^2-11x+24=(x-3)(x-8).\]

Why identical outside coefficients can give different middle coefficients

Notice that \(x^2+10x+24=(x+4)(x+6)\), while \(x^2+11x+24=(x+3)(x+8)\) and \(x^2+14x+24=(x+2)(x+12)\). Each pair has the same product, 24, but a different sum. That is why the \(x^2\) coefficient and constant term can stay fixed while the \(x\) coefficient changes.

Packet clarification — Section 2.6, main task problem 18What does “couldn't make the rectangle” mean?

The area \(x^2+7x+9\) cannot be factored as \((x+m)(x+n)\) with integer extensions, because no two integers add to 7 and multiply to 9. In that intended integer-extension model, the shop cannot use two whole-number additions.

This does not show that no rectangle is possible: \(x^2+7x+9\) is positive for \(x\ge0\), so one can construct a rectangle of area \(x^2+7x+9\), for example with side lengths \(1\) and \(x^2+7x+9\). Moreover, the expression has real linear factors \(\bigl(x+\frac{7+\sqrt{13}}2\bigr)\bigl(x+\frac{7-\sqrt{13}}2\bigr)\). The packet's intended restriction to integer extensions needs to be stated.

3. Ready review — Common factors and difference of squares

The Ready exercises introduce two additional ways of expressing quadratics as products. Both start with familiar multiplication rules.

Factor out a shared monomial

Worked example 6 — Greatest common factor

Factor \(15x^2-25x\). Both terms contain \(5x\), so take it out:

\[15x^2-25x=5x(3x-5).\]

Check by distributing: \(5x\cdot3x=15x^2\) and \(5x\cdot(-5)=-25x\). There is no constant term in this quadratic: \(c=0\).

Multiply conjugates to get a difference of squares

The pair \((u+v)\) and \((u-v)\) are called conjugates. Their cross-products cancel.

Worked example 7 — Conjugates\[\begin{aligned}(4x+3)(4x-3)&=16x^2-12x+12x-9\\&=16x^2-9.\end{aligned}\]

Notice \(b=0\). More generally, \((u+v)(u-v)=u^2-v^2\), called a difference of squares.

Distinguish the zero coefficients. Multiplying \(x(3x+5)\) produces no constant term, so \(c=0\). Multiplying \((3x+5)(3x-5)\) produces no middle term, so \(b=0\). These are different patterns.

4. Set review — Factoring organized by a fixed constant

For homework problems 14–25, the leading coefficient is 1. Organize your search by factor pairs. Even if the last term matches in three successive problems, you must test the required sum separately each time.

Worked example 8 — Three trinomials with a common constant

Factor each expression.

\[\begin{aligned}x^2+9x+18&=(x+3)(x+6),\\x^2+11x+18&=(x+2)(x+9),\\x^2+19x+18&=(x+1)(x+18).\end{aligned}\]

All three products have constant 18; the different pairs of side extensions make the middle coefficients 9, 11, and 19.

Worked example 9 — Recognize a perfect square among the trinomials

Factor \(x^2+16x+64\). Since \(8+8=16\) and \(8\cdot8=64\), the factors are equal:

\[x^2+16x+64=(x+8)^2.\]

Not every trinomial with a square constant is itself a perfect-square trinomial. The middle coefficient must be exactly twice the square root of the constant (with the appropriate sign).

5. Go review — Square roots of perfect squares

The principal square root \(\sqrt{N}\) means the nonnegative number whose square is \(N\). Consequently \(\sqrt{U^2}=|U|\), not always \(U\). Absolute-value bars are necessary whenever \(U\) could be negative.

Worked Go problem A — Straightforward

Evaluate \(\sqrt{(5x+2)^2}\).

\[\sqrt{(5x+2)^2}=|5x+2|.\]

For instance, if \(x=-1\), the inside expression is \((-3)^2=9\), whose principal square root is 3; \(|5(-1)+2|=3\), while \(5(-1)+2=-3\) is incorrect.

Worked Go problem B — Perfect-square trinomial

Simplify \(\sqrt{x^2-18x+81}\).

Half of \(-18\) is \(-9\), and \((-9)^2=81\), so the radicand is \((x-9)^2\).

\[\sqrt{x^2-18x+81}=\sqrt{(x-9)^2}=|x-9|.\]

Check: the square of \(|x-9|\) returns \(x^2-18x+81\).

Worked Go problem C — Square monomial with variables

Simplify \(\sqrt{144m^2n^6}\).

\[144m^2n^6=(12mn^3)^2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\sqrt{144m^2n^6}=|12mn^3|=12|m|\,|n|^3.\]

Do not silently assume variables are nonnegative. If the homework or teacher explicitly assumes positive variable values, the absolute values may then be omitted.

Worked Go problem D — Not a perfect-square trinomial

Decide whether \(\sqrt{x^2+12x+32}\) is the square root of a perfect-square trinomial.

A square with middle term \(12x\) would require constant \((12/2)^2=36\), but the constant here is 32. In the packet's instructions, leave the entire expression under the radical sign: \(\sqrt{x^2+12x+32}\). Do not force a simplification.

Packet clarification — Section 2.6 Go, problems 27–35Checking that a radicand is a perfect square is not the whole job

The instruction to “take the square root” requires the principal square root. In particular, problem 28 gives \(\sqrt{(3x-7)^2}=|3x-7|\), and the complete-square trinomials in problems 30, 31, 34, and 35 also need absolute-value signs unless a sign assumption about \(x\) is supplied. Problem 27 has an entire squared expression; problem 29 has \(121a^2b^6\), which equals \((11ab^3)^2\). For the other radicands, follow the packet's instruction to leave non-perfect-square expressions unsimplified.

Teacher / parent review — Material issues only

Main task, problem 18. The question asks whether an area \(x^2+7x+9\) can form a rectangle; the intended answer appears to require extensions by whole-number amounts but that assumption is unstated. Without it the blanket claim “cannot make the rectangle” is false. Clarify: “Can it be made by adding nonnegative integer amounts to the two sides of the original \(x\)-by-\(x\) square?”

Go, problems 27–35. A correct principal-square-root answer sometimes requires absolute values. Model \(\sqrt{u^2}=|u|\), or explicitly specify nonnegative expressions if that is the convention desired. The latter should not be taken for granted.
