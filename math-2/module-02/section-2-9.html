2.9 — Lining Up Quadratics

Supplementary instruction for Secondary Math II students using the Mathematics Vision Project (MVP) curriculum · Module 2, Section 2.9
Independently created to support students using MVP Math. Not official publisher material and not affiliated with or endorsed by the Mathematics Vision Project.

Today's goal. Read a quadratic in factored form to locate its x-intercepts, vertex, axis of symmetry, y-intercept, and vertical scale; explain how a product of two lines creates a parabola; review binomial multiplication; and interpret the same features from vertex form.
Navigate: Factored form Products of lines Ready Set Go Resources Teacher review Coverage

You already know how to factor trinomials, graph parabolas, and read vertex form. Today's lesson connects these skills. Study each example, and reproduce the key graph points yourself before referring to the pictures.

1. Read the important points from factored form

A quadratic expressed as a product of two linear factors often looks like \(y=a(x-r)(x-s)\), where \(a\ne0\). The zero-product property says that a product is zero if at least one factor is zero. Because an x-intercept lies where \(y=0\), the zeros are \(x=r\) and \(x=s\). Write intercepts as points: \((r,0)\) and \((s,0)\).

How to find features from factored form. (1) Set each factor equal to zero for the x-intercepts. (2) Average the two intercept x-coordinates to get the axis of symmetry: \(x=(r+s)/2\). (3) Substitute that x-value into the original function to get the vertex y-coordinate. (4) Substitute \(x=0\) to get the y-intercept. (5) The factor \(a\) gives the vertical scale and determines whether the parabola opens up (positive) or down (negative).
Worked example 1 — Find every feature.

Analyze \(y=(x-1)(x+3)\).

Zeros: \(x-1=0\Rightarrow x=1\); \(x+3=0\Rightarrow x=-3\). So the x-intercepts are \((1,0)\) and \((-3,0)\).

Axis: \(x=(1+(-3))/2=-1\).

Vertex: substitute \(x=-1\): \(y=(-2)(2)=-4\), hence \((-1,-4)\), a minimum.

y-intercept: when \(x=0\), \(y=(-1)(3)=-3\), hence \((0,-3)\).

Scale: \(a=1\); opens upward, neither vertically stretched nor compressed relative to \(y=x^2\).

Worked example 2 — A non-unit multiplier.

Analyze \(y=2(x-2)(x-6)\).

The zeros are 2 and 6, so the axis is \(x=(2+6)/2=4\). At \(x=4\), \(y=2(2)(-2)=-8\), giving vertex \((4,-8)\). The y-intercept is \((0,2(-2)(-6))=(0,24)\). The parabola opens upward and its outputs are scaled by 2 relative to \((x-2)(x-6)\).

For \(y=-x(x+4)\), the x-intercepts are \((0,0)\) and \((-4,0)\); the axis is \(x=-2\); the vertex is \((-2,4)\) and is a maximum. Notice that the origin is both an x-intercept and the y-intercept. If both roots are the same, the axis passes through that single touching x-intercept.

Why the midpoint rule works

The graph is symmetric, so two points with the same y-value lie equally far to the left and right of the symmetry axis. Both roots have y-value zero. Thus the center must lie halfway between their x-coordinates. This is the same symmetry you saw earlier when graphing parabolas from tables.

2. A parabola from the product of two lines

Suppose two lines are \(L_1(x)=x+1\) and \(L_2(x)=x-3\). Multiplying their output values produces \(P(x)=L_1(x)L_2(x)=(x+1)(x-3)\), which is a quadratic function. Its graph is a parabola, not a straight line.

At \(x=-1\), the first line has output 0, so the product must be 0. At \(x=3\), the second line has output 0, so the product is again 0. The product has precisely the x-intercepts of the two factors (unless the factors share the same root).

At \(x=0\), the two lines have y-intercepts 1 and −3. The product's y-intercept is their product, \(1\cdot(-3)=-3\).

General product rule. For \(L_1(x)=m_1(x-r)\) and \(L_2(x)=m_2(x-s)\), their product is \(P(x)=m_1m_2(x-r)(x-s)\). The quadratic multiplier is \(a=m_1m_2\). The product's y-intercept is the product of the individual lines' y-intercepts.
Worked example 3 — Different linear slopes.

Let \(L_1(x)=2(x-1)\) and \(L_2(x)=-3(x+2)\). Then \(P(x)=-6(x-1)(x+2)\).

It crosses at \((1,0)\) and \((-2,0)\); the axis is \(x=-\tfrac12\). At \(x=-\tfrac12\), \(P(x)=-6(-\tfrac32)(\tfrac32)=\tfrac{27}{2}\), a maximum. At zero, the lines have y-intercepts −2 and −6, so the product's y-intercept is \((0,12)\). This matches substitution into the quadratic.

Working backward from a parabola: if its x-intercepts are \(r,s\), factors could be \(m(x-r)\) and \(\frac{a}{m}(x-s)\) for any nonzero \(m\). Their product is the same \(a(x-r)(x-s)\). Thus the individual factor lines are not unique, even when the parabola is fully specified.

Worked example 4 — Recover factors from graph features.

Imagine a parabola crossing at \((-2,0)\) and \((4,0)\) with y-intercept \((0,-16)\). Begin with \(y=a(x+2)(x-4)\). At \(x=0\), \(-16=a(2)(-4)=-8a\), so \(a=2\). Therefore \(y=2(x+2)(x-4)\). One pair of factor lines is \(L_1(x)=2(x+2)\), \(L_2(x)=x-4\); another is \(L_1(x)=x+2\), \(L_2(x)=2(x-4)\).

Packet clarification — Main task, problems 9–13Choosing factor lines from a graph

The sketch indicates the intended zeros and general shape, but precise algebraic equations require readable coordinates and a scale point such as the y-intercept. Use the plotted x-intercepts to create \((x-r)(x-s)\); use a further point to determine \(a\). Multiple pairs of lines can have exactly the same product, but if all graph coordinates are specified exactly, the resulting quadratic function is unique.

3. Ready review — Multiply binomials using a two-way table

To multiply two binomials, multiply every term in the first factor by every term in the second. A four-cell table keeps those products organized.

Worked example 5 — A multiplication table.

Expand \((3x-2)(2x+5)\).

\(\times\)\(2x\)\(+5\)
\(3x\)\(6x^2\)\(15x\)
\(-2\)\(-4x\)\(-10\)

Combine the four cells: \(6x^2+15x-4x-10=\boxed{6x^2+11x-10}\).

Two especially useful patterns come from products of matching or opposite binomials:

\[(u+v)^2=u^2+2uv+v^2\] \[(u-v)^2=u^2-2uv+v^2\] \[(u+v)(u-v)=u^2-v^2\]
Worked example 6 — Square a binomial.

Expand \((4x-3)^2\). The first square is \(16x^2\); the two cross-products total \(-24x\); the last square is 9. Thus \(\boxed{16x^2-24x+9}\).

If we write the binomial as \((px+q)^2\), the coefficient of x in the expanded expression is \(2pq\), not \(p+q\). This is the pattern sought in Ready problem 10.

4. Set practice — Factored-form features

Now apply the procedure to different signs and repeated factors. Be careful: the number written beside \(x\) inside a factor has the opposite sign from the zero.

Worked example 7 — Negative multiplier and repeated zero.

For \(y=-\tfrac12(x-3)^2\): the only x-intercept is \((3,0)\); the axis is \(x=3\); the vertex is \((3,0)\), a maximum; the y-intercept is \((0,-\tfrac92)\); and \(a=-\tfrac12\) reflects downward and compresses the outputs by one half. A repeated zero touches the x-axis instead of crossing it.

Worked example 8 — Fractional midpoint.

For \(y=\tfrac13(x+1)(x-4)\), the zeros are −1 and 4, so the symmetry axis is \(x=\tfrac32\). Substituting gives \(y=\tfrac13(\tfrac52)(-\tfrac52)=-\tfrac{25}{12}\). The vertex is \((\tfrac32,-\tfrac{25}{12})\); the y-intercept is \((0,-\tfrac43)\). A fractional vertex coordinate is perfectly valid.

5. Go review — Recover features from vertex form

In earlier sections you learned \(y=a(x-h)^2+k\). The vertex is \((h,k)\), and the axis is \(x=h\). The multiplier \(a\) still tells whether the parabola opens upward or downward and how steeply it changes. For the y-intercept, set \(x=0\). For the x-intercepts, set \(y=0\) and solve:

\[0=a(x-h)^2+k\quad\Rightarrow\quad(x-h)^2=-\frac{k}{a}.\]

If \(-k/a\) is positive, there are two distinct real x-intercepts; if zero, one touching intercept; if negative, none. When positive, \(x=h\pm\sqrt{-k/a}\). The square root step is necessary unless the value is an obvious perfect square.

New Go Problem A — Straightforward.

Find all features of \(y=(x+1)^2-9\).

Vertex: \((-1,-9)\). Axis: \(x=-1\). Scale: \(a=1\), opens upward. y-intercept: \(y=(0+1)^2-9=-8\), or \((0,-8)\). x-intercepts: \((x+1)^2=9\), so \(x+1=\pm3\), giving \((-4,0)\) and \((2,0)\).

New Go Problem B — More challenging.

Find all features of \(y=-2(x-1)^2+8\).

Vertex: \((1,8)\), a maximum. Axis: \(x=1\). Scale: \(a=-2\), downward reflection and vertical stretch by 2. y-intercept: \(-2(1)+8=6\), or \((0,6)\). x-intercepts: \(0=-2(x-1)^2+8\) means \((x-1)^2=4\), so \(x-1=\pm2\), hence \((-1,0)\) and \((3,0)\).

New Go Problem C — No real x-intercepts.

For \(y=3(x+2)^2+12\), the vertex is \((-2,12)\), the axis is \(x=-2\), and the scale is 3 upward. The y-intercept is \((0,24)\). To find x-intercepts, \(0=3(x+2)^2+12\) implies \((x+2)^2=-4\), impossible for real x. Therefore there are no real x-intercepts.

Proving two forms are equal

A factored expression and a vertex-form expression describe the same function when expansion makes them identical. For example,

\[4(x-2)(x+6)=4(x^2+4x-12)=4x^2+16x-48.\]

\[4(x+2)^2-64=4(x^2+4x+4)-64=4x^2+16x-48.\]

Both forms produce exactly the same quadratic. This two-sided expansion strategy is useful for Go problem 26; it does not depend on testing a few x-values.

Packet clarification — Section 2.9, Go problem 26How to prove equivalent expressions

The pairs in this exercise are meant to be the same functions, not merely graphs that happen to have matching vertices or intercepts. Expand each expression independently and compare the resulting terms, or perform valid algebra to transform one expression into the other.
