2.4 — A Square Deal

1. Review: One square, then several squares

In Section 2.3, a square whose side is \(x+q\) has area \((x+q)^2=x^2+2qx+q^2\). The four regions have areas \(x^2\), \(qx\), \(qx\), and \(q^2\). A perfect-square trinomial is an expression with exactly this structure.

Worked example 1 — A single enlarged square.

Take a square with side \(x+4\). Divide each side into a segment of length \(x\) and a segment of length 4. The regions contribute \(x^2\), \(4x\), \(4x\), and 16 square units.

\[(x+4)^2=x^2+8x+16.\]

Expanding confirms the geometry: \((x+4)(x+4)=x^2+4x+4x+16\).

Now suppose there are three separate identical squares. Their total area is three times the area of one square. This is why the factor 3 multiplies all parts of the expansion.

Worked example 2 — Three copies of a square.

Each square has side length \(x+2\), so its area is \((x+2)^2=x^2+4x+4\).

\[3(x+2)^2=3(x^2+4x+4)=3x^2+12x+12.\]

The coefficient 3 means three copies; it does not mean the side of each square becomes \(3(x+2)\). Three squares have total area \(3(x+2)^2\), whereas one square of side \(3(x+2)\) has area \(9(x+2)^2\).

Scaling a square: For \(a\) identical squares, each of side \(x+q\), their total area is \(a(x+q)^2=ax^2+2aqx+aq^2\). In a literal area model, \(a\) is a positive whole-number count; algebraically, the identity holds for any real \(a\).

2. Recognizing a multiple of a perfect square

Consider \(2x^2+12x+18\). The leading coefficient 2 tells us to look for two copies of one perfect square. Factor 2 out of every term:

\[2x^2+12x+18=2(x^2+6x+9)=2(x+3)^2.\]

The interior square has side \(x+3\), because twice 3 is 6 and \(3^2=9\). The total area is the area of two such squares.

Worked example 3 — Find the missing constant.

Complete \(4x^2+24x+\square\) so it represents four copies of a perfect square.

Step 1. Factor 4 from the terms containing \(x\): \(4(x^2+6x+\square)\).

Step 2. Half of 6 is 3; square it to get 9 inside the parentheses.

Step 3. Multiply by the outside 4. The missing constant in the original expression is \(4\cdot9=36\).

\[4x^2+24x+36=4(x+3)^2.\]

Each square is \((x+3)\) by \((x+3)\); there are four squares.

Worked example 4 — A negative middle term.

Complete \(3x^2-30x+\square\).

\[3(x^2-10x+\square),\qquad \left(\frac{-10}{2}\right)^2=25.\]\[3x^2-30x+75=3(x-5)^2.\]

The sign of the interior binomial follows the middle term, even though the missing constant is positive. For a physical square, require \(x-5\geq0\).

3. When the original trinomial is not a perfect square

Sometimes the required constant does not match the constant already present. We can still build a perfect square and then subtract or add a correction to keep the expression unchanged. This process is called completing the square.

Worked example 5 — An expression that needs an adjustment.

Rewrite \(2x^2+12x+13\) without changing its value.

1. Factor the coefficient from the variable terms: \(2(x^2+6x)+13\).

2. Inside the parentheses, half of 6 is 3, and \(3^2=9\).

3. Add 9 inside, but also subtract its full value \(2\cdot9=18\) outside:

\[2(x^2+6x+9)+13-18.\]

4. Replace the perfect square and combine constants:

\[\boxed{2x^2+12x+13=2(x+3)^2-5.}\]

Check: \(2(x^2+6x+9)-5=2x^2+12x+18-5\), exactly the original expression.

The quantity \(2(x+3)^2\) is the matching completed-square expression; the original is 5 less. “Closest perfect square” in the packet refers to matching the \(x^2\) and \(x\) coefficients, not to the physically closest shape or the smallest numerical distance between functions.

Worked example 6 — Original constant is too large.

Rewrite \(x^2-8x+21\).

The perfect-square part is \(x^2-8x+16=(x-4)^2\). Since 21 is 5 greater than 16:

\[x^2-8x+21=(x-4)^2+5.\]
Worked example 7 — Fractional halfway number.

Rewrite \(2x^2+6x-1\). Factor 2 out of the variable terms:

\[2(x^2+3x)-1.\]

Half of 3 is \(\frac32\); its square is \(\frac94\). Multiplying by 2 adds \(\frac92\), which we must subtract:

\[2\left(x^2+3x+\frac94\right)-1-\frac92\]\[\boxed{2\left(x+\frac32\right)^2-\frac{11}{2}.}\]

This is valid even if the midway number or the final correction is not an integer.

General procedure (after the examples). For \(ax^2+bx+c\), with \(a\ne0\):
  1. Factor \(a\) out of the \(x^2\) and \(x\) terms only.
  2. Inside parentheses, take half the new coefficient of \(x\), then square it.
  3. Add that square inside the parentheses and subtract \(a\) times that same square outside, preserving equality.
  4. Rewrite the trinomial as a binomial squared, then combine the remaining constants.
In compact form, \[ax^2+bx+c=a\left(x+\frac{b}{2a}\right)^2+c-\frac{b^2}{4a}.\] The examples above explain why this expression is equivalent; the formula is a summary, not a separate procedure to memorize.
Common mistake. \(2(x^2+4x+4)\) adds 8 compared with \(2(x^2+4x)\)—not 4. If you insert 4 inside a factor of 2, subtract 8 outside to keep the total unchanged.

Using the completed square to understand a graph

From Section 2.2, \(y=a(x-h)^2+k\) has vertex \((h,k)\). So \(2(x+3)^2-5\) has vertex \((-3,-5)\), opens upward, and is vertically stretched by 2. The new algebra does not change the graph; it reveals features already present in the original expression.

Worked example 8 — Rewrite and verify the vertex.

For \(y=3x^2-18x+20\), factor and complete the square:

\[y=3(x^2-6x)+20=3(x^2-6x+9)+20-27\]\[\boxed{y=3(x-3)^2-7.}\]

The vertex is \((3,-7)\). At \(x=3\), the original gives \(27-54+20=-7\), which verifies the minimum.

4. Ready — Matching a parabola to its equation

Ready 1–6 asks for each graph’s y-intercept and then a match to one of six quadratic equations. A y-intercept is where the graph crosses the vertical axis, so \(x=0\). In \(f(x)=ax^2+bx+c\), this gives \(f(0)=c\): the y-intercept is \((0,c)\).

When matching, check the y-intercept and opening direction (positive \(a\) opens upward; negative \(a\) opens downward). Finally use the vertex or another point if needed. A graph crossing at \((0,-5)\) must correspond to an equation with constant term \(-5\), but that alone would not identify it if multiple choices had the same constant.

Worked example 9 — Match using key features.

Compare \(f(x)=x^2-4x+4\) and \(g(x)=-x^2+2x+4\). Both cross the y-axis at \((0,4)\), but \(f\) opens up and \(g\) opens down. Also \(f(x)=(x-2)^2\) has vertex \((2,0)\), whereas \(g(x)=-(x-1)^2+5\) has vertex \((1,5)\). These checks distinguish the graphs decisively.

Packet clarification — Section 2.4, Ready 1–6.

The printed graphs are small. Treat the grid marks as units and use the equations as an independent check. Read the point where each curve meets the y-axis (the line \(x=0\)), not the parabola’s lowest or highest point, which may be elsewhere.

5. Set — Five copies, scrambled tiles, and deciding if a square is complete

Set 7–9: The packet says each expression represents five perfect squares. Write \(5(x+q)^2\), expand to \(5x^2+10qx+5q^2\), and match the middle term. The constant is \(5q^2\), not merely \(q^2\). Each of the five square pieces has side \(x+q\).

Worked example 10 — Five squares.

Complete \(5x^2-40x+\square\).

Match \(10q=-40\), so \(q=-4\). The required constant is \(5(-4)^2=80\).

\[\boxed{5x^2-40x+80=5(x-4)^2.}\]

There are five identical \((x-4)\)-by-\((x-4)\) squares (when \(x\ge4\)).

Set 10: Count how many \(x^2\)-tiles, \(x\)-rectangles, and unit squares appear in the drawing. Combine their areas to make the expanded expression; group them into whole enlarged squares where possible for the equivalent squared expression. A tile diagram of disjoint pieces need not already be arranged as squares.

Set 11–13: Find the unique scaled perfect square that has the same quadratic and linear terms as the original; compare constants to determine the adjustment. For example:

Worked example 11 — Not initially a perfect square.

For \(3x^2+12x-7\), half of \(12/3=4\) is 2. The matching scaled square is \(3(x+2)^2=3x^2+12x+12\). The desired constant is \(-7\), which is 19 below 12:

\[\boxed{3x^2+12x-7=3(x+2)^2-19.}\]

So the original expression is not itself a scaled perfect square; it is the completed square minus 19.

Packet clarification — Section 2.4, task 8 and Set 11–13.

“Closest perfect square” is not a distance measurement. For these problems, it means the expression of the form \(a(x+q)^2\) that matches the given \(x^2\) and \(x\) coefficients, before adding the constant correction. Even for a negative or noninteger correction, the algebra is valid.

6. Go — Evaluate quadratic functions

To evaluate \(f(t)\), substitute \(t\) for every occurrence of \(x\) in the function rule, then compute exponents before multiplication and addition. For a negative input, always use parentheses around the substituted value.

Go Problem A — Straightforward.

Given \(f(x)=2x^2-5x+1\), calculate \(f(3)\).

\[f(3)=2(3)^2-5(3)+1=18-15+1=\boxed4.\]

We calculate the square first, then multiply. \(f(3)\) means an output, not \(f\) multiplied by 3.

Go Problem B — Negative input.

Given \(g(x)=-3x^2+2x-4\), calculate \(g(-2)\).

\[g(-2)=-3(-2)^2+2(-2)-4=-3(4)-4-4=\boxed{-20}.\]

Important: \(-3(-2)^2=-12\), because the square applies to \((-2)\), not to the coefficient \(-3\).

Go Problem C — Combine two function values.

For \(f(x)=x^2-4x+3\) and \(g(x)=2x^2+x-6\), find \(f(0)+g(-1)\).

\[f(0)=0-0+3=3.\]\[g(-1)=2(-1)^2+(-1)-6=2-1-6=-5.\]\[\boxed{f(0)+g(-1)=3+(-5)=-2.}\]

Evaluate each function independently before adding the outputs.

Go Problem D — Fractions and decimals.

If \(h(x)=4x^2-3x+2\), find \(h(\tfrac12)\).

\[h\left(\frac12\right)=4\left(\frac12\right)^2-3\left(\frac12\right)+2=1-\frac32+2=\boxed{\frac32}.\]

Teacher / parent review of the source packet

Section 2.4, Task 8; Set 11–13. The phrase “closest perfect square” lacks a defined metric and can suggest several unrelated interpretations. A precise version is: Find the scaled binomial square with the same coefficients of \(x^2\) and \(x\), and then give the additive constant required to recover the original expression. This interpretation also permits negative constants and avoids treating a symbolic expression as a literal positive area.

Section 2.4, Task 5 and Set 7–9. The wording refers to multiple physical squares, but a binomial side such as \(x-5\) only represents a physical length when nonnegative. If using the quilts as literal geometry, give the domain restriction. The polynomial identities themselves are valid for every real \(x\).
