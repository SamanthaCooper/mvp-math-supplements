Teacher notes

You have already learned that a perfect square such as \((x+4)^2\) expands to \(x^2+8x+16\), and that the form \(y=a(x-h)^2+k\) makes a parabola's vertex easy to see. Today you will connect these ideas in the reverse direction: starting with an expanded expression and rewriting it without changing its value.

1. In class: A Practice Understanding Task

Revisit the ideas from class

This section explains some of the ideas you explored in class through the guided exercises, in a more formal, step-by-step way. Use it alongside the “A Practice Understanding Task” portion of Section 2.5 in your MVP packet.

From expanded form to a perfect square

In your MVP packetSection 2.5 · Main task · Problems 1–8 · pp. 24–27

Consider \(y=x^2-6x+9\). The middle coefficient is \(-6\), so half is \(-3\). Squaring that number gives \(9\), exactly the constant term already present. Therefore

\[x^2-6x+9=(x-3)^2.\]

Both expressions give the same output for every input. The right-hand form shows that the lowest point is \((3,0)\).

Perfect-square pattern. \((x+p)^2=x^2+2px+p^2\). To find the constant that completes \(x^2+bx+\square\), calculate \((b/2)^2\). This works even when \(b\) is odd, negative, or fractional.

What if the constant does not complete the square?

In your MVP packetSection 2.5 · Main task · Problems 1–8 · pp. 24–27

For \(x^2+4x+2\), half of \(4\) is \(2\), so the square needs \(4\), not \(2\). Add and subtract the same quantity to keep the expression unchanged:

Worked example 1 — Preserve equality \[\begin{aligned}x^2+4x+2 &=x^2+4x+4-4+2\\&=(x+2)^2-2.\end{aligned}\]

Check by expanding: \((x+2)^2-2=x^2+4x+4-2=x^2+4x+2\). Its vertex is \((-2,-2)\), and it opens upward.

Common mistake: Writing \(x^2+4x+2=(x+2)^2\) changes the function, because the right side has a constant of 4 instead of 2. Always compensate for any number you introduce.

Why does completing the square work visually?

In your MVP packetSection 2.5 · Main task · Problems 1–8 · pp. 24–27

The pieces \(x^2+6x\) can be pictured as an \(x\)-by-\(x\) square plus two strips, each \(x\)-by-3. They leave a missing 3-by-3 corner. Adding its area, \(9\), completes an \((x+3)\)-by-\((x+3)\) square.

When the coefficient of x is odd

In your MVP packetSection 2.5 · Main task · Problems 1–8 · pp. 24–27
Worked example 2 — An odd middle coefficient

Rewrite \(f(x)=x^2+7x+10\) in vertex form.

Half of 7 is \(7/2\), and \((7/2)^2=49/4\).

\[\begin{aligned}f(x)&=x^2+7x+\frac{49}{4}-\frac{49}{4}+10\\&=\left(x+\frac72\right)^2-\frac{49}{4}+\frac{40}{4}\\&=\left(x+\frac72\right)^2-\frac94.\end{aligned}\]

Vertex: \((-7/2,-9/4)\). Do not round the fractions; exact numbers are useful for identifying the vertex and checking work.

When the coefficient of x² is not 1

In your MVP packetSection 2.5 · Main task · Problems 1–8 · pp. 24–27
Worked example 3 — Factor the leading coefficient first

Convert \(y=2x^2-12x+17\).

\[\begin{aligned}y&=2(x^2-6x)+17\\&=2\big[(x^2-6x+9)-9\big]+17\\&=2(x-3)^2-18+17\\&=2(x-3)^2-1.\end{aligned}\]

Vertex: \((3,-1)\). The graph opens up, and its vertical stretch factor is 2. The added 9 inside the parentheses is multiplied by 2, so 18 must be subtracted outside.

Procedure for a general quadratic. Given \(y=ax^2+bx+c\) with \(a\ne0\): (1) factor \(a\) from the x-terms; (2) inside the parentheses, add and subtract the square of half the new x coefficient; (3) rewrite the first three terms as a binomial square; (4) distribute and combine the constants. The result is \(y=a(x-h)^2+k\).

Graph accurately from the rewritten equation

In your MVP packetSection 2.5 · Main task · Problems 1–8 · pp. 24–27

Once the equation reads \(y=a(x-h)^2+k\), mark the vertex \((h,k)\), draw the dotted symmetry line \(x=h\), and choose x-values equal distances from h. Calculate outputs in pairs. The sign of a determines up or down, and its magnitude determines width relative to \(y=x^2\).

Worked example 4 — From equation to seven points

Rewrite and graph \(y=x^2-4x+1\).

\[y=x^2-4x+4-4+1=(x-2)^2-3.\]

Vertex \((2,-3)\); axis \(x=2\); minimum \(-3\). At x-values 2, 1/3, 0/4, and −1/5, the y-values are −3, −2, 1, and 6 respectively.

What makes a parabola look wider?

In your MVP packetSection 2.5 · Main task · Problems 1–8 · pp. 24–27

Compare \(y=(x+1)^2+4\) with \(y=\tfrac12(x-1)^2+\tfrac92\). The second graph changes y by only half as much for the same horizontal distance from its vertex; therefore it appears wider. A shift alone never changes width.

2. Ready review — Recognize a quadratic equation

In your MVP packetSection 2.5 · Ready · Problems 1–6 · p. 28

The first six homework problems ask whether an equation is quadratic. Simplify both sides first. An equation is quadratic in x when, after collecting like terms, its highest surviving power of x is 2. If the x² terms cancel completely, the equation is not quadratic.

Worked example 5 — Cancellation changes the degree \[5x^2+2x=5x^2+7\quad\Longrightarrow\quad2x=7.\]

This is a linear equation, not quadratic, even though x² appeared at first.

Compare \(3x(x-4)=0\): distributing gives \(3x^2-12x=0\), which is quadratic.

Packet clarification — 2.5 Ready, problems 1–6Equations versus functions

The prompt says to identify whether each equation represents a quadratic function, but several are equalities such as \(x^2+13x-4=0\), rather than functions written as \(y=f(x)\). Interpret the task as asking whether the equation is quadratic in x after simplification; an associated function can be made by setting \(y\) equal to the resulting quadratic expression.

3. Set review — Complete squares with fractions

In your MVP packetSection 2.5 · Set · Problems 7–17 · pp. 28–29

For the shared ideas used in these exercises, revisit the In class explanation.

The Set exercises extend today’s main technique to halves, thirds, fifths, and fourths. Use the same rule, with fraction arithmetic: \(x^2+bx+(b/2)^2=(x+b/2)^2\).

Worked example 6 — A fractional middle term

Complete the trinomial \(x^2-\tfrac35x+\square\).

\[\frac{-3/5}{2}=-\frac3{10},\qquad\left(-\frac3{10}\right)^2=\frac9{100}.\] \[x^2-\frac35x+\frac9{100}=\left(x-\frac3{10}\right)^2.\]

For the homework, keep exact fractions rather than decimal approximations.

Worked example 7 — A negative leading coefficient

Convert \(y=-x^2+2x-5\) to vertex form.

\[\begin{aligned}y&=-(x^2-2x)-5\\&=-[(x-1)^2-1]-5\\&=-(x-1)^2-4.\end{aligned}\]

The vertex \((1,-4)\) is a maximum, because the parabola opens downward.

Packet clarification — 2.5 Set, problem 11The “wider” parabola

Compare the leading multipliers after rewriting problems 9 and 10: problem 9 has \(a=1\); problem 10 has \(a=\tfrac12\). Thus problem 10 is wider. The graph’s apparent width should be compared at a common scale on both coordinate grids.

4. Go review — Linear and quadratic recursive rules

In your MVP packetSection 2.5 · Go · Problems 18–21 · p. 30

In a table with consecutive integer x-values, the first differences are changes between adjacent outputs. The second differences are the differences between those first differences. Equal first differences indicate the linear pattern intended in these exercises; equal, nonzero second differences indicate the quadratic pattern intended here.

A recursive rule gives a starting value and tells how to calculate the next output from the preceding one. An explicit rule calculates a value directly from its input, without needing the previous output.

Worked Go problem A — Linear sequence

For x=1,2,3,4,5, suppose f(x)=4,9,14,19,24.

First differences: +5, +5, +5, +5. The rule is linear, with slope 5. Since f(1)=4, the explicit equation is \(f(x)=5x-1\).

\[\boxed{f(1)=4,\quad f(n)=f(n-1)+5\text{ for }n\ge2.}\]

Both the initial value and update rule are required for a complete recursive description.

Worked Go problem B — Quadratic sequence

For x=1,2,3,4,5, suppose f(x)=3,8,15,24,35.

The step from input n−1 to n is \(2n+1\): at n=2 it is 5; at n=3 it is 7. Thus

\[\boxed{f(1)=3,\quad f(n)=f(n-1)+(2n+1)\text{ for }n\ge2.}\]

Check: \(f(3)=f(2)+7=8+7=15\). These exercises ask for the recursive rule only when the pattern is quadratic.

Worked Go problem C — Decreasing first differences

Let g(1)=12, g(2)=17, g(3)=20, g(4)=21, g(5)=20.

First differences: +5, +3, +1, −1. Second differences: −2, −2, −2, so the pattern is quadratic and opens downward if extended as a quadratic function.

The step into position n is \(9-2n\) (for n=2 it is 5). Therefore

\[\boxed{g(1)=12,\quad g(n)=g(n-1)+(9-2n)\text{ for }n\ge2.}\]
Finite-table limitation. A finite table never proves that the underlying function is quadratic for every real input. In the Go homework, assume the data come from either a linear or quadratic rule, as instructed; classify them using first and second differences under that assumption.

How to construct a recursive rule, step by step

In your MVP packetSection 2.5 · Go · Problems 18–21 · p. 30
  1. List outputs in increasing input order; check equal input spacing (the packet uses increments of 1).
  2. Subtract consecutive outputs to obtain first differences.
  3. If they are constant, state the initial output, constant-step recursive rule, and explicit linear rule.
  4. If first differences change by a constant nonzero amount, express the first difference as a linear function of n, and state the starting value plus the recurrence.
  5. Check two later outputs using the rule.
Packet clarification — 2.5 Go, problems 18–21Expected type and notation

Assume each table is generated by a linear or quadratic function, with domain inputs continuing at integer steps. For a quadratic example, a valid response is an initial value such as \(f(1)=7\) and a recurrence valid for \(n\ge2\). An equation giving only the changing differences is incomplete without the starting value.

Teacher / parent review of the packet

Ready 1–6: The wording switches between “quadratic equations” and “quadratic functions” for equations in x set equal to constants, without giving a y-variable. A clearer prompt is “Simplify each equation and determine whether it is quadratic in x.”

Go 18–21: The instructions ask students to decide linear versus quadratic from a finite table. As written, this conclusion is not logically forced without specifying the intended model family. Add: “Assume each table is generated by a linear or quadratic rule on consecutive integer inputs.”
