1. In class: A Solidify Understanding Task

Revisit the ideas from class

This section explains some of the ideas you explored in class through the guided exercises, in a more formal, step-by-step way. Use it alongside the “A Solidify Understanding Task” portion of Section 2.8H in your MVP packet.

From rectangle dimensions to quadratic expressions

In your MVP packetSection 2.8H · Main task · Problems 1–12 (the final prompt is also numbered 12) · pp. 43–47

Recall that the area of a rectangle is length × width. A rectangle with side lengths \(x+1\) and \(x+3\) has area \((x+1)(x+3)\). Multiplying gives \(x^2+4x+3\). Factoring reverses that multiplication.

Worked example 1 — A multiple of a basic rectangle (main task 1)

Suppose a customer orders two copies of the rectangle with dimensions \(x+1\) and \(x+3\). Its total area is

\[2(x+1)(x+3)=2(x^2+4x+3)=2x^2+8x+6.\]

The leading coefficient is 2 because there are two identical rectangles. Keep the 2 outside the parentheses when you want the number of copies to be visible.

A different situation gives the same sort of expression: a single rectangle may have dimensions \(2x+2\) and \(x+3\). Then \((2x+2)(x+3)=2(x+1)(x+3)\). The picture differs even though the total area formula is equivalent.

Distinction: A factor written outside parentheses, such as \(2(x+1)(x+3)\), can indicate two copies of a rectangle. A factor built into a dimension, as in \((2x+2)(x+3)\), describes one rectangle. Equal algebraic areas do not force identical geometric arrangements.

A single rectangle with a multiple of x in one side

In your MVP packetSection 2.8H · Main task · Problems 1–12 (the final prompt is also numbered 12) · pp. 43–47

Instead of two copies, suppose a rectangular tile layout has sides \(2x+1\) and \(x+4\):

\[(2x+1)(x+4)=2x^2+8x+x+4=2x^2+9x+4.\]

The leading term \(2x^2\) tells us that at least one side can involve a multiple of x. The coefficient of the middle term comes from the two cross-products.

Worked example 2 — Both sides have multiples of x

Expand \((3x+2)(2x+5)\).

\[(3x)(2x)+(3x)(5)+2(2x)+2(5)\] \[=6x^2+15x+4x+10=6x^2+19x+10.\]

Notice how the leading coefficient is \(3\cdot2=6\), the constant is \(2\cdot5=10\), and the middle coefficient is \(15+4=19\).

Factoring when the coefficient of x² is not 1

In your MVP packetSection 2.8H · Main task · Problems 1–12 (the final prompt is also numbered 12) · pp. 43–47

The two-number method from 2.7 worked directly on \(x^2+bx+c\). For \(ax^2+bx+c\), where \(a\) is not 1, we must account for the leading coefficient.

Method 1 — Factor a greatest common factor first

In your MVP packetSection 2.8H · Main task · Problems 1–12 (the final prompt is also numbered 12) · pp. 43–47

A greatest common factor (GCF) is a factor shared by every term.

Worked example 3 — GCF followed by simpler factoring\[3x^2+21x+36=3(x^2+7x+12).\]

Now find two numbers with product 12 and sum 7: 3 and 4.

\[3(x^2+7x+12)=\boxed{3(x+3)(x+4)}.\]

Check: \((x+3)(x+4)=x^2+7x+12\); multiplying by 3 returns the original expression.

Worked example 4 — Difference of squares after extracting a GCF\[3x^2-27=3(x^2-9)=\boxed{3(x-3)(x+3)}.\]

The middle term is zero because the cross-products cancel: \(3x-3x=0\).

Method 2 — The ac method (split and group)

In your MVP packetSection 2.8H · Main task · Problems 1–12 (the final prompt is also numbered 12) · pp. 43–47

To factor \(ax^2+bx+c\) with integer coefficients, look for two integers \(m,n\) such that

\[mn=ac\qquad\text{and}\qquad m+n=b.\]

Replace \(bx\) with \(mx+nx\), then factor the four terms in pairs. This works because we are creating two groups with a shared binomial factor.

Factoring procedure. (1) Factor out any GCF. (2) Multiply \(a\cdot c\). (3) Find integers with product \(ac\) and sum \(b\). (4) Split the middle term. (5) Factor by grouping. (6) Multiply to verify. If there is no suitable integer pair, the polynomial may not factor into integer-coefficient linear factors.
Worked example 5 — Positive coefficients

Factor \(6x^2+7x+2\).

\[ac=6\cdot2=12;\qquad 3+4=7,\quad3\cdot4=12.\] \[6x^2+7x+2=6x^2+3x+4x+2\] \[=3x(2x+1)+2(2x+1)\] \[=\boxed{(3x+2)(2x+1)}.\]

Check: \(6x^2+3x+4x+2=6x^2+7x+2\).

Worked example 6 — A negative constant

Factor \(4x^2-8x+3\).

\[ac=12;\qquad(-6)+(-2)=-8,\quad(-6)(-2)=12.\] \[4x^2-8x+3=4x^2-6x-2x+3\] \[=2x(2x-3)-1(2x-3)\] \[=\boxed{(2x-1)(2x-3)}.\]

Both constants are negative because the constant term is positive and the middle coefficient is negative.

Worked example 7 — Opposite signs

Factor \(3x^2+x-10\).

\[ac=-30;\qquad 6+(-5)=1,\quad 6(-5)=-30.\] \[3x^2+x-10=3x^2+6x-5x-10\] \[=3x(x+2)-5(x+2)=\boxed{(3x-5)(x+2)}.\]

Because \(c\) is negative, the constants in the linear factors must have opposite signs (when the leading coefficient is positive and the factors are written with positive leading terms).

A known side helps you find the other side

In your MVP packetSection 2.8H · Main task · Problems 1–12 (the final prompt is also numbered 12) · pp. 43–47
Worked example 8 — Recover a missing rectangle dimension (main task 6)

The area is \(5x^2+8x+3\), and one side is \(x+1\). Which other side makes the area?

Because the first term must be \(5x^2\), try \(5x\) in the other factor. The constant terms must multiply to 3; with \(+1\) already present, try \(+3\).

\[(x+1)(5x+3)=5x^2+3x+5x+3=5x^2+8x+3.\]

Therefore the other side is \(\boxed{5x+3}\).

Common mistakes. Do not look for numbers with product \(c\) and sum \(b\) when \(a\ne1\); use product \(ac\). Do not forget a GCF. And always multiply the final factors to check both the middle and constant terms.
Worked example 9 — Two x-multiples, both sides (main task 12)

Factor \(10x^2+17x+3\). Find \(ac=30\), with \(15+2=17\).

\[10x^2+17x+3=10x^2+15x+2x+3\] \[=5x(2x+3)+1(2x+3)=\boxed{(5x+1)(2x+3)}.\]

Check: \(10x^2+15x+2x+3\) agrees with the original.

Reading the layout, not just the expression

In your MVP packetSection 2.8H · Main task · Problems 1–12 (the final prompt is also numbered 12) · pp. 43–47

In a rectangle model, \(x^2\) tiles represent squares of side x, the \(x\)-tiles represent rectangles of area x, and unit squares represent area 1. To construct a single rectangle, place them so the entire outer boundary is rectangular and read its side lengths. Different arrangements of the same collection of tiles can produce different outer dimensions or may leave gaps; verify any proposed dimensions algebraically.

2. Ready review — Arithmetic versus geometric sequences

In your MVP packetSection 2.8H · Ready · Problems 1–3 · p. 48

The packet gives the first and fifth terms and asks for the missing three terms. Four equal steps connect term 1 to term 5.

SequenceWhat stays constant?First-to-fifth relationship
ArithmeticAdd a common difference \(d\)\(a_5=a_1+4d\), so \(d=(a_5-a_1)/4\)
GeometricMultiply by a common ratio \(r\)\(a_5=a_1r^4\), so \(r^4=a_5/a_1\) (if \(a_1\ne0\))
Worked example 10 — Arithmetic

The first term is 6 and the fifth term is 30.

\[d=\frac{30-6}{4}=6.\]

Terms 1–5: \(\boxed{6,12,18,24,30}\). Four additions of 6 produce the fifth term.

Worked example 11 — Geometric

The first term is 2 and the fifth term is 162.

\[r^4=\frac{162}{2}=81.\]

Taking a positive common ratio gives \(r=3\): \(\boxed{2,6,18,54,162}\). But \(r=-3\) also works: \(\boxed{2,-6,18,-54,162}\). Both have the same first and fifth terms.

Packet clarification — 2.8H Ready, problems 1–3

The first and fifth terms alone do not determine a unique real geometric sequence when the fifth-to-first ratio is positive. Both positive and negative ratios can give the same fifth term because \((-r)^4=r^4\). If the intended task is to find a unique geometric sequence, it must specify a positive common ratio (or some equivalent restriction). When no restriction is stated, show both possibilities.

3. Set — Area models and additional factoring practice

In your MVP packetSection 2.8H · Set · Problems 4–23 · pp. 49–50

For the shared ideas used in these exercises, revisit the In class explanation.

For the area diagrams (problems 4–7), count the \(x^2\) squares, the \(x\)-rectangles, and the unit tiles to write the expanded expression. Then read the entire rectangle's length and width to write the factored form. Compare the arrangements rather than assuming that identical tile counts mean identical dimensions.

Worked example 12 — Read two equivalent expressions

A rectangle has dimensions \(2x+3\) and \(2x+1\). Write its area both ways.

\[A=(2x+3)(2x+1)\] \[=4x^2+2x+6x+3=\boxed{4x^2+8x+3}.\]

The four area contributions are \(4x^2\), \(2x\), \(6x\), and 3.

Worked example 13 — A negative constant with a non-unit leading coefficient

Factor \(4x^2+7x-2\). Here \(ac=-8\); choose 8 and −1, which sum to 7.

\[4x^2+8x-x-2=4x(x+2)-1(x+2)\] \[=\boxed{(4x-1)(x+2)}.\]

To check your Set factoring, expand each answer and compare the three coefficients with the original. When an expression has two terms, check for a GCF or difference of squares before trying a four-term grouping method.

4. Go review — Finding a parabola’s axis of symmetry from its x-intercepts

In your MVP packetSection 2.8H · Go · Problems 24–29 · p. 50

The x-intercepts are the places a graph crosses the x-axis, and a parabola's axis of symmetry passes halfway between two distinct x-intercepts. If they are at \((r_1,0)\) and \((r_2,0)\), average their x-coordinates:

\[\boxed{x=\frac{r_1+r_2}{2}}\]

This is the midpoint of the two x-intercepts along the x-axis. The answer is an equation for a vertical line, not just a number.

Worked Go problem A — Straightforward

A parabola has x-intercepts at \((-5,0)\) and \((7,0)\). Find the axis of symmetry.

\[x=\frac{-5+7}{2}=\frac{2}{2}=\boxed{1}.\]

The required equation is \(\boxed{x=1}\). It is 6 units from each intercept.

Worked Go problem B — More challenging

The x-intercepts are \((-\tfrac{7}{4},0)\) and \((\tfrac{5}{2},0)\). Find the axis of symmetry.

\[x=\frac{-\frac74+\frac52}{2}=\frac{-\frac74+\frac{10}4}{2}=\frac{\frac34}{2}=\boxed{\frac38}.\]

So the line of symmetry is \(\boxed{x=\frac38}\). The common mistake is forgetting the final division by 2.

Worked Go problem C — Both intercepts negative

Find the axis when the intercepts are \((-11,0)\) and \((-3,0)\).

\[x=\frac{-11+(-3)}2=\frac{-14}2=-7.\]

The line is \(\boxed{x=-7}\). Being between two negative numbers does not make the midpoint positive.

Why the midpoint rule works: the two sides of a parabola are mirror images across its axis. Two points at the same height \(y=0\) are therefore equally far from the axis. This rule applies to a parabola with two distinct x-intercepts.
