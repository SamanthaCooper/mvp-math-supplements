Finding the midpoint of a segment

In your MVP packetSection 6.6 · Main task · pp. 30–32 · Problems 1–6
Find and justify midpoint coordinates, then extend the argument to arbitrary endpoint coordinates.

A midpoint splits a line segment into two parts of equal length. On a number line, the number halfway between 4 and 10 is 7. In the coordinate plane, you find the halfway value separately for the horizontal and vertical coordinates.

Worked example 1 — The garden stakes.

Let the endpoints be \(A=(-9,6)\) and \(B=(3,10)\). Moving from A to B requires going 12 units right and 4 units up:

\[\Delta x=3-(-9)=12,\qquad \Delta y=10-6=4.\]

Half the move is 6 units right and 2 units up. Starting at A gives

\[(-9+6,\ 6+2)=\boxed{(-3,8)}.\]

Check from this point to B: the remaining move is also 6 right and 2 up. Therefore the two halves have equal length.

Packet correction — Section 6.6, Main task, Problem 2 · printed p. 31 (PDF p. 34).

The full instruction in the MVP packet reads:

“2. Explain to Malik why the logic that made him think the midpoint was (6, 2) is almost right, and how to extend his thinking to use the coordinates of the endpoints to get the midpoint of (-3,6).”

The previous paragraph says Malik found (−3, 8), which is correct: the endpoints given in Problem 1 are \((-9,6)\) and \((3,10)\), whose midpoint is \((-3,8)\). The final coordinate in Problem 2 is therefore a typo: “(-3,6)” should read “(-3,8).”

Why the average gives the correct coordinates

If the segment begins at \((x_1,y_1)\) and ends at \((x_2,y_2)\), then the total horizontal move is \(x_2-x_1\) and the total vertical move is \(y_2-y_1\). Halfway from the first point is

\[x_M=x_1+\frac{x_2-x_1}{2}=\frac{x_1+x_2}{2},\] \[y_M=y_1+\frac{y_2-y_1}{2}=\frac{y_1+y_2}{2}.\]
Midpoint formula

For endpoints \((x_1,y_1)\) and \((x_2,y_2)\),

\[\boxed{M=\left(\frac{x_1+x_2}{2},\frac{y_1+y_2}{2}\right).}\]

Average the x-coordinates; average the y-coordinates. Do not average all four numbers together.

Worked example 2 — An odd difference is fine.

Find the midpoint of \((-5,4)\) and \((2,9)\):

\[M=\left(\frac{-5+2}{2},\frac{4+9}{2}\right)=\boxed{\left(-\frac32,\frac{13}{2}\right)}.\]

The midpoint need not have integer coordinates.

Connecting to the two congruent right triangles

In the packet’s drawing, Sapana calls the unknown midpoint \((x,y)\). Because the small right triangles have equal horizontal and vertical legs, their horizontal distances satisfy

\[x-(-9)=3-x\quad\Longrightarrow\quad 2x=-6\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x=-3.\]

Their vertical distances satisfy

\[y-6=10-y\quad\Longrightarrow\quad 2y=16\quad\Longrightarrow\quad y=8.\]

This proves the same result without first assuming the coordinates of the midpoint.

Important distinction: \((12,4)\) describes the change in coordinates between the packet’s endpoints; \((6,2)\) is half of that change. Neither is the midpoint’s location. You must add the half-change to the starting coordinates.

Dividing a segment in a given ratio

In your MVP packetSection 6.6 · Main task · pp. 32–33 · Problems 7–8
Find a point that divides a segment into unequal lengths, including when the segment moves left or downward.

A midpoint divides a segment into two equal pieces. A point can also divide a segment into unequal pieces. For example, if \(AP:PB=1:2\), imagine dividing the whole segment into three equal parts: one from A to P and two from P to B. That puts P one-third of the way from A to B.

We already know how to find a midpoint by taking half the horizontal change and half the vertical change. The same idea works here: take one-third of each coordinate change. That keeps P on the straight line between A and B.

Worked example 3 — One part followed by two parts.

Find P on the segment from \(A=(2,1)\) to \(B=(11,7)\) such that \(AP:PB=1:2\).

From A to B, we move \(11-2=9\) units right and \(7-1=6\) units up. There are three equal parts, so one part is a move of \((9/3,\,6/3)=(3,2)\).

Starting at A, make that one move:

\[P=(2+3,\;1+2)=\boxed{(5,3)}.\]

Check: A to P is a move of \((3,2)\). P to B is \((6,4)\)—twice the same move—so the segment lengths are in the required ratio \(1:2\).

Worked example 4 — Two parts followed by three, moving left and down.

Find P between \(A=(4,10)\) and \(B=(-6,-5)\) so that \(AP:PB=2:3\).

There are five equal parts in all. From A to B, the horizontal change is \(-6-4=-10\) and the vertical change is \(-5-10=-15\). One part is therefore a move of \((-2,-3)\).

To reach P, take two of those parts, moving \((-4,-6)\) from A:

\[P=(4-4,\;10-6)=\boxed{(0,4)}.\]

Check: P to B is a move of \((-6,-9)\), exactly three of the same equal parts. The two pieces have lengths in the ratio \(2:3\).

In general: add the numbers in the ratio to find the total number of equal parts. Work out the horizontal and vertical changes from the first endpoint to the second. Divide each change by the total number of parts, then move the required number of parts from the first endpoint. The steps are the same whether the coordinates increase or decrease.

Common mistake: For a \(2:3\) partition, taking \(2/3\) of the total move is incorrect. The full segment has \(2+3=5\) parts, so you take \(2/5\) from the first endpoint.

2. Ready review — Arithmetic means and halfway values

Study the worked examples first, then try the problems yourself. An answer key at the bottom of this section is provided to check your work.

In your MVP packetSection 6.6 · Ready · p. 34 · Problems 1–12
Review the arithmetic mean of several numbers and the halfway value between two numbers, including negative values.

The arithmetic mean is the sum divided by the number of values. The average of two numbers gives the point exactly halfway between them on a number line.

\[\text{Mean}=\frac{\text{sum of all values}}{\text{number of values}},\qquad \text{Halfway from }a\text{ to }b=\frac{a+b}{2}.\]
Worked example 5 — Mean of four numbers.

The mean of \(6,10,14,18\) is

\[\frac{6+10+14+18}{4}=\frac{48}{4}=12.\]

The mean lies between the smallest and largest values. It need not be one of the listed numbers.

Worked example 6 — Between two negative numbers.

The halfway value between \(-17\) and \(-5\) is

\[\frac{-17+(-5)}{2}=\frac{-22}{2}=\boxed{-11}.\]

Check: \(-11-(-17)=6\) and \(-5-(-11)=6\).

Worked example 7 — A negative and a positive number.

The halfway value between \(-18\) and \(7\) is

\[\frac{-18+7}{2}=\boxed{-\frac{11}{2}}.\]

Do not replace negative numbers with their absolute values before averaging.

3. Set review — Find midpoints on coordinate graphs

In your MVP packetSection 6.6 · Set · pp. 34–35 · Problems 13–18
Read endpoints from graphs or coordinates, and compute midpoint coordinates; diagrams with more than one segment require all requested midpoints.

When a graph gives you the endpoints, you can use the same intuitive method as in the main lesson: start at the first point and move halfway toward the second. Find the total horizontal and vertical changes, take half of each, and add those half-changes to your starting coordinates. This works for slanted, horizontal, and vertical segments.

Worked example 8 — Read coordinates from a graph.

Suppose one plotted segment runs from \(A=(-6,3)\) to \(B=(4,-5)\).

Step 1: Find the movement from A to B.

\[\Delta x=4-(-6)=10,\qquad \Delta y=-5-3=-8.\]

Step 2: Go halfway along that movement, starting at A. Half of 10 is 5; half of \(-8\) is \(-4\).

\[M=\left(-6+\frac{10}{2},\;3+\frac{-8}{2}\right)=(-6+5,\;3-4)=\boxed{(-1,-1)}.\]

You can check: moving from \(M\) to \(B\) also goes 5 units right and 4 units down.

The familiar midpoint formula is simply a shorter way to write this same reasoning:

\[\begin{aligned} x_M&=x_1+\frac{x_2-x_1}{2}=\frac{x_1+x_2}{2},\\ y_M&=y_1+\frac{y_2-y_1}{2}=\frac{y_1+y_2}{2}. \end{aligned}\]

So you can either start and move halfway or average the endpoints—whichever is easier for the problem. On a graph, read each endpoint's coordinates carefully; counting grid squares can help check your result. If a diagram contains several segments, identify the two endpoints of each before calculating.

Worked example 9 — Multiple segments in one diagram.

If \(A=(0,0)\), \(B=(8,0)\), and \(C=(8,6)\), and the drawn segments are \(AB\), \(BC\), and \(AC\), they have three different midpoints:

\[M_{AB}=(4,0),\qquad M_{BC}=(8,3),\qquad M_{AC}=(4,3).\]

Each midpoint belongs to the specific endpoints of its own segment.

4. Set review — Locate points with parallel lines and similar triangles

In your MVP packetSection 6.6 · Set · pp. 35–36 · Problems 19–24
Draw lines parallel to an existing triangle side, find intersection coordinates using proportions, and explain why right angles are unnecessary.

From Sections 6.2–6.4, you know that a line parallel to one side of a triangle forms a smaller similar triangle. Corresponding sides are proportional, even when the triangle is not a right triangle.

Worked example 10 — Solve a partition by parallel sides.

In the diagram, \(DE=4\), \(DB=12\), and \(DC=9\). Because \(EF\) is parallel to \(BC\), the smaller triangle has one-third the corresponding side lengths of the larger:

\[\frac{DF}{DC}=\frac{DE}{DB}=\frac{4}{12},\qquad DF=9\cdot\frac13=\boxed{3}.\]

So the parallel segment meets the vertical side at \(F=(0,3)\).

For coordinate problems, a useful rule follows: if a parallel line cuts a fraction \(t\) of one side measured from a triangle’s vertex, it cuts the same fraction of the other side from that same vertex. That is exactly the coordinate-partition idea from the main lesson.

Worked example 11 — Locate an intersection coordinate.

In the figure, \(D=(-1,-1)\), \(B=(-1,8)\), \(C=(11,-1)\), and \(A=(-1,2)\). Draw \(AP\) parallel to \(BC\), with P on \(DC\).

Along the left side, \(DA=3\) and \(DB=9\), so the smaller triangle is at scale \(t=1/3\) from D. Therefore P is one-third of the way from D to C:

\[x_P=-1+\frac13(11-(-1))=3,\qquad y_P=-1+\frac13(-1-(-1))=-1.\]

Thus \(\boxed{P=(3,-1)}\).

Worked example 12 — A parallel line in a non-right triangle.

Let \(A=(2,1)\), \(B=(8,4)\), and \(C=(5,10)\). Choose \(P=(4,2)\) on \(AB\). The changes A to B and A to P are \((6,3)\) and \((2,1)\), so \(AP/AB=1/3\).

A line through P parallel to BC meets AC at Q, also one-third from A:

\[Q=(2,1)+\frac13(5-2,10-1)=\boxed{(3,4)}.\]

Neither triangle is a right triangle. What matters is the parallelism and corresponding ratios.

Parallel-segment principle: If \(P\) is on \(AB\) and \(Q\) is on \(AC\), with \(PQ\parallel BC\), then \(AP/AB=AQ/AC\). This also means \(AP/PB=AQ/QC\) when the internal segments have nonzero lengths.

5. Go review — Proportions from parallel lines

Study the worked examples first, then try the problems yourself. An answer key at the bottom of this section is provided to check your work.

In your MVP packetSection 6.6 · Go · p. 36 · Problems 25–26
Use corresponding segments along transversals to form proportions and solve for the missing lengths.

When several parallel lines cut two transversals, the segments between the same pair of parallel lines have equal ratios. Keep their order consistent on both transversals. These exercises review the proportionality theorem from Section 6.4.

Go Problem A — Straightforward (new example).

Two rays from the same vertex meet two parallel segments. On the upper ray, the distance from the vertex to the first parallel is 6 units and from the first to the second parallel is 4 units. On the lower ray, the corresponding distances are 9 units and \(x\). Find \(x\).

Match the near segments and the farther segments:

\[\frac{6}{4}=\frac{9}{x}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad 6x=36\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\boxed{x=6}.\]

Check: both rays scale by \(9/6=3/2\), so \(4(3/2)=6\).

Go Problem B — More challenging (new example).

Three parallel lines cut two crossing transversals. The successive segments on the first transversal are 7 and 12. The corresponding successive segments on the other are \(x+1\) and \(2x+1\). Find \(x\) and both unknown segment lengths.

\[\begin{aligned} \frac{7}{12}&=\frac{x+1}{2x+1},\\ 7(2x+1)&=12(x+1),\\ 14x+7&=12x+12,\\ 2x&=5,\qquad x=\frac52. \end{aligned}\]

The actual lengths are \(x+1=\frac72\) and \(2x+1=6\). Check: \((7/12)=(3.5/6)\).

Correspondence before algebra: A proportion can be algebraically solvable but geometrically wrong if you mismatch segments. Use the same pair of parallel lines in each numerator, and the other corresponding pair in each denominator.
