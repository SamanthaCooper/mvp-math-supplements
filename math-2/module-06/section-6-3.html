In Section 6.2, you used a dilation to produce a new triangle with the same angles and proportionally changed sides. This lesson asks the reverse question: if we know some matching angles or sides, is that enough to conclude that two shapes are similar?

1. In class: A Solidify Understanding Task

Revisit the ideas from class

This section explains some of the ideas you explored in class through the guided exercises. Use it alongside the “A Solidify Understanding Task” portion of Section 6.3 in your MVP packet.

What does it mean for figures to be similar?

In your MVP packetSection 6.3 · Main task · Problems 1–11 · pp. 13–17

Congruent figures can be matched through translations, rotations, and reflections: their sizes do not change. Similar figures may additionally use a dilation, so the size can change while the shape stays the same. We write \(\triangle ABC\sim\triangle DEF\) when the named vertices correspond in order: \(A\leftrightarrow D\), \(B\leftrightarrow E\), \(C\leftrightarrow F\).

For polygons, there is an equivalent practical description: every pair of corresponding angles is congruent and every pair of corresponding side lengths is in the same ratio. Both requirements matter. A side-length ratio such as \(\frac{DE}{AB}\) represents image length divided by matching original length; the common value is a scale factor.

Two checks for similar polygons:
  1. Match the vertices and check corresponding angles.
  2. Compare corresponding sides in one consistent direction. Every ratio must agree.

Which familiar families of polygons are always similar?

In your MVP packetSection 6.3 · Main task · Problems 1–11 · pp. 13–17

The main task presents five conjectures. We can evaluate each by asking whether angle measures and side ratios are forced to match.

Rectangles: not all similar

In your MVP packetSection 6.3 · Main task · Problems 1–11 · pp. 13–17

All rectangles have four right angles, but their side ratios need not match. An \(8\times4\) rectangle has width-to-height ratio \(8/4=2\), while a \(6\times5\) rectangle has ratio \(6/5=1.2\). No uniform dilation can make both pairs of sides match.

Worked example 1 — A counterexample to “all rectangles are similar”

Compare a \(2\times3\) rectangle with a \(2\times5\) rectangle. If the short sides are matched, their scale factor would be \(2/2=1\), but matching long sides would require \(5/3\). Since these disagree, the rectangles are not similar. Equal right angles alone do not settle polygon similarity.

Equilateral triangles: always similar

In your MVP packetSection 6.3 · Main task · Problems 1–11 · pp. 13–17

All equilateral triangles have three \(60^\circ\) angles. Once you match one vertex and align its two sides, one dilation matches both side lengths because all three sides in either triangle are equal. Thus every equilateral triangle is similar to every other.

Isosceles triangles: not all similar

In your MVP packetSection 6.3 · Main task · Problems 1–11 · pp. 13–17

“Isosceles” guarantees two equal sides (and the opposite base angles), but it does not fix the angle sizes. A triangle with angles \(70^\circ,70^\circ,40^\circ\) and one with \(50^\circ,50^\circ,80^\circ\) are both isosceles but not similar.

Rhombuses: not all similar

In your MVP packetSection 6.3 · Main task · Problems 1–11 · pp. 13–17

A rhombus has four equal sides, but the angles may differ. A square is a rhombus with four right angles; a rhombus with angles \(60^\circ\) and \(120^\circ\) cannot be similar to a square.

Squares: always similar

In your MVP packetSection 6.3 · Main task · Problems 1–11 · pp. 13–17

Every square has four right angles and four equal sides. Any square can be aligned to another by rigid motions and then dilated by the ratio of its side length to the target side length.

Mason's rotation-and-dilation argument assumes one scale factor will make both the width and height match. That works only if the two rectangles had equal width-to-height ratios to begin with. Aligning one right angle does not guarantee a uniform dilation can match the other vertices.

Why triangles are special: AA, SAS, and SSS similarity

In your MVP packetSection 6.3 · Main task · Problems 1–11 · pp. 13–17

A quadrilateral can change its shape while keeping all four angles fixed. A triangle is more restricted: once its angle measures are fixed, its side proportions are determined.

AA (angle–angle)

In your MVP packetSection 6.3 · Main task · Problems 1–11 · pp. 13–17

If two pairs of corresponding angles are congruent, two triangles are similar. Because every triangle's angles total \(180^\circ\), the third pair also matches. This is often called AAA similarity, but matching just two angles is enough.

Worked example 2 — AA

Triangle \(ABC\) has angles \(45^\circ,65^\circ,70^\circ\). Triangle \(DEF\) has angles \(45^\circ,65^\circ,70^\circ\) in the same corresponding order. Two angles match, so \(\triangle ABC\sim\triangle DEF\) by AA. The triangles might have different sizes.

SAS (side–angle–side)

In your MVP packetSection 6.3 · Main task · Problems 1–11 · pp. 13–17

Two triangles are similar if two pairs of corresponding sides are proportional and the included angles between those sides are congruent. “Included” means the angle made by the two given sides.

Worked example 3 — SAS

Suppose \(AB=6\), \(AC=9\), \(DE=10\), \(DF=15\), and \(\angle A\cong\angle D\). Then \(DE/AB=10/6=5/3\) and \(DF/AC=15/9=5/3\). Because the included angles match, \(\triangle ABC\sim\triangle DEF\).

Important: Two proportional sides and a nonincluded matching angle are not the SAS similarity criterion.

SSS (side–side–side)

In your MVP packetSection 6.3 · Main task · Problems 1–11 · pp. 13–17

Two triangles are similar if all three pairs of corresponding sides are proportional.

Worked example 4 — SSS

Triangle \(ABC\) has side lengths \(3,4,5\), and \(DEF\) has matching side lengths \(4.5,6,7.5\). Check: \(4.5/3=6/4=7.5/5=1.5\). Therefore the triangles are similar by SSS.

Why those three similarity tests work

In your MVP packetSection 6.3 · Main task · Problems 1–11 · pp. 13–17

Each test can be understood with the transformations already learned. The basic strategy is to line up a vertex and one side, then use a dilation to make that side the correct length.

AA — Argument using transformations (main task 8–10)

Translate and rotate the first triangle until a matching vertex coincides with its partner and one side points along the matching ray. Dilate around the common vertex until that side matches the second triangle. Because both angles at that vertex agree, the second side points along the correct ray. Matching the other given angle forces the remaining vertex to be the same intersection point. Hence the triangles coincide after the similarity transformations.

SAS — Argument using transformations (main task 11a)

Align the equal included angles by a rigid motion. The proportional side-length ratios give one common dilation factor \(k\); dilating the first triangle around the angle vertex makes each of the two sides land on its corresponding side. Their endpoints, and therefore the connecting third side, coincide.

SSS — Argument using transformations (main task 11b)

Dilate the first triangle so that one side matches its partner. Because all three corresponding side ratios are equal, every side length now matches the second triangle. The triangles are congruent by SSS congruence, so rigid motions finish the alignment.

These tests are specifically tests for triangles. Do not apply AA or SSS to arbitrary polygons without considering their additional freedom to change shape.

2. Ready — Solving proportions (problems 1–9)

Study the worked examples first, then try the problems yourself. An answer key at the bottom of this section is provided to check your work.

In your MVP packetSection 6.3 · Ready · Problems 1–9 · p. 18

A proportion is an equation between two ratios. If \(a/b=c/d\) and both denominators are nonzero, multiplying both sides by \(bd\) gives \(ad=bc\). You can also scale numerators and denominators or simplify ratios before solving.

Worked example 5 — Cross multiplication

Solve \(\frac{3}{4}=\frac{x}{20}\). Cross-multiply: \(3(20)=4x\), so \(60=4x\) and \(x=15\). Check: \(15/20=3/4\).

Worked example 6 — An expression in the numerator

Solve \(\frac{3}{4}=\frac{b+3}{20}\). Then \(3(20)=4(b+3)\); \(60=4b+12\); \(b=12\). Check: \((12+3)/20=15/20=3/4\).

Worked example 7 — An expression in the denominator

Solve \(\frac{3}{b+2}=\frac65\). Multiply: \(15=6(b+2)\), giving \(15=6b+12\), then \(b=1/2\). Check the denominator: \(b+2=2.5\ne0\), and \(3/2.5=6/5\).

Worked example 8 — Square roots (Ready 9)

Solve \(\frac{\sqrt3}{2}=\frac{\sqrt{12}}{c}\). Since \(\sqrt{12}=2\sqrt3\), cross-multiplication gives \(c\sqrt3=4\sqrt3\). Thus \(c=4\). A useful reminder: simplify radicals before solving when possible.

Ready includes simple unknown numerators, unknown denominators, and expressions such as \(b+3\) and \(b+2\). Check each result by substitution into the original proportion, making sure no denominator is zero.

Check your work — Ready 1–9 answer key
  1. \(x=15\): \(4x=60\).
  2. \(x=6\): \(21x=126\).
  3. \(x=16\): \(3x=48\).
  4. \(c=15\): \(6c=90\).
  5. \(b=12\): \(4(b+3)=60\).
  6. \(a=14\): \(12a=168\).
  7. \(a=\frac{13}{10}\): \(20a=26\).
  8. \(b=\frac12\): \(6(b+2)=15\).
  9. \(c=4\): \(c\sqrt3=2\sqrt{12}=4\sqrt3\).

3. Set — Proofs, counterexamples, and transformations (problems 10–17)

Study the worked examples first, then try the problems yourself. An answer key at the bottom of this section is provided to check your work.

In your MVP packetSection 6.3 · Set · Problems 10–17 · pp. 18–19

Prove or disprove a universal claim

In your MVP packetSection 6.3 · Set · Problems 10–17 · pp. 18–19

To prove that all figures in a group share a property, give a reason that works for every member. To disprove it, one valid counterexample is enough.

Worked example 9 — All right triangles are similar?

No. One right triangle can have acute angles \(30^\circ\) and \(60^\circ\), another \(45^\circ\) and \(45^\circ\). Their angle sets differ, so they cannot be similar. Knowing only the right angle is not enough for AA.

Worked example 10 — Regular polygons with the same number of sides

Yes. A regular \(n\)-gon has all \(n\) equal side lengths and all interior angles \(\frac{(n-2)180^\circ}{n}\). Two regular polygons with the same \(n\) therefore have equal corresponding angles, and every corresponding side ratio is the constant ratio of their side lengths. Hence they are similar.

Read a grid diagram and justify a mapping

In your MVP packetSection 6.3 · Set · Problems 10–17 · pp. 18–19

For Set 12–13, match corresponding vertices in order, count the horizontal and vertical grid movements for each side, and check that lengths change by a common factor and angles are preserved. For Set 14–17, name a specific sequence: for example, translate a selected vertex to the target position, rotate or reflect to align matching edges, and dilate by the ratio of their lengths. The original packet diagrams already provide labeled vertices—use them rather than guessing a shape's correspondence from its position.

Worked example 11 — A precise sequence

Let a triangle have vertices \(A=(1,1)\), \(B=(3,1)\), \(C=(1,3)\). Its image has \(A'=(5,2)\), \(B'=(9,2)\), \(C'=(5,6)\). First dilate by factor 2 about \(A\), giving \(A=(1,1)\), \(B=(5,1)\), \(C=(1,5)\). Then translate by vector \((4,1)\). All three resulting vertices equal the image vertices, so the steps are verified—not merely suggested by appearance.

Packet clarification — 6.3, Set 12–17

The grid diagrams must be read with their original vertex correspondence. Different valid sequences of transformations can produce the same image; unless the task restricts the order, a correct sequence need not match someone else's. State your steps clearly and check every vertex.

For example, \(IJ=3\) grid units becomes \(I\prime J\prime=\frac32\), and \(JK=2\sqrt2\) becomes \(J\prime K\prime=\sqrt2\).

4. Go — Ratios in similar polygons (problems 18–21)

Study the worked examples first, then try the problems yourself. An answer key at the bottom of this section is provided to check your work.

In your MVP packetSection 6.3 · Go · Problems 18–21 · p. 19

The Go exercises ask for three equivalent ratios of corresponding sides. First match corresponding vertices and keep the same direction (image length divided by original length) for every pair. For the rectangles immediately below, the width changes from \(7\) to \(10.5\), and the height changes from \(4\) to \(6\). Both ratios are \(1.5\), so the same dilation factor works in both directions.

Go Problem A — Straightforward

Two similar triangles have corresponding sides \(6,8,10\) and \(3,4,5\). Write three equivalent ratios and explain their order.

Step 1: Match shortest to shortest, medium to medium, longest to longest.

Step 2: Write larger/smaller throughout: \(\frac63=\frac84=\frac{10}5=2\).

Check: Each larger side is twice its partner. If you reverse one fraction, your ratios will disagree.

Go Problem B — More challenging

Two similar quadrilaterals have corresponding sides \(5,7,9,x\) and \(8,11.2,14.4,20.8\). Find \(x\) and three equivalent ratios.

Step 1: The scale factor from first to second is \(8/5=1.6\). Verify \(11.2/7=1.6\) and \(14.4/9=1.6\).

Step 2: Solve \(20.8/x=1.6\). Hence \(x=13\).

Step 3: Three equivalent second/first ratios are \(\frac85=\frac{11.2}{7}=\frac{20.8}{13}=1.6\).

Go Problem C — With a radical (similarity plus the Pythagorean theorem)

A smaller right triangle has legs 3 and 7. A similar larger triangle has corresponding base 21. Find its height and hypotenuse.

Step 1: The length scale factor is \(21/7=3\).

Step 2: The height is \(3(3)=9\).

Step 3: The smaller hypotenuse is \(\sqrt{3^2+7^2}=\sqrt{58}\), so the larger hypotenuse is \(3\sqrt{58}\).

Ratios: \(\frac{21}{7}=\frac93=\frac{3\sqrt{58}}{\sqrt{58}}=3\). This reinforces the radical lengths shown in the packet's final Go problem.

Common error: Corresponding sides can be in a different visual orientation after reflection or rotation. Use matching vertex order and angle features before writing a ratio.
Check your work — Go 18–21 answer key
  1. \(\frac84=\frac63=\frac{10}{5}=2\).
  2. \(\frac a6=\frac84=\frac n4=2\); thus \(a=12,x=4,y=5,n=8\), also \(\frac42=\frac{10}{y}=2\).
  3. \(\frac3{1.5}=\frac5a=\frac c3=2\); \(a=2.5,c=6\).
  4. \(\frac{21}{7}=\frac x3=\frac d{\sqrt{58}}=3\); \(x=9,d=3\sqrt{58}\).
