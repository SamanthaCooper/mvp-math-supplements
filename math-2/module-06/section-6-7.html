1. In class: A Practice Understanding Task

Revisit the ideas from class

This section explains some of the ideas you explored in class through the guided exercises, in a more formal, step-by-step way. Use it alongside the “A Practice Understanding Task” portion of Section 6.7 in your MVP packet.

Why the three right triangles are similar

In your MVP packetSection 6.7 · Main task · p. 37 · Steps 1–6
Cut a right triangle along an altitude to its hypotenuse, compare the resulting triangles, and write corresponding-side proportions.

Begin with a right triangle. Draw an altitude from its right-angle vertex to the hypotenuse—a line segment perpendicular to that side. The altitude divides the original triangle into two smaller right triangles.

Why are the three triangles similar? All three have a right angle. The triangle on the left shares angle L with the original triangle; the triangle on the right shares angle R. Two equal corresponding angles are enough to establish AA similarity. Consequently the original triangle and both smaller triangles are similar to one another.

Corresponding sides must match. The hypotenuse of the large triangle is \(c\). The hypotenuses of the small triangles are \(a\) (left) and \(b\) (right). A side called the “base” in one triangle can correspond to an entirely different-looking side after rotating a triangle.

The altitude divides the hypotenuse into pieces \(x\) and \(y\), so we also know \(x+y=c\). These facts are enough to discover the relationships we need.

Prove the Pythagorean theorem using proportions

In your MVP packetSection 6.7 · Main task · p. 37 · Steps 6–8
Use the matching sides to produce two equations, then combine them to show the Pythagorean theorem.

In the large triangle, the left leg has length \(a\). In the smaller left triangle, the side next to the shared angle L has length \(x\), and its hypotenuse is \(a\). Matching the left small triangle to the large one gives

\[\frac{a}{c}=\frac{x}{a}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad a^2=cx.\]

The same reasoning on the right gives

\[\frac{b}{c}=\frac{y}{b}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad b^2=cy.\]

Add the two equations. Because the two hypotenuse pieces together are the whole hypotenuse, \(x+y=c\):

\[\begin{aligned}a^2+b^2&=cx+cy\\&=c(x+y)\\&=c\cdot c\\&=\boxed{c^2}.\end{aligned}\]
Pythagorean theorem (proved): for a right triangle with legs \(a,b\) and hypotenuse \(c\), \(\boxed{a^2+b^2=c^2}\).

Notice that we did not assume the Pythagorean theorem to derive it. The proof used angle similarity, equal side ratios, and the fact that the two hypotenuse pieces add to \(c\).

The altitude is the geometric mean of the two pieces

In your MVP packetSection 6.7 · Main task · p. 38 · Right Triangle Altitude Theorem 1
Compare the two smaller triangles and establish a proportionality relationship for the altitude.

Compare the two smaller right triangles. Their sides correspond so that the altitude \(h\) in one triangle matches the segment \(y\) in the other, while \(x\) matches \(h\). Thus

\[\frac{x}{h}=\frac{h}{y}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\boxed{h^2=xy}.\]

The geometric mean of two positive numbers is the positive square root of their product: \(\sqrt{xy}\). Since a length is positive,

\[\boxed{h=\sqrt{xy}}.\]
Worked example 1 — Find an altitude from the two hypotenuse pieces.

A right triangle's hypotenuse is divided into segments of lengths 9 and 16 by the altitude from the right angle. Find the altitude.

\[h^2=9\cdot16=144\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\boxed{h=12}.\]

We take the positive square root because a length cannot be negative.

Common mistake: The altitude is not usually the arithmetic average \((x+y)/2\). It is the geometric mean \(\sqrt{xy}\). In the example, the arithmetic average is 12.5, but the actual altitude is 12.

Each leg is a geometric mean, too

In your MVP packetSection 6.7 · Main task · p. 38 · Right Triangle Altitude Theorem 2
Prove a relation between each leg, the full hypotenuse, and the piece of the hypotenuse beside that leg.

Recall the two proportions from the Pythagorean proof. They already give

\[\boxed{a^2=cx}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\boxed{b^2=cy}.\]

So each leg is the geometric mean of the whole hypotenuse and its adjacent segment:

\[a=\sqrt{cx},\qquad b=\sqrt{cy}.\]

Look at the diagram: the left leg \(a\) touches the left part \(x\) of the hypotenuse; the right leg \(b\) touches the right part \(y\). Pairing a leg with the wrong hypotenuse piece produces the wrong answer.

Worked example 2 — Find both legs when the hypotenuse is divided into 9 and 16.

The whole hypotenuse is \(c=9+16=25\). Apply the adjacent-segment rule to each leg:

\[a^2=25(9)=225\Rightarrow \boxed{a=15},\] \[b^2=25(16)=400\Rightarrow \boxed{b=20}.\]

Check with the Pythagorean theorem: \(15^2+20^2=225+400=625=25^2\).

Finding unknown lengths in altitude diagrams

In your MVP packetSection 6.7 · Main task · p. 38 · Final \(x,y\) diagram
Use a given altitude and one hypotenuse piece to find the whole hypotenuse and a missing leg.

Sometimes you know the altitude and one piece of the hypotenuse instead of both pieces. Find the other piece first, using \(h^2=xy\); then add the pieces to get \(c\). If you need a leg, use the full hypotenuse and its adjacent piece.

Worked example 3 — Given one hypotenuse piece and the altitude.

In an original example, the left piece of the hypotenuse is 4 and the altitude is 6. Find the other piece, the whole hypotenuse, and the left leg.

Step 1: \(6^2=4y\), so \(y=9\).

Step 2: \(c=4+9=13\).

Step 3: The left leg touches the piece of length 4, so

\[a^2=cx=13(4)=52\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{a=2\sqrt{13}}.\]

Check: \(h^2=36=4\cdot 9\), as required.

Common mistake: The two hypotenuse pieces must be added to get the full hypotenuse. Do not treat the length of one piece as \(c\). Also distinguish the altitude to the hypotenuse from the legs of the original triangle.

2. Ready review — Recognize similar and congruent triangles

Study the worked examples first, then try the problems yourself. An answer key at the bottom of this section is provided to check your work.

In your MVP packetSection 6.7 · Ready · p. 39 · Problem 1 (Triangles A–H)
Use the angle arcs and side tick marks to identify which pairs are similar and which are provably congruent.

Similar triangles have equal corresponding angles, with corresponding side lengths in the same ratio. Congruent triangles have equal corresponding angles and matching side lengths. Every pair of congruent triangles is also similar, but similar triangles can have different sizes.

What the diagram tells youWhat you can conclude
Two corresponding angles are marked equal (AA).The triangles are similar. Their size need not match.
All three corresponding sides are marked equal (SSS).The triangles are congruent.
Two corresponding angles and one corresponding side are equal (ASA/AAS).The triangles are congruent.
Worked example 4 — A marking is evidence, a sketch is not.

Two triangles both have angles of \(42^\circ\) and \(68^\circ\). Their third angles are \(70^\circ\), so they are similar by AA. If a corresponding side in the first is 5 and in the second is 10, they are not congruent. If those corresponding sides are instead marked equal, the equal scale factor makes the triangles congruent by AAS or ASA.

When reviewing the packet's triangles, start with the identical angle-arc patterns (A, C; B, G; E, F) and identical side-tick patterns (D, H). Decide congruence only when the marks justify it. Do not rely on which triangle looks bigger.

Check your work — Ready 1 answer key
  1. Similar group: A, B, C, E, F, G. The one-, two-, and three-arc angle markings identify the same three angle measures; the triangle sum supplies the unmarked third angle, so AA applies throughout. B and G are congruent by ASA (the marked included side matches); D and H are congruent by SSS and therefore also similar. No other congruence or similarity involving D/H is forced by the markings.

3. Set review — Use the right-triangle proportions

Study the worked examples first, then try the problems yourself. An answer key at the bottom of this section is provided to check your work.

In your MVP packetSection 6.7 · Set · p. 40 · Problems 2–7 (proportions), 8–9 (lengths)
Complete ratios using corresponding sides, then find lengths from right triangles with altitudes to their hypotenuses.

Set problems 2–7 use the same picture, with the notation \(a,b,c\), altitude \(f\), and hypotenuse pieces \(d,e\). It is the same geometry as our diagram, but the symbols have changed: \(h\) becomes \(f\), \(x\) becomes \(d\), and \(y\) becomes \(e\).

Relationships for the Set diagram: \(c=d+e\), \(f^2=de\), \(a^2=cd\), \(b^2=ce\). Every proportional statement must come from corresponding sides of similar triangles.
Worked example 5 — Complete a proportion.

Suppose you are asked to finish \(\dfrac{a}{c}=\dfrac{d}{\square}\). The left small triangle has hypotenuse \(a\), while the original has hypotenuse \(c\). Match the segment \(d\) in the small triangle to the side \(a\) in the large triangle:

\[\frac{a}{c}=\frac{d}{\boxed{a}}.\]

Cross-multiplying yields \(a^2=cd\), the leg theorem.

Worked example 6 — Use the altitude first, not the Pythagorean theorem first.

A right triangle's altitude divides the hypotenuse into two pieces of lengths 4 and 25. Find the altitude and the original leg adjacent to the piece of length 4.

\[f^2=4\cdot25=100\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\boxed{f=10}.\]

The whole hypotenuse is 29, so that original leg has squared length \(4\cdot29=116\), and therefore length \(\boxed{2\sqrt{29}}\).

Worked example 7 — The two original legs are known.

A right triangle has legs 6 and 8, and an altitude from its right angle to the hypotenuse. Find the altitude.

First find the hypotenuse: \(c=\sqrt{6^2+8^2}=10\). The two hypotenuse pieces satisfy

\[x=\frac{a^2}{c}=\frac{36}{10}=\frac{18}{5},\qquad y=\frac{b^2}{c}=\frac{64}{10}=\frac{32}{5}.\]

Then the altitude is

\[h=\sqrt{xy}=\sqrt{\frac{18}{5}\cdot\frac{32}{5}}=\boxed{\frac{24}{5}}.\]
Worked example 8 — The whole hypotenuse and one original leg are known.

A right triangle has hypotenuse 13 and left leg 5. Find the altitude to the hypotenuse.

First find the other leg: \(b=\sqrt{13^2-5^2}=12\). Now use the leg relations to identify the two hypotenuse pieces:

\[x=\frac{5^2}{13}=\frac{25}{13},\qquad y=\frac{12^2}{13}=\frac{144}{13}.\]

Finally,

\[h=\sqrt{xy}=\sqrt{\frac{25\cdot144}{169}}=\boxed{\frac{60}{13}}.\]

For Set 8–9: if two original legs are given, first find the hypotenuse using the Pythagorean theorem; if a hypotenuse and one original leg are given, find the missing hypotenuse segment using the leg theorem. Once both hypotenuse pieces are known, the altitude follows from the altitude theorem. These are applications of the same relationships, not new trigonometry.

Check your work — Set 2–9 answer key
  1. \(\frac ac=\frac fb\): blank \(b\).
  2. \(\frac af=\frac cb\): blank \(b\).
  3. \(\frac ab=\frac fe\): blank \(e\).
  4. \(\frac ad=\frac ca\): blank \(a\).
  5. \(\frac fd=\frac ef\): blank \(f\).
  6. \(\frac bc=\frac eb\): blank \(b\).
  7. Hypotenuse \(13\); altitude \(\frac{60}{13}\), since \(h=\frac{5\cdot12}{13}\).
  8. Missing leg \(\sqrt{39}\); altitude \(\frac{5\sqrt{39}}8\). The hypotenuse is \(8\), so the missing leg squared is \(8^2-5^2=39\).

4. Go review — Solve proportions from parallel lines

In your MVP packetSection 6.7 · Go · p. 41 · Problems 10–11
Use parallel segments to identify similar triangles and solve unknown side lengths with proportions.

A line segment parallel to one side of a triangle creates a smaller triangle with the same angles as the whole triangle. Corresponding side lengths are proportional. You may compare small to large lengths, or compare the upper portion to lower portion on each transversal; just keep the order consistent.

Go practice A — Straightforward proportion.

In an original diagram, a segment inside a triangle is parallel to one side. On the first transversal, the sections are 6 and 3. On the other, the corresponding sections are 10 and an unknown \(x\). Find \(x\).

\[\frac{6}{3}=\frac{10}{x}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad6x=30\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\boxed{x=5}.\]

The same ratio holds on each transversal because the small and large triangles are similar.

Go practice B — Find a missing lower section from full lengths.

Two sides of a triangle have total lengths 20 and 35. A parallel segment cuts off a smaller triangle with a corresponding side of length 8 on the first side. Find the lower section on the second side.

The small-to-large scale factor is \(8/20=2/5\). The corresponding small side on the second transversal is \((2/5)(35)=14\). Therefore the lower section is

\[35-14=\boxed{21}.\]

Common mistake: the proportion \(8/20=z/35\) finds the whole upper section \(z\), not the leftover lower section asked for.

5. Go review — Complete arithmetic and geometric sequences

In your MVP packetSection 6.7 · Go · p. 41 · Problems 12–15
Fill missing values in tables whose terms increase by a constant difference or a constant multiplier.

An arithmetic sequence changes by adding the same number each step, called the common difference. A geometric sequence changes by multiplying by the same number each step, called the common ratio.

TypeHow to move one termFour sample terms
ArithmeticAdd 4 each step3, 7, 11, 15
GeometricMultiply by 3 each step2, 6, 18, 54

For a table, count the number of jumps between term numbers, not the number of term labels visible. The move from term 1 to term 4 contains three jumps.

Go practice C — Straightforward arithmetic sequence.

Term 2 equals 11 and term 6 equals 27. Fill terms 3, 4, and 5.

Four jumps carry 11 to 27. The total change is \(27-11=16\); divide by four jumps to get a difference of 4.

\[\boxed{11,\ 15,\ 19,\ 23,\ 27}\qquad\text{(terms 2 through 6)}.\]
Go practice D — More challenging geometric sequence.

Term 3 equals 64 and term 6 equals 8. Find terms 4 and 5, assuming the common ratio is real.

There are three jumps, so \(64r^3=8\), giving \(r^3=1/8\) and \(r=1/2\). Multiply by one-half at each jump:

\[\boxed{64,\ 32,\ 16,\ 8}\qquad\text{(terms 3 through 6)}.\]

The common ratio is a multiplier, not a quantity to subtract. For positive values separated by three jumps, the real ratio here is uniquely \(1/2\).

Common mistake: If four consecutive terms are displayed, there are only three changes between the first and last terms. For arithmetic sequences divide the total difference by the number of changes; for geometric sequences find a multiplier whose repeated product gives the total factor.
