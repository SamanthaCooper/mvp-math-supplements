Constructing a dilation from a given center

In your MVP packetSection 6.2 · Main task · p. 6 · Problems 1–2; Set · p. 10 · Problems 7–10
Draw enlarged or reduced images about the specified center. The same point-by-point construction works for triangles, parallelograms, and pentagons.

A dilation changes all distances from a fixed point by the same scale factor. The fixed point is the center of dilation. A figure before the dilation is the pre-image; the new figure is its image.

For a positive scale factor \(k\), a point \(P\) is sent to \(P'\) on the ray from the center \(O\) through \(P\), with

Construction rule\[OP'=k\cdot OP.\]

When \(k>1\), every point (other than the center) moves farther from \(O\); when \(0<k<1\), it moves closer. The center itself stays fixed.

To dilate a polygon: draw a ray from the center through each vertex, mark its image at the appropriate multiple of its original distance, and connect the image vertices in corresponding order.

Worked example 1 — An enlargement.

Point \(A\) is 3 cm from center \(O\); point \(B\) is 5 cm away. Dilate a triangle with scale factor \(k=3\).

1. Extend the ray \(OA\) and place \(A'\) 9 cm from \(O\).

2. Extend \(OB\) and place \(B'\) 15 cm from \(O\).

3. Do the same for \(C\); connect \(A',B',C'\).

The new side lengths are three times their matching original lengths. In the packet's Problem 1, the center is \(M\), not the origin of a coordinate system; that does not change the method.

Worked example 2 — A reduction.

With \(k=\tfrac12\), mark each image vertex halfway along the segment from \(O\) to the original vertex. A point 8 cm from \(O\) goes to a point 4 cm from \(O\); a side 6 cm long becomes 3 cm.

In the packet's Problem 2, use the center \(N\). The original triangle has not changed: it is the center and the scale factor that determine where the new triangle appears.

Worked example 3 — The same construction for any polygon.

Suppose a pentagon has two vertices \(P\) and \(Q\) that are 12 cm and 20 cm from center \(C\). For a reduction by \(\tfrac14\), place \(P'\) at 3 cm and \(Q'\) at 5 cm along rays \(CP\) and \(CQ\). Continue for all remaining vertices, then join them in order. No special rule is needed for pentagons.

Common mistake. For a scale factor of 3, do not move each vertex 3 units from the center. Multiply each original center-to-vertex distance by 3. The distances are generally different.

Corresponding sides and proportional relationships

In your MVP packetSection 6.2 · Main task · p. 7 · Problem 3
Label the two resulting triangles and write and check several correct proportions among the original, enlarged, and reduced sides.

A dilation scales every side by the same factor. In particular, if \(\triangle ABC\) is dilated by \(k=3\) to \(\triangle A'B'C'\), then

\[\frac{A'B'}{AB}=\frac{B'C'}{BC}=\frac{A'C'}{AC}=3.\]

All fractions compare corresponding sides in the same direction: enlarged length over original length. Turning every fraction upside down gives the equally valid common ratio \(\tfrac13\).

Worked example 4 — Check several proportions.

Suppose the original triangle has sides \(AB=4\), \(BC=5\), \(AC=3\). The enlarged copy uses \(k=3\); the reduced copy uses \(k=\tfrac12\).

SideOriginalEnlarged \(k=3\)Reduced \(k=\tfrac12\)
\(AB\)4122
\(BC\)5152.5
\(AC\)391.5

Enlarged to original: \(\tfrac{12}{4}=\tfrac{15}{5}=\tfrac93=3\).

Reduced to original: \(\tfrac24=\tfrac{2.5}{5}=\tfrac{1.5}{3}=\tfrac12\).

Enlarged to reduced: \(\tfrac{12}{2}=\tfrac{15}{2.5}=\tfrac9{1.5}=6\).

Side comparisons within the same triangle: \(\tfrac{AB}{AC}=\tfrac43=\tfrac{A'B'}{A'C'}=\tfrac{12}{9}\), and likewise \(\tfrac{AB}{BC}=\tfrac45=\tfrac{A'B'}{B'C'}=\tfrac{12}{15}\).

These equalities supply many valid proportionality statements, with different choices of corresponding side pairs and orientations. The packet asks for at least ten; rather than guessing ten unrelated equations, systematically use the three side pairs and the three figure-to-figure comparisons.

Watch the order. \(\tfrac{A'B'}{AB}=3\) is correct, but \(\tfrac{AB}{A'B'}=3\) is not: the second fraction is \(\tfrac13\). Matching the correct vertices matters more than the letters themselves.

What happens to lines and angles?

In your MVP packetSection 6.2 · Main task · p. 7 · Problems 4–5
Determine when a segment and its dilated image lie on parallel lines and explain the exception when they lie on the same line.

A dilation with positive scale factor preserves angle measures and sends every line either to a parallel line or to the same line. The distinction depends on where the center lies.

Line behavior under a dilation

• If a line does not pass through the center of dilation, its image is a different, parallel line.

• If the original line does pass through the center, its image lies on the very same line.

For a positive factor, the direction of a side is unchanged, and its length is multiplied by \(k\).

Worked example 5 — Why one side can remain on the same line.

Place the center \(O\) at \((0,0)\) and the endpoints of a side at \(A=(2,0)\) and \(B=(4,0)\). If \(k=\tfrac32\), then \(A'=(3,0)\) and \(B'=(6,0)\). Both \(AB\) and \(A'B'\) lie on the x-axis.

The two supporting lines coincide. In school geometry, which usually requires parallel lines to be distinct, this is not a pair of parallel lines. A side not through \(O\), however, maps to a genuinely parallel line.

Packet clarification — Section 6.2, Main task, Problems 4–5.

“Are corresponding segments parallel?” has a convention-sensitive edge case. A dilation sends the line containing each side to either a distinct parallel line or itself. If your class uses “parallel” only for distinct coplanar lines, the answer to “never, sometimes, always” is sometimes: the exception is a side whose line passes through the center. If coincident lines are also treated as parallel in the classroom's terminology, that answer changes. State the convention rather than presenting an unconditional rule.

A proof using dilation: the triangle midsegment theorem

In your MVP packetSection 6.2 · Main task · p. 8 · Problem 6
Dilate a triangle by a factor of 2 about one vertex, then use the diagram to justify the midpoint theorem.

The midpoint of a segment divides it into two equal parts. A midsegment of a triangle joins the midpoints of two sides.

Triangle midsegment theorem

The segment joining the midpoints of two sides of a triangle is parallel to the third side and half as long.

Worked example 6 — Prove the midsegment theorem by dilation.

Start with \(\triangle ABC\) and let \(D\) be the midpoint of \(AB\) and \(E\) the midpoint of \(AC\).

Step 1. Dilate triangle \(ABC\) about vertex \(A\), using \(k=\tfrac12\). Since \(AD=\tfrac12 AB\) and \(AE=\tfrac12 AC\), the new triangle's vertices are precisely \(A,D,E\).

Step 2. A dilation sends side \(BC\), whose line does not pass through \(A\), to side \(DE\) on a distinct parallel line. Hence \(DE\parallel BC\).

Step 3. The side-length scale factor is \(\tfrac12\), so \(DE=\tfrac12 BC\). Both claims are proved.

The packet uses the equivalent reverse construction: expand a triangle about \(A\) by \(k=2\). The original two outer vertices then become the midpoints of the sides of the larger triangle.

Worked example 7 — Apply the theorem.

If \(D\) and \(E\) are midpoints of two sides of a triangle and the third side is 18 cm, then \(DE=\tfrac12(18)=9\) cm. If a midsegment is 6.5 cm, the third side is \(2(6.5)=13\) cm.

2. Ready review — Recognize basic angle relationships

Study the worked examples first, then try the problems yourself. An answer key at the bottom of this section is provided to check your work.

In your MVP packetSection 6.2 · Ready · p. 9 · Problems 1–6
Match six diagrams to alternate interior, vertical, complementary, triangle sum, linear pair, and same-side interior angle relationships.

When two lines intersect, vertical angles lie opposite one another and have equal measures. A linear pair consists of adjacent angles whose outside rays form a straight line; its measures total \(180^\circ\).

When a line (a transversal) crosses two other lines, angles between the two lines are interior. A pair of interior angles on opposite sides of the transversal is alternate interior; a pair on the same side is same-side interior. When the two crossed lines are parallel, alternate interior angles are equal and same-side interior angles are supplementary.

RelationshipHow to recognize itWhat follows
Vertical anglesOpposite one another where two lines crossEqual measures
Linear pairAdjacent angles that together make a straight angleSum to \(180^\circ\)
ComplementaryTwo angles that form a right angle or otherwise add to \(90^\circ\)Sum to \(90^\circ\)
Alternate interiorBetween two lines, opposite sides of their transversalEqual if the two lines are parallel
Same-side interiorBetween two lines, same side of their transversalSum to \(180^\circ\) if parallel
Triangle sumThree interior angles of any triangleSum to \(180^\circ\)
Worked example 8 — Identify first, then calculate.

Two opposite angles at an intersection are labeled \(2x+10\) degrees and \(4x-30\) degrees. They are vertical angles, so

\[2x+10=4x-30\quad\Rightarrow\quad 40=2x\quad\Rightarrow\quad x=20.\]

Each angle is \(50^\circ\). The equation works because they are vertical, not merely because they look equal in the picture.

Worked example 9 — Angle sums.

Angles \(a\) and \(b\) split a right angle. If \(a=37^\circ\), then \(b=90^\circ-37^\circ=53^\circ\). In a separate triangle, if two angles are \(48^\circ\) and \(61^\circ\), the third is \(180^\circ-48^\circ-61^\circ=71^\circ\).

Packet clarification — Section 6.2, Ready, Problem 5.

The diagram appears to intend a perpendicular horizontal and vertical line, with the slanted ray splitting a right angle. That is why the intended name is complementary angles. The drawing does not explicitly mark the right angle, however; angle sum \(90^\circ\) requires that assumption.

3. Set — Find the center and scale factor from two figures

Study the worked examples first, then try the problems yourself. An answer key at the bottom of this section is provided to check your work.

In your MVP packetSection 6.2 · Set · p. 11 · Problems 11–14
Use corresponding pre-image and image vertices to identify a dilation center, direction, and scale factor.

For the shared method, revisit constructing a dilation in the In class section.

When the original figure and its image are both supplied, work backward. Connect a vertex and its corresponding image vertex, such as \(P\) to \(P'\). Do the same for a second pair. The full lines usually meet at the dilation center. Then divide a matching image-side length by the pre-image-side length.

Reverse-dilation check\[k=\frac{\text{image side length}}{\text{pre-image side length}}=\frac{OP'}{OP}.\]

Use the same ratio for a second vertex pair and a second side. For the positive scale factors used here, each original vertex and its image must lie on the same ray from the center.

Worked example 10 — Recover a center and a ratio.

Two triangles have corresponding sides 4 cm and 6 cm. The line through \(A,A'\) intersects the line through \(B,B'\) at \(O\). Measurements give \(OA=5\) cm and \(OA'=7.5\) cm.

First, the side-length ratio is \(k=6/4=3/2\). Second, \(OA'/OA=7.5/5=3/2\), so this pair is consistent. Check that \(B'\) and \(C'\) also lie at 1.5 times the center distances. The dilation center is \(O\), and the image is an enlargement by \(\tfrac32\).

Worked example 11 — One vertex can stay fixed.

If a triangle and its reduced image share vertex \(A\), and the other image vertices lie halfway along rays \(AB\) and \(AC\), the center is \(A\). In particular, \(A'=A\) and \(k=\tfrac12\). This is a useful pattern for the nested triangles in Set Problem 14.

If traced corresponding-point lines do not meet consistently, or different sides give different ratios, the two drawings do not establish a single exact dilation. Printed diagrams may be schematic; confirm the intended geometry with the labeled correspondence rather than trusting approximate measurements alone.

4. Go review — Classify and justify a transformation

Study the worked examples first, then try the problems yourself. An answer key at the bottom of this section is provided to check your work.

In your MVP packetSection 6.2 · Go · p. 12 · Problems 15–18
Decide which single transformation maps the labeled pre-image to the image, then give geometric evidence for it.

Earlier transformation work distinguishes four actions. A translation slides every point by one vector; a reflection flips a figure across a line; a rotation turns a figure through a fixed angle about a fixed point; a dilation scales distances from a fixed point.

TransformationWhat to verify, not just notice
TranslationEvery corresponding point moves the same distance in the same direction.
ReflectionThe same mirror line is the perpendicular bisector of each segment connecting corresponding points (except fixed points on the mirror).
RotationAll corresponding points have equal distances to one center, and their turns around it have the same directed angle.
DilationAll corresponding points lie on center-to-point rays and satisfy one common positive distance ratio; sides are scaled consistently.
New Go problem A — Straightforward: translation.

A triangle has vertices \(P=(0,0)\), \(Q=(3,0)\), \(R=(0,2)\). Its image has \(P'=(4,-1)\), \(Q'=(7,-1)\), \(R'=(4,1)\). Identify and prove the transformation.

Solution. Subtract corresponding coordinates:

\[P'-P=(4,-1),\quad Q'-Q=(4,-1),\quad R'-R=(4,-1).\]

Every vertex moved 4 units right and 1 unit down. Thus the mapping is the translation \((x,y)\mapsto(x+4,y-1)\); the equal displacement of all three vertices establishes the claim.

New Go problem B — More challenging: rotation.

Triangle \(PQR\) has \(P=(1,0)\), \(Q=(3,0)\), \(R=(1,2)\). Its image has \(P'=(0,1)\), \(Q'=(0,3)\), \(R'=(-2,1)\). Find and justify the transformation.

Solution. Each point follows the same quarter-turn about the origin:

\[(1,0)\mapsto(0,1),\quad(3,0)\mapsto(0,3),\quad(1,2)\mapsto(-2,1).\]

The distances to the origin stay unchanged: \(1\) to \(1\), \(3\) to \(3\), and \(\sqrt5\) to \(\sqrt5\). Each point turns \(90^\circ\) counterclockwise. Therefore it is a \(90^\circ\) counterclockwise rotation about \((0,0)\).

New Go problem C — Reflection.

Reflect \(P=(1,1)\), \(Q=(0,3)\), \(R=(1,4)\) across the line \(x=2\). The image vertices are \(P'=(3,1)\), \(Q'=(4,3)\), \(R'=(3,4)\). Explain why this is a reflection.

Solution. Each original and image point has the same \(y\)-coordinate. The midpoint of each horizontal joining segment has \(x=2\): for example, midpoint \(PP'=(2,1)\), midpoint \(QQ'=(2,3)\). A vertical line is perpendicular to every horizontal joining segment, so \(x=2\) is the perpendicular bisector of each. That establishes the reflection.

New Go problem D — Dilation.

Center \(O=(1,1)\). Original vertices \(A=(3,3)\), \(B=(7,3)\), \(C=(3,5)\) correspond to \(A'=(2,2)\), \(B'=(4,2)\), \(C'=(2,3)\). Identify and justify the transformation.

Solution. From \(O\), the vectors to original vertices are \((2,2)\), \((6,2)\), \((2,4)\). The image vectors are \((1,1)\), \((3,1)\), \((1,2)\), exactly half as large. Hence the center is \(O\) and \(k=\tfrac12\). As a second check, \(AB=4\) while \(A'B'=2\).

Use the labels to choose corresponding points. Looking alike is not a proof: some shapes admit more than one transformation. The packet explicitly requests evidence for the claimed transformation. For a rotation, name the center and angle; for a dilation, name the center and scale factor.

Teacher review — Substantive packet issues

1. Main task, Problems 4–5 (p. 7): “never, sometimes, always parallel.” A dilation maps each side's supporting line either to a distinct parallel line or onto itself. Under the usual school definition that parallel lines are distinct, some corresponding sides are coincident rather than parallel when the center lies on the supporting line. The task should explicitly distinguish “parallel or coincident” from “parallel.” A precise revision would ask students to determine when the supporting lines are parallel and distinct, and when they coincide.

2. Ready, Problem 5 (p. 9): missing right-angle marking. The intended answer is complementary angles, apparently using a horizontal and vertical ray that are perpendicular. A true right angle is not marked, so the sum of 90° relies on visual convention instead of an explicit given. Mark the right angle or say that the two rays are perpendicular.

Other construction and identification exercises rely on drawing and measurement rather than stated numerical data; students should check their sketches rather than infer exact ratios from apparent proportions alone.
