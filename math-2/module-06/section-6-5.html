1. In class: A Practice Understanding Task

Revisit the ideas from class

This section explains some of the ideas you explored in class through the guided exercises. Use it alongside the “A Practice Understanding Task” portion of Section 6.5 in your MVP packet.

Work systematically with parallel lines and angles

In your MVP packetSection 6.5 · Main diagram · p. 25 · opening figure; Set 8–9 · p. 28
Apply corresponding, vertical and supplementary angles to a network of parallel lines and transversals.

When several lines cross, one angle often determines many others. The most useful facts come from straight lines, intersecting lines, parallel lines, and triangles.

RelationshipRuleExample

Worked example 1 — Follow one angle through parallel lines.

A transversal crosses parallel lines and makes a \(45^\circ\) angle below the upper line. A corresponding acute angle at the lower crossing is also \(45^\circ\). The angle adjacent to either acute angle is

\[180^\circ-45^\circ=135^\circ.\]

The opposite angle (a vertical angle) is equal to its partner. Labeling only one acute angle and one obtuse angle at a crossing determines the other two.

Worked example 2 — Two diagonals crossing.

Suppose one diagonal rises at \(53^\circ\) to the horizontal and another falls at \(33.7^\circ\) to the horizontal. Their acute angle is the sum

\[53^\circ+33.7^\circ=86.7^\circ.\]

The other angle between the diagonals is supplementary:

\[180^\circ-86.7^\circ=93.3^\circ.\]

This matches the pair of diagonals shown in the main packet illustration on p. 25. The stated angles are rounded, so do not interpret the decimal measures as exact beyond the given precision.

Important distinction. An angle that merely looks the same size is not enough. Mark the particular lines known to be parallel, state whether you used corresponding, alternate interior, or vertical angles, and only then record the equality.

Find lengths by using similar triangles and the Pythagorean theorem

In your MVP packetSection 6.5 · Main diagram · p. 25 · opening figure
Use the marked vertical and horizontal distances to obtain unknown slanted lengths from similar triangles and right triangles.

In the main task, four horizontal lines \(p,q,r,s\) are parallel. Vertical and slanted transversals create right triangles with the same acute angle. These triangles are similar by AA similarity; their corresponding sides share a common scale factor.

Worked example 3 — A 3–4–5 triangle becomes a 9–12–15 triangle.

A small right triangle has legs 3 and 4. Its hypotenuse is

\[\sqrt{3^2+4^2}=\sqrt{25}=5.\]

If the corresponding horizontal leg of a larger similar triangle is 9, the scale factor is \(9/3=3\). Multiply every length by 3:

\[\text{vertical leg}=4(3)=12,\qquad \text{hypotenuse}=5(3)=15.\]

Notice that both the \(3,4\) pair above line \(p\) and the \(9,12\) dimensions elsewhere in the packet’s main diagram fit this pattern.

Worked example 4 — Recover a vertical separation without measuring.

In the packet’s main diagram, the descending transversal crosses from line \(p\) to line \(q\) while going down 12 and horizontally 18. It then crosses from \(q\) to \(r\) while moving horizontally 12. Because the lines are parallel, the two right triangles are similar. Let \(h\) be the drop from \(q\) to \(r\):

\[\frac{h}{12}=\frac{12}{18},\qquad 18h=144,\qquad \boxed{h=8}.\]

The 8-unit vertical separation between \(q\) and \(r\), combined with a 6-unit horizontal interval along the rising transversal, gives a \(6\)-\(8\)-\(10\) right triangle. The packet’s marked length 10 is consistent with this calculation.

Worked example 5 — Check a slanted length against the diagram.

The diagonal from \(p\) to \(q\) drops 12 and moves horizontally 18. Its length should be

\[\sqrt{12^2+18^2}=\sqrt{468}=6\sqrt{13}\approx21.63.\]

The packet labels this segment \(21.6\), which is the same length rounded to one decimal place. A small discrepancy in a computed length is expected when a label is rounded.

A reliable solving order: first determine equal angles from parallelism, then identify triangles that are similar, then set up a proportion with matching corresponding sides. Use \(a^2+b^2=c^2\) when you have a right triangle with two known side lengths.

Interior angles of quadrilaterals, pentagons, and other polygons

In your MVP packetSection 6.5 · Main task · p. 26 · Problems 1–4
Move from the shapes in the diagram to angle-sum conjectures, a general formula, and a proof.

Every triangle has \(180^\circ\) of interior angles. For a polygon, drawing nonoverlapping diagonals to form triangles allows us to use that fact again. A polygon is a closed figure made of straight segments; the formula below applies to a simple polygon (one whose sides do not cross).

Quadrilaterals: two triangles

In your MVP packetSection 6.5 · Main task · p. 26 · Problem 1
Compare interior-angle totals for different quadrilaterals.

Any simple quadrilateral can be divided into two triangles by a diagonal. The four interior angles of the quadrilateral comprise all of the angles in the two triangles, so

\[\boxed{S_4=2(180^\circ)=360^\circ}.\]
Worked example 6 — An unknown quadrilateral angle.

A quadrilateral has angles \(92^\circ\), \(104^\circ\), \(76^\circ\), and \(x\). Then

\[x=360^\circ-(92^\circ+104^\circ+76^\circ)=\boxed{88^\circ}.\]

Its four angles do not need to be right angles or come in matching pairs.

Pentagons: three triangles, even when concave

In your MVP packetSection 6.5 · Main task · p. 26 · Problem 2
Compare convex and concave pentagons; include the reflex interior angle.

A simple pentagon can be divided into three triangles, so its interior angles sum to

\[\boxed{S_5=3(180^\circ)=540^\circ}.\]

A concave polygon has at least one interior angle greater than \(180^\circ\), sometimes called a reflex angle. That large interior angle still counts toward the total. An arbitrary diagonal from a vertex may fall outside a concave polygon, so choose diagonals that stay inside.

Worked example 7 — A concave pentagon.

A pentagon’s four known interior angles are \(85^\circ\), \(110^\circ\), \(95^\circ\), and \(60^\circ\). Then the remaining, reflex angle is

\[540^\circ-(85^\circ+110^\circ+95^\circ+60^\circ)=\boxed{190^\circ}.\]

A \(190^\circ\) angle is possible in a concave pentagon but not in a convex pentagon.

The general pattern and why it works

In your MVP packetSection 6.5 · Main task · p. 26 · Problems 3–4
Express the polygon-angle pattern symbolically and justify why it always holds for simple polygons.

Each extra side beyond a triangle adds one triangle to a triangulation, and therefore adds \(180^\circ\) to the interior-angle sum:

Number of sides \(n\)Nonoverlapping trianglesInterior-angle sum
31\(180^\circ\)
42\(360^\circ\)
53\(540^\circ\)
64\(720^\circ\)
\(n\)\(n-2\)\((n-2)180^\circ\)
Interior-angle sum of a simple \(n\)-gon:\[\boxed{S_n=(n-2)180^\circ},\qquad n\geq 3.\]

Why this is true: A simple polygon with \(n\) vertices can be triangulated into exactly \(n-2\) nonoverlapping triangles using its existing vertices. All the triangles’ angles together equal the polygon’s interior angles. Multiply the number of triangles by \(180^\circ\). Do not assume every interior angle equals \(S_n/n\); that happens only if the polygon is equiangular.

Worked example 8 — A seven-sided polygon.

For a heptagon, \(n=7\), so

\[S_7=(7-2)(180^\circ)=\boxed{900^\circ}.\]

It does not matter whether the sides all have the same length.

Packet clarification — 6.5 main task, p. 26, Problem 2.

The packet explicitly allows non-convex pentagons. An inward-pointing corner has a reflex interior angle greater than \(180^\circ\), not the smaller angle visible outside the pentagon at that vertex. Count the reflex angle in the \(540^\circ\) total. The formula is for simple pentagons, not self-crossing star polygons.

2. Ready — Right triangles and similarity

Study the worked examples first, then try the problems yourself. An answer key at the bottom of this section is provided to check your work.

In your MVP packetSection 6.5 · Ready · p. 27 · Problems 1–7
Compute missing right-triangle sides and compare side-length ratios to determine similarity.

In a right triangle, the two sides forming the right angle are the legs. The side opposite it is the hypotenuse. The hypotenuse is always the longest side.

\[a^2+b^2=c^2\quad\text{where }c\text{ is the hypotenuse}.\]
Worked example 9 — Missing hypotenuse.

A right triangle has legs 6 and 8. Then

\[c^2=6^2+8^2=36+64=100,\qquad\boxed{c=10}.\]
Worked example 10 — Missing leg.

A right triangle has hypotenuse 13 and one leg 5. Let \(b\) be the other leg:

\[b^2+5^2=13^2,\quad b^2=169-25=144,\quad\boxed{b=12}.\]

Subtract the known leg squared when finding another leg; do not add.

To decide if two right triangles are similar, put the side lengths in the same order (short leg, long leg, hypotenuse) and compare corresponding ratios.

Worked example 11 — Similarity from side-length ratios.

Triangle A has sides \(3,4,5\), while Triangle B has sides \(6,8,10\). Then

\[\frac63=\frac84=\frac{10}5=2.\]

All three sides were multiplied by the same factor. The triangles are similar by SSS similarity. But a triangle with sides \(5,12,13\) is not similar to \(3,4,5\), even though both are right triangles: their three corresponding ratios are unequal.

Ready 1–7: The packet explicitly says the drawings are not to scale. Use the printed side lengths, not how long the sides appear. For similarity, match the hypotenuse with the hypotenuse, then the short leg with the short leg and long leg with the long leg.

3. Set — Combine parallel lines, angles, and proportional lengths

Study the worked examples first, then try the problems yourself. An answer key at the bottom of this section is provided to check your work.

In your MVP packetSection 6.5 · Set · p. 28 · Problems 8–9
Find unknown angles and lengths in diagrams with two or three families of parallel lines.

For the shared method, revisit reasoning with parallel lines and angles in the In class section.

When a diagram contains two or three different families of parallel lines, handle one family at a time. Parallelism tells you which angles agree; that information produces similar triangles; similar triangles give side-length ratios.

Worked example 12 — Two parallel cross-sections of a triangle.

In the diagram, \(BC\parallel DE\), so \(\triangle ABC\sim\triangle ADE\). The left side of the smaller triangle is \(AB=6\) and the extra left segment is \(BD=4\). Thus \(AD=10\). The matching smaller right segment is \(AC=9\); let \(CE=x\). Using corresponding full sides:

\[\frac{AB}{AD}=\frac{AC}{AE},\qquad \frac6{10}=\frac9{9+x}.\]

Cross-multiply:

\[6(9+x)=90\Rightarrow 54+6x=90\Rightarrow\boxed{x=6}.\]

A second equivalent proportion compares the strips between the parallel lines:

\[\frac{AB}{BD}=\frac{AC}{CE}=\frac64=\frac96.\]
Worked example 13 — One angle unlocks the others.

Suppose a slanted line forms \(38^\circ\) with the lower of two parallel horizontal lines. Its corresponding acute angle with the upper parallel is \(38^\circ\), and each adjacent obtuse angle measures

\[180^\circ-38^\circ=\boxed{142^\circ}.\]

If a second transversal falls at \(67^\circ\) below the horizontal, the angle between the rising rightward ray and the falling rightward ray is \(38^\circ+67^\circ=\boxed{105^\circ}\). The adjacent, smaller angle between the crossing lines is \(180^\circ-105^\circ=75^\circ\). Mark the actual rays before deciding which angle the question requires.

Worked example 14 — Three parallel lines cut by two transversals.

Three lines \(q,r,s\) are parallel. Along one transversal the consecutive segments between \(q\) and \(r\), and between \(r\) and \(s\), measure 6 and 9. Along a second transversal those corresponding segments measure 10 and \(x\). The lines cut the two transversals proportionally:

\[\frac{6}{9}=\frac{10}{x},\qquad 6x=90,\qquad \boxed{x=15}.\]

If the first transversal meets \(q\) at an acute angle of \(62^\circ\), its corresponding acute angles at \(r\) and \(s\) also measure \(62^\circ\); adjacent obtuse angles measure \(118^\circ\). If some of the other transversals are declared parallel to one another, those create additional equal angle pairs, but do not change which corresponding segments belong together.

For packet Set 8: \(m\parallel n\) and \(o\parallel p\) are two separate facts. Use \(m,n\) to transfer horizontal-transversal angle measures and compare corresponding spans; use \(o,p\) for the matching slanted transversals. The written segment labels (including 20, 9, 6, and 4) supply the lengths for proportions.

For packet Set 9: there are three parallel-line families: \(q\parallel r\parallel s\), \(t\parallel u\), and \(p\parallel w\parallel v\). Highlight one family mentally before moving to another. For example, an interval measured along \(q\) corresponds to an interval along \(r\) or \(s\) only after you identify the same two crossing transversals. Do not form a proportion from unrelated distances just because they are printed near each other.

Packet clarification — 6.5 Set, p. 28, Problems 8–9.

The intersecting diagrams show several angles and line-segment lengths but do not mark every pair of corresponding parts. Treat the declared parallel lines as the givens; identify triangle vertices and matching segments yourself before writing a proportion. The picture alone is not a reliable source of measured lengths or angles.

4. Go — Solve linear equations and proportions

Study the worked examples first, then try the problems yourself. An answer key at the bottom of this section is provided to check your work.

In your MVP packetSection 6.5 · Go · p. 29 · Problems 10–18
Review one-variable linear equations, fractions, parentheses and proportions.

These problems review algebra rather than polygon geometry. Whatever form an equation takes, the goal is to produce an equivalent equation that isolates the unknown. Work on both sides of an equation, and check the result in the original equation.

Distribute and combine like terms

Go worked problem A — Straightforward.

Solve \(4x-7=2x+11\).

\[4x-2x=11+7\Rightarrow2x=18\Rightarrow\boxed{x=9}.\]

Check: \(4(9)-7=29\) and \(2(9)+11=29\).

Go worked problem B — Parentheses on both sides.

Solve \(5+3(2x-4)=2(x+7)+x\).

\[5+6x-12=2x+14+x\] \[6x-7=3x+14\] \[3x=21\Rightarrow\boxed{x=7}.\]

Check: original left side \(5+3(14-4)=35\); right side \(2(7+7)+7=35\).

Clear fractions before isolating the variable

Go worked problem C — Fractional coefficients.

Solve \(\tfrac23x-5=\tfrac14x+5\). Multiply every term by 12, the least common denominator:

\[8x-60=3x+60\Rightarrow5x=120\Rightarrow\boxed{x=24}.\]

Check: \(\tfrac23(24)-5=11\) and \(\tfrac14(24)+5=11\).

Cross-multiply a proportion carefully

Go worked problem D — Simple proportion.

Solve \(\frac7{10}=\frac{x}{30}\). Cross-multiply:

\[7(30)=10x\Rightarrow210=10x\Rightarrow\boxed{x=21}.\]
Go worked problem E — Expressions in numerators.

Solve \(\frac{x+4}{6}=\frac{2(x+1)}9\).

\[9(x+4)=12(x+1)\] \[9x+36=12x+12\] \[24=3x\Rightarrow\boxed{x=8}.\]

Check: \((8+4)/6=2\) and \(2(8+1)/9=2\).

Go worked problem F — Variable in a denominator.

Solve \(\frac3x=\frac{12}{2x+5}\), with \(x\ne0\) and \(2x+5\ne0\).

\[3(2x+5)=12x\Rightarrow6x+15=12x\Rightarrow\boxed{x=\frac52}.\]

Checking gives \(3/(5/2)=6/5\) and \(12/(2(5/2)+5)=12/10=6/5\). The solution is not an excluded value.

Two frequent mistakes: \(3(x-5)=3x-15\), not \(3x-5\). And cross-multiplication only works after you have written one fraction equals one fraction, with nonzero denominators.

Teacher review — Substantive packet issues

Section 6.5 main task, p. 25: The opening asks for “all missing sides and angles” but the diagram does not enumerate target unknowns or mark specific requested measures. Since the figure has many possible composite segments and angles, different students can reasonably stop at different levels of completeness. Suggested clarification: ask for the distances between consecutive parallel lines, the indicated line-segment lengths, the angles at the primary diagonal intersections, and a small number of composite examples.

Section 6.5 main task, p. 26, Problem 2: The hint permits non-convex pentagons, but it does not remind students that their interior angles include reflex angles greater than \(180^\circ\). This distinction affects the correctness of their measurements and total. Suggested clarification: specify that an inward vertex contributes its interior reflex angle and that polygons must be simple (non-self-crossing).

Other problems: No additional serious mathematical error was identified during this review.
