Imagine copying a small drawing at 200% magnification. The drawing looks twice as tall and twice as wide, but why might it move off the edge of the page? And is it “twice as big” or “four times as big”? Both statements can describe different measurements. A dilation explains exactly what happens.

1. Understanding a dilation

A dilation is a transformation that changes the size of a figure while keeping its shape. The original figure is the pre-image; the resized figure is the image. Each point of the pre-image is paired with a corresponding point of the image.

A dilation is determined by two pieces of information: a fixed center of dilation (a reference point) and a positive scale factor \(k\) (the multiplier for distances from that center).

Enlargement If \(k>1\), distances from the center increase.
Reduction If \(0<k<1\), distances from the center decrease.
No size change If \(k=1\), every point stays where it is.

If \(O\) is the center and \(P\) is any point, its image \(P'\) is on ray \(OP\), with distance \(OP'=k\cdot OP\). The center itself stays fixed. Thus, a dilation changes not only side lengths but also how far each point lies from the chosen center.

Worked example 1 — Resizing from a center

Point \(A\) is 3 cm to the right of center \(O\). Point \(B\) is 5 cm above \(O\). Dilate by \(k=2\).

\(OA'=2(3)=6\) cm and \(OB'=2(5)=10\) cm. Put \(A'\) on the ray from \(O\) through \(A\), 6 cm from \(O\). Put \(B'\) on the ray through \(B\), 10 cm from \(O\). The directions from the center remain the same; distances double.

2. Why the center controls placement

A scale factor specifies the size of the image, but it does not by itself specify where the image will be. Moving the center changes where the enlarged drawing lands. This is why a photocopier can enlarge an image accurately and still place it incorrectly on a sheet.

Worked example 2 — Centered versus off-center

A 4-by-6 cm sketch is enlarged to 200%. Its copy measures \(8\) by \(12\) cm. If enlargement is taken about the sketch's upper-left corner, that corner stays fixed and the copy grows rightward and downward. If enlargement is instead centered about the sketch's center, the copy expands equally to either side of that center.

Takeaway: To center the final copy on the paper, align the effective dilation center with the intended center of the final image, accounting for how the copying equipment anchors and positions the image.

Common mistake. “Set the copier to 200%” does not necessarily mean “center the copied picture.” Scale factor and center/placement are separate settings or geometric choices.

3. Length factors are different from area factors

The scale factor \(k\) multiplies every corresponding length by \(k\). For a rectangle with width \(w\) and height \(h\), an enlargement has width \(kw\), height \(kh\), and area

\[A_{\text{new}}=(kw)(kh)=k^2wh=k^2 A_{\text{original}}.\]

Thus the area factor is \(k^2\). This follows because both dimensions are scaled, not because the copier applies the length factor twice to each single side.

Copy settingLength scale factor \(k\)Area multiplier \(k^2\)
50%\(\tfrac12\)\(\tfrac14\)
200%\(2\)\(4\)
300%\(3\)\(9\)
Worked example 3 — Understanding the photocopy complaint

An original picture is 5 inches wide and 7 inches high. A 200% copy is \(2(5)=10\) inches wide and \(2(7)=14\) inches high. The original area is \(5\cdot7=35\) square inches; the new area is \(10\cdot14=140\) square inches.

\(140\div35=4\). So the picture is twice as wide and tall, but four times the area. If one quarter of the complete enlarged image has the same area as the original, the area statement is consistent with 200% enlargement.

4. Constructing a dilation with rays

You can create or complete an enlarged tracing using a straightedge, ruler, or a rubber-band stretcher that keeps each original point, its image, and the fixed center aligned.

Step 1 — Mark the center Choose the fixed point \(O\).
Step 2 — Draw a ray For each important point \(P\) on the original, draw ray \(OP\).
Step 3 — Multiply the distance Measure \(OP\) and locate \(P'\) on the ray at \(OP'=k(OP)\).
Step 4 — Connect corresponding points Draw the edges, or trace enough points on a curved outline, to complete the image.
Worked example 4 — Complete a traced sketch

Suppose a point on a drawing lies 2.5 cm from the center and the photocopier uses a 200% scale. The corresponding point lies \(2(2.5)=5\) cm from the same center along the same ray. Repeat for points around the outline. Curved parts require more sample points to preserve the outline accurately.

Packet clarification — Section 6.1, main task, problems 1–3Interpreting the copier drawing

The packet depicts a sketch and a cropped enlargement. To reconstruct the remaining image, use the center implied by the aligned original and enlarged features, then extend each ray at twice the original distance. The copy's off-center position is a consequence of the placement/anchor used for the enlargement. Problem 3 asks for a placement that centers the resulting image, not merely for positioning the original sketch at the visual center of the starting paper.

5. Ready — Find scale factors and missing lengths (problems 1–6)

For corresponding sides, the scale factor is the ratio image length divided by pre-image length:

\[k=\frac{\text{image side length}}{\text{pre-image side length}}.\]

Then apply \(\text{image length}=k(\text{pre-image length})\). A missing pre-image length is found by dividing the image length by \(k\).

Worked example 5 — Find the scale factor

A small triangle has a side of 4 cm and its image has the corresponding side of 10 cm.

\(k=10/4=2.5\). A second pre-image side of 6 cm becomes \(2.5(6)=15\) cm. All corresponding sides must have the same ratio if the figures are related by this dilation.

Worked example 6 — Reverse a reduction

A reduced image has a side length of 3 cm. The reduction scale factor is \(\tfrac38\). What was the original length?

Let \(L\) be the original length. Then \(3=\tfrac38 L\), so \(L=3\div\tfrac38=3\cdot\tfrac83=8\) cm. Do not multiply by \(\tfrac38\) again when finding the original.

Worked example 7 — An image inside a larger right triangle

A smaller right triangle has vertical leg 4 units. A similar larger right triangle has corresponding vertical leg 10 units. Find the full horizontal leg if the small horizontal leg is 6 units.

Scale factor \(k=10/4=2.5\). Full horizontal leg \(=2.5(6)=15\) units. It does not matter that the small triangle lies inside the large triangle: identify the corresponding sides first.

Check your orientation. A reduction has \(k<1\), even if the two diagrams are drawn in different positions. Pay attention to the words “pre-image” and “image,” not which drawing looks more prominent.

Worked example 8 — A 300% photocopy

A machine is set to 300%. Its length scale factor is \(k=300/100=3\); a 2-inch segment becomes 6 inches long and a 5-square-inch patch becomes \(3^2(5)=45\) square inches. The tool is the copier; the effective center depends on the copier's scan and placement conventions.

7. Go — Recognize linear, exponential, and quadratic functions (problems 13–18)

This is a review of earlier function families, not a new property of dilations. You must classify information given by a table, equation, or graph and justify the classification.

Function familyTypical equationHow to recognize it
Linear\(y=mx+b\)Straight-line graph; equal changes in \(x\) give equal first differences in \(y\).
Exponential\(y=ab^x\), \(b>0\), \(b\ne1\)Multiplicative pattern for equal \(x\)-steps; characteristic increasing or decreasing curved graph.
Quadratic\(y=ax^2+bx+c\), \(a\ne0\)Parabola-shaped graph; equal \(x\)-steps give constant second differences of \(y\).

Important: The first- and second-difference tests assume equally spaced \(x\)-values. If the input gaps differ, compute slopes \(\Delta y/\Delta x\) for a linear table, or analyze the quadratic/exponential rule directly. A small finite table can be consistent with a function family without uniquely proving its global rule.

Worked example 9 — Quadratic from equal-spaced values

Classify the table.

\(x\)0123
\(y\)361118

First differences: \(6-3=3\), \(11-6=5\), \(18-11=7\). Second differences: \(5-3=2\) and \(7-5=2\). The constant second difference indicates a quadratic pattern.

Worked example 10 — Linear from unequal input intervals

Consider \((x,y)=(1,4),(4,13),(10,31),(12,37)\). The input gaps are 3, 6, and 2, so the raw output differences \(9,18,6\) should not be tested for equality. Instead compute slopes:

\(9/3=3\), \(18/6=3\), \(6/2=3\). Equal rates of change suggest the linear rule \(y=3x+1\), which matches all the pairs.

Go Problem A — Straightforward original practice

Determine the type of function represented by \(x=0,1,2,3\) and \(y=5,10,20,40\). Justify your answer.

Consecutive output ratios are \(10/5=2\), \(20/10=2\), and \(40/20=2\). Since each increase of 1 in \(x\) doubles \(y\), the table represents an exponential pattern, modeled by \(y=5(2^x)\).

Go Problem B — More challenging original practice

A table shows \((x,y)=(-2,13),(0,5),(2,5),(4,13)\). Classify the pattern and find one quadratic rule consistent with it.

The \(x\)-steps are all 2; the first differences are \(-8,0,8\), and the second differences are \(8,8\). Thus the table follows a quadratic pattern. The values are symmetric around \(x=1\), so try \(y=a(x-1)^2+c\). From \(x=0\), \(a+c=5\); from \(x=2\), the same equation holds. From \(x=4\), \(9a+c=13\). Subtracting gives \(8a=8\), hence \(a=1\) and \(c=4\). One matching rule is \(\boxed{y=(x-1)^2+4}\).

Go Problem C — Classifying formulas and graphs

Classify \(f(x)=6x-4\), \(g(x)=2(3^x)\), and \(h(x)=-2x^2+5x+1\). Then describe their graph shapes.

\(f\) is linear because its highest power of \(x\) is 1; its graph is a straight line. \(g\) is exponential because the input is an exponent; its graph grows multiplicatively. \(h\) is quadratic because it has a nonzero \(x^2\) term; its graph is a downward-opening parabola.

Packet clarification — Section 6.1, Go, problem 18Unequal input steps

The table's \(x\)-values are 2, 7, 14, and 25, while \(y\)-values are −5, 5, 19, and 41. The corresponding changes are \(\Delta x=5,7,11\) and \(\Delta y=10,14,22\). Each rate \(\Delta y/\Delta x\) equals 2, so the entries fit the linear rule \(y=2x-9\). Comparing only the raw \(y\)-differences would give the wrong impression.

9. Quick self-check

1. A drawing is enlarged by 150%. What is its length multiplier? Its area multiplier?

2. An image side is 18 cm long and the original corresponding side was 12 cm. Find \(k\).

3. Does a 200% enlargement guarantee centering? Explain.

4. What kind of function has first differences \(4,6,8,10\) for consecutive whole-number inputs?

For teachers and parents

Teacher's review of the packet

Section 6.1, Set 7–10 — physical modeling ambiguity. The exercises describe changes of perspective, zoom, and distance as dilations without defining an exact mathematical model or the intended meaning of “center.” Depending on whether one means optical center, viewpoint, vanishing point, or center of a planar enlargement, answers can differ. A clearer prompt would specify that students should use an idealized ray model and identify the viewing/projection center.

Section 6.1, main task 3 — copier reference/placement is implicit. The prompt asks where the original should have been placed for the image to be centered, but no precise geometric mapping from copier glass to output page is stated in words. Students can reason from the packet illustration; an unambiguous numerical construction would need the dilation center or copier anchor explicitly marked.

Section 6.1, Go 18 — nonuniform inputs. This is mathematically solvable, but may catch students who apply the standard first-difference test without checking input spacing. The task could specify that students should compare rates \(\Delta y/\Delta x\) when \(x\)-steps are unequal. This is important instructional guidance, rather than an error in the data.
