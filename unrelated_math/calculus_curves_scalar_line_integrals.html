Calculus of Curves and Scalar Line Integrals

This slide introduces three closely related ideas:

  1. Velocity: How fast are you moving along a curve, and in what direction?
  2. Arc length: How far do you travel along the curve?
  3. Scalar line integrals: What happens when you add up the values of a function along the curve?

The important connection is that all three use the derivative of the parametrization, \(\mathbf r'(t)\).

1. Curves and tangent vectors

A curve in 3D can be described by

\[\mathbf r(t)=(x(t),y(t),z(t))\]

Think of \(\mathbf r(t)\) as the position of a particle at time \(t\).

For example,

\[\mathbf r(t)=(\cos t,\sin t,t)\]

describes a helix.

The derivative is

\[\mathbf r'(t)=(x'(t),y'(t),z'(t))\]

This vector is tangent to the curve and tells us the particle's velocity.

But remember: velocity has both direction and magnitude.

2. Speed is the magnitude of velocity

\[\boxed{\text{Speed}=\|\mathbf r'(t)\|}\]

For a three-dimensional curve,

\[\|\mathbf r'(t)\|=\sqrt{(x'(t))^2+(y'(t))^2+(z'(t))^2}\]

This tells us how much distance the particle travels per unit of time.

If the speed is 5 units per second, then over a very short time interval \(dt\), the particle travels approximately

\[ds=5\,dt\]

More generally,

\[\boxed{ds=\|\mathbf r'(t)\|\,dt}\]

This is the key equation connecting the rest of the slide.

3. Arc length: Add up all the tiny distances

Suppose a particle moves along a curve from \(t=a\) to \(t=b\).

The total distance traveled is

\[L=\int_C ds\]

Using our formula for \(ds\):

\[\boxed{L=\int_a^b\|\mathbf r'(t)\|\,dt}\]

Example: Unit circle

Consider

\[\mathbf r(t)=(\cos t,\sin t),\qquad 0\le t\le2\pi\]

Its derivative is

\[\mathbf r'(t)=(-\sin t,\cos t)\]

Its speed is

\[\|\mathbf r'(t)\|=\sqrt{\sin^2t+\cos^2t}=1\]

Therefore,

\[L=\int_0^{2\pi}1\,dt\]
\[\boxed{L=2\pi}\]

Exactly what we'd expect: the circumference of the unit circle.

4. Scalar line integrals: Add up a function along the curve

This is the new idea.

An ordinary arc length integral adds up little pieces of length:

\[L=\int_C ds\]

A scalar line integral instead multiplies each little piece of length by the value of a function at that location:

\[\boxed{\int_C f(x,y,z)\,ds}\]

Think of a thin wire following a curved path, with varying density.

  • \(ds\) represents a tiny length of wire.
  • \(f(x,y,z)\) represents the density at that location.
  • \(f(x,y,z)\,ds\) represents a tiny amount of mass.

Adding everything gives the total mass:

\[M=\int_C f(x,y,z)\,ds\]

How do we actually calculate it?

We need to convert the integral along the curve into an ordinary integral with respect to \(t\).

We make two substitutions:

First, replace \(x,y,z\) with the parametrization:

\[f(x,y,z)\longrightarrow f(\mathbf r(t))\]

Second, replace \(ds\) with \(\|\mathbf r'(t)\|dt\).

This gives us the formula:

\[\boxed{\int_C f(x,y,z)\,ds=\int_a^b f(\mathbf r(t))\|\mathbf r'(t)\|\,dt}\]

5. The difference between arc length and scalar line integrals

Arc lengthScalar line integral
What are we adding?Tiny lengthsFunction values weighted by tiny lengths
Formula\(\displaystyle\int_C ds\)\(\displaystyle\int_C f\,ds\)
Parametric formula\(\displaystyle\int_a^b\|\mathbf r'(t)\|dt\)\(\displaystyle\int_a^b f(\mathbf r(t))\|\mathbf r'(t)\|dt\)
ExampleLength of a wireMass of a wire with variable density

The main idea to remember:

A scalar line integral is just an arc length integral with an extra function \(f\) multiplying each small piece of length.

When \(f=1\), the scalar line integral becomes the ordinary arc length integral:

\[\int_C 1\,ds=\boxed{\text{Length of }C}\]

That's why the two formulas on your slide look almost identical.
