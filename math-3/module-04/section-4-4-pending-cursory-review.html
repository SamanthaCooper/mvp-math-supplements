Numbers and functions written as ratios

A rational number is a number expressible as \(\frac pq\), where p and q are integers and q≠0. Integers, terminating decimals and repeating decimals are rational.

Main 1 answers: circle \(3,-5,\frac23,\frac{20}3,14,2.7,2^3,3^{-3}\). For example, \(2.7=\frac{27}{10}\), \(2^3=8\), and \(3^{-3}=\frac1{27}\). Do not circle \(\sqrt5\) or \(\log_2 9\); both are irrational. The expression \(\frac70\) is undefined, so it is not a number to classify as rational or irrational.

To justify the less familiar logarithm: if \(\log_2 9=\frac pq\) with positive integers p,q, then \(2^p=9^q=3^{2q}\). Unique prime factorization makes that impossible: a positive power of 2 cannot equal a positive power of 3.

A rational function has the form \(f(x)=\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\) with polynomial numerator and denominator, where Q is not identically zero. This permits individual zeros of Q; those inputs are excluded from the function’s domain.

Main 2 sample answer: a polynomial divided by another polynomial, using only inputs where the denominator is nonzero. Three examples are \(\frac1x\), \(\frac{x+1}{x-2}\), and \(\frac{x^2+1}{x^2+4}\).

Main 3 sample answer: both kinds are ratios and obey the same fraction identities whenever denominators are nonzero. A rational number is one fixed value; a rational function gives outputs depending on an input. Its outputs need not all be rational numbers, and its denominator may exclude inputs. See 4.3’s rational definition (draft pending human review).

Cancel factors while retaining the original domain

“Reducing” a fraction simplifies its form without changing its value. A factor multiplies the rest of an expression; a term is part of a sum. Cancellation divides an entire numerator and denominator by a common nonzero factor.

  1. Find and record every zero of the original denominator.
  2. Factor both numerator and denominator completely.
  3. Divide out common factors; never cancel only part of a sum.
  4. Write the simplified rule together with the original exclusions.
\[\frac{C(x)A(x)}{C(x)B(x)}=\frac{A(x)}{B(x)}\quad\text{where }C(x)B(x)\ne0.\]
Worked solution — Main 4

Original problem: Complete the factoring and common-factor steps for \(\frac{x^2-x-6}{x^2-4}\); the packet supplies the final reduced rule.

Worked solution: the denominator is zero at ±2. Factor:

\[x^2-x-6=(x+2)(x-3),\quad x^2-4=(x+2)(x-2).\]
\[\frac{(x+2)(x-3)}{(x+2)(x-2)}=\frac{x-3}{x-2},\qquad x\ne-2,2.\]

Answers: fill the numerator factoring blank with \((x+2)(x-3)\), and divide the full numerator and denominator by x+2. The resulting rule still excludes −2 because the original fraction is undefined there.

Worked solution — Main 5

Original problem: Complete the factoring and common-factor steps for \(\frac{x^2+8x+15}{x^2+9x+18}\).

Worked solution:

\[\frac{(x+3)(x+5)}{(x+3)(x+6)}=\frac{x+5}{x+6},\qquad x\ne-3,-6.\]

Answers: numerator factors (x+3)(x+5), denominator factors (x+3)(x+6); divide both by x+3. The supplied simplified form is valid only on the original domain.

Main 6 answer: dividing both parts by the same nonzero factor preserves the ratio: the common factor contributes \(\frac{C(x)}{C(x)}=1\). For numerical fractions, \(\frac{24}{30}=\frac{6\cdot4}{6\cdot5}=\frac45\).

Main 7 answer: the proposed cancellation is incorrect. In \(\frac{x}{x^2-1}=\frac{x}{(x-1)(x+1)}\), x is not a common factor of the numerator and entire denominator. For example, at x=2 the original gives \(\frac23\), whereas \(\frac1{x-1}\) gives 1. No factor cancels; the domain excludes ±1.

A canceled factor can leave a hole

A hole is a missing point at a finite location on an otherwise continuous part of a curve. In Main 4’s function, cancellation leaves a rule that would have a finite output at x=−2, but the original function excludes that input.

\[\frac{-2-3}{-2-2}=\frac54.\]

Draw an open circle at \((-2,\frac54)\). The vertical asymptote is x=2, where the remaining denominator is zero. The original numerator is nonzero there. To find a hole, simplify first and evaluate the simplified rule at a canceled denominator zero; it gives a hole when that output is finite.

Main 9: compare your predictions with a technology graph. If you predicted two vertical asymptotes from the original denominator alone, revise the prediction: −2 is a hole. A graphing screen may hide the missing point at its resolution; checking the original rule at that input confirms it is undefined.

Proper and improper fractions

Main 10: for nonnegative integers with positive denominator, a proper fraction has numerator smaller than denominator, such as \(\frac23,\frac45\). An improper fraction has numerator at least as large, such as \(\frac75,\frac{12}5\).

A proper rational expression has numerator degree smaller than denominator degree. It is improper when the numerator degree is equal to or greater than the denominator degree. Compare degrees, not polynomial values at a particular input.

Use quotient plus remainder to rewrite an improper expression

Main 12a: \(37=5\cdot7+2\), so \(\frac{37}5=7+\frac25=7\frac25\). 12b: \(150=12\cdot12+6\), so \(\frac{150}{12}=12+\frac6{12}=12\frac12\).

Polynomial division follows the same structure. If \(P(x)=Q(x)q(x)+r(x)\), then

\[\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}=q(x)+\frac{r(x)}{Q(x)},\qquad Q(x)\ne0.\]

Here q is the quotient and r the remainder, whose degree is less than Q’s degree (or r=0). Review 3.5: polynomial long division (draft pending human review).

A linear quotient gives a slant asymptote

A slant (oblique) asymptote is a nonhorizontal line \(y=mx+b\) whose vertical difference from the curve approaches zero at an end of the graph. In Main 13’s divided form, \(\frac1{x+2}\to0\) as \(x\to\pm\infty\), so the graph approaches the line \(y=x+3\).

\[f(x)-(x+3)=\frac1{x+2}\to0.\]

The outputs do not approach one finite constant; they stay closer and closer to the sloping line. Draw the line dashed to distinguish it from the function.

Main / ruleVertical asymptoteInterceptsAsymptote from divided form
16 · p. 25 / 17: \(\frac{x^2+5x+7}{x+2}\)\(x=-2\)No real x-intercept; \((0,\frac72)\)\(y=x+3\)
18: \(\frac{x^2+2x+5}{x+3}\)\(x=-3\)No real x-intercept; \((0,\frac53)\)\(y=x-1\)

The first numerator has discriminant \(25-28=-3\), so no real zeros. The second is \((x+1)^2+4\), always positive. At the excluded inputs the original numerators are 1 and 8, respectively, so those inputs are vertical asymptotes.

Main 17 graph: the curve is above y=x+3 for x>−2 and below it for x<−2, because the remainder term’s sign changes there.

Plot not shown — Main 18: use technology to graph the original function and the line y=x−1. Check (−4,−13), (−2,5), (0,\(\frac53\)) and (5,5). The curve is above the slant line for x>−3 and below it for x<−3 because \(\frac8{x+3}\) has that sign.

Main 19 — A process for the greater-degree case

  1. Record original denominator exclusions, then simplify common factors and identify any finite holes.
  2. Divide the numerator by the denominator to write quotient plus a proper remainder fraction.
  3. Use the quotient to describe the ends: the remainder fraction approaches zero. A quotient of degree 1 gives a slant line. A higher-degree quotient is a polynomial curve, not a slant line.
  4. Find vertical asymptotes from remaining denominator zeros and find valid intercepts from the original domain.
  5. Plot a few points on each side of the vertical asymptotes and check the graph with technology, marking finite holes.
Wording clarification — Main introduction on p. 25

Read “numerator ... greater than denominator” here as numerator degree greater than denominator degree. A slant asymptote occurs when the simplified numerator degree is exactly one greater; not every greater-degree case has a slant line.
