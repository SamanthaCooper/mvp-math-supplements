1. In class: A Solidify Understanding Task

Revisit the ideas from class

This section explains some of the ideas you explored in class through the guided exercises. Use it alongside the “A Solidify Understanding Task” portion of Section 5.7 in your MVP packet.

In your MVP packetSection 5.7 · Main 1–10 · printed pp. 35–38
Try the packet task on your own first

Attempt the task, then use the explanations and worked solutions below to check your reasoning or work through difficulties.

Match each side to its opposite angle

Use lowercase \(a,b,c\) for the sides opposite uppercase \(A,B,C\). The Law of Cosines relates three sides and an angle; the Law of Sines relates opposite side–angle pairs. Both grow from the shared-altitude reasoning in 5.6.

Main 9 — one worked justification

Original problem: use the packet’s altitude diagram to derive one form of the Law of Cosines. Its base is \(a\), split into \(x\) and \(a-x\), and its height is \(h\).

Worked solution: the two right triangles give the same height:

\[h^2=c^2-x^2=b^2-(a-x)^2\]

Expand and cancel \(x^2\), then use \(x=c\cos B\):

\[b^2=a^2+c^2-2ax\]
\[b^2=a^2+c^2-2ac\cos B\]

Answer: the last equation is one form of the Law of Cosines. Notice that \(B\) is the angle between sides \(a\) and \(c\).

The same height gives a sine relationship

In Main10’s same diagram, \(h=c\sin B=b\sin C\). Divide by \(bc\) to get \(\sin B/b=\sin C/c\). Applying the height idea at another vertex completes the Law of Sines:

\[\frac{\sin A}{a}=\frac{\sin B}{b}=\frac{\sin C}{c}\]

For Main1–8, use the packet’s square-and-rectangle diagram to explain where the cosine correction comes from. Try those steps before opening the compact checks.

2. Sample colors: blue for both \(ab\cos C\) rectangles, red for both \(ac\cos B\) rectangles, green for both \(bc\cos A\) rectangles. Coloring/area annotations are not shown: use the endpoint locations above to label and shade the packet diagram.

3. \(a^2=\text{blue}+\text{red}\); \(b^2=\text{blue}+\text{green}\); \(c^2=\text{green}+\text{red}\).

4. Choosing the first equation, substitute blue = \(b^2-\text{green}\) and red = \(c^2-\text{green}\), giving \(a^2=b^2+c^2-2\text{green}\).

5. Green is \(bc\cos A\), so \(a^2=b^2+c^2-2bc\cos A\).

6. The three versions are:

\[\begin{aligned}a^2&=b^2+c^2-2bc\cos A\\b^2&=a^2+c^2-2ac\cos B\\c^2&=a^2+b^2-2ab\cos C\end{aligned}\]

7. When C is right, \(\cos C=0\). The blue rectangles have zero width; the squares on the legs are no longer split into two positive rectangles. The hypotenuse square is still split by the altitude, and \(c^2=a^2+b^2\).

8. When C is acute, \(ab\cos C\) is positive. Adding \(a^2+b^2\) counts the blue area twice, so subtract \(2ab\cos C\) to get \(c^2\).

9. See the worked derivation above.

10. \(c\sin B=b\sin C\) gives \(\sin B/b=\sin C/c\). For an acute triangle, another altitude includes A. For an obtuse A, use an exterior altitude and \(\sin(180^\circ-A)=\sin A\) to obtain the same third ratio.

3. Set — use the laws to solve triangles

In your MVP packetSection 5.7 · Set 13–22 · printed pp. 39–41

With two angles and a side, find the third angle and use the Law of Sines. With two sides and their included angle, use the Law of Cosines to find the third side. With three sides, solve for an angle, for example

\[\cos A=\frac{b^2+c^2-a^2}{2bc}\]

A calculator’s \(\cos^{-1}\) returns an angle from 0° to 180°, including obtuse angles. For an obtuse angle \(\theta\), its acute supplement \(180^\circ-\theta\) has the same sine and the opposite cosine: \(\sin\theta=\sin(180^\circ-\theta)\), \(\cos\theta=-\cos(180^\circ-\theta)\). This extends the right-triangle ratios to the obtuse angles in these exercises. Keep unrounded values during work; the key rounds measurements to hundredths. Try Set, then check.

17. To use the Law of Sines, start with a known opposite side–angle pair and another side or angle. Two known angles and any side determine a triangle. Two sides and a nonincluded angle can give zero, one, or two triangles, so check possible supplementary angles.

22. The Law of Cosines solves a triangle from three sides (SSS), or from two sides and their included angle (SAS).

For 13–16, use the known opposite pair to calculate each missing side. For 18–19, first compute \(a^2=b^2+c^2-2bc\cos A\), then find B with the cosine formula and C with the angle sum. For 20–21, use the cosine formula on each side–angle pair. Rounded angle sums may differ slightly from 180°.
