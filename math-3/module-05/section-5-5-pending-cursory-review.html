Two ratios worth understanding

A square’s diagonal makes two 45°–45°–90° triangles. Their equal legs have length \(u\), so the Pythagorean theorem gives hypotenuse \(u\sqrt2\). An altitude of an equilateral triangle makes two 30°–60°–90° triangles. If the short leg is \(u\), the original equilateral side is \(2u\), and the other leg is \(\sqrt{(2u)^2-u^2}=u\sqrt3\).

AnglesSide lengths, in order
45°–45°–90°leg \(u\), leg \(u\), hypotenuse \(u\sqrt2\)
30°–60°–90°opposite 30°: \(u\); opposite 60°: \(u\sqrt3\); hypotenuse: \(2u\)

Identify the given side first. A long leg is not the short leg, and a hypotenuse is not a leg. Recover \(u\) from the known side, then use the ratio.

One invented example — start with the long leg

Example: a 30°–60°–90° triangle has long leg 9 cm. Find its other sides.

Worked solution: \(u\sqrt3=9\), so \(u=9/\sqrt3=3\sqrt3\). The short leg is \(3\sqrt3\) cm and the hypotenuse is \(6\sqrt3\) cm.

Try Main before checking the generalizations. For a refresher on choosing ratios, see Math II 6.8: SOHCAHTOA.

4. Go — arithmetic with functions

At the same input \(x\), combine the two outputs. In 19, “subtract \(f\) from \(g\)” means \(g-f\). In a quotient, exclude inputs where the divisor is zero. For a refresher, see 3.6: polynomial operations. Try Go, then check.
