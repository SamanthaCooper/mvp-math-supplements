What a cross section shows

A cross section is the flat shape where a plane intersects a solid. Think of a clean, straight slice and look directly at the cut surface. A plane is a flat surface extending in all directions. A cube has six square faces, twelve edges where two faces meet, and eight vertices where edges meet.

Changing the plane’s direction or position changes the cross section. The two-dimensional drawing of the cube uses perspective, so lengths and angles in that drawing may look different from the true cut surface.

  1. Identify which faces the plane meets.
  2. Mark where it crosses the edges.
  3. On each intersected face, join the intersection points with a straight segment.
  4. Follow those segments around the slice. Classify the actual shape using the solid’s geometry, rather than its appearance in perspective.

Main 1–5: slices of a cube

In your MVP packetSection 5.1 · Main 1–5 · printed pp. 1–2

Main 1 — sample response: both students can reasonably interpret “corner” correctly. Jumal removes a region around a vertex; Jabari removes a region along an edge. Say “slice off a vertex” or describe the intended plane to make the instruction precise.

Main 2: Jumal’s cut surface is a triangle. A plane cutting the three edges meeting at one vertex produces three boundary segments. It is equilateral if the cut points are equally far from that vertex; that equality is not marked in the packet.

Main 3: Jabari’s cut surface is a rectangle: the slice runs along the edge direction, with two other sides parallel in the top and bottom faces. Those directions are perpendicular in the cube.

Main 4 — sample drawings: the gallery below gives possible flat slices viewed straight on. Other choices are possible.

The drawings come from plane intersections with a unit cube; each preserves the slice’s true lengths and angles up to a uniform scale. For your packet drawings, trace the same sort of boundary on the cube. A slice can have three, four, five, or six sides; it cannot have more than six because each of the six cube faces supplies at most one boundary segment.

Main 5 — Original problem and explanation

Original problem: classify the slice through diagonally opposite edges of a cube and explain.

Answer: a rectangle. If the cube’s edge length is \(a\), two sides of the slice are cube edges of length \(a\). The other two are face diagonals of length \(\sqrt{a^2+a^2}=a\sqrt2\). A cube edge perpendicular to a face is perpendicular to any line in that face through its endpoint, including the diagonal. Thus all four angles are right angles. The slice is not a square because \(a\ne a\sqrt2\) for a positive edge length.

Main 6: slices of a right circular cylinder

In your MVP packetSection 5.1 · Main 6 · printed p. 3

A circular cylinder has two circular bases and a curved side. Its axis joins the centers of its bases. Sample cross sections are:

Plane positionCross section
Perpendicular to the axis, between the basesA circular disk.
Oblique to the axis, meeting only the curved sideAn ellipse: a stretched circle viewed in the cutting plane.
Parallel to the axis, cutting the interiorA rectangle; opposite sides come from the parallel bases and straight side generators.

A steep oblique cut can reach a base and give a shape with both straight and curved boundary pieces. To see a rectangle with water, use a sealed cylindrical container and turn it on its side. Sample drawings not shown: draw a circle, ellipse, and rectangle for Main 6, then match each to a plane position.

2. Ready — perimeter, area, volume and units

In your MVP packetSection 5.1 · Ready 1–11 · printed p. 4

Perimeter measures distance around a flat figure; area measures its surface in square units; volume measures space inside a solid in cubic units. Surface area is the total area of a solid’s exposed faces or curved surface.

MeasurementFormula
Rectangle perimeter / area\(P=2(l+w),\quad A=lw\)
Rectangular box volume\(V=lwh\)
Circle circumference / area\(C=2\pi r,\quad A=\pi r^2\)
Sphere volume / surface area\(V=\frac43\pi r^3,\quad S=4\pi r^2\)

3. Set — slices of a cone

In your MVP packetSection 5.1 · Set 12–15 · printed p. 5

Use the cutting plane’s position

A right circular cone has a circular base and a vertex directly above its center. A straight segment from the vertex to the base circle is a generator. The cross section is a filled region when we slice the solid; its boundary is the outline of that region.

A plane parallel to the base gives a disk. A plane through the vertex and the cone’s interior gives a triangle. A plane parallel to a generator, but missing the vertex, gives a parabolic boundary arc on the curved surface. You only need to recognize that curved outline here, not derive its equation.

Finite-cone clarification — Set 13 and 15, printed p. 5

For the pictured finite cone, the parabola in 13 is a boundary arc ending at the base; a complete parabola belongs to the extended cone surface. The “line” in 15 is a finite line segment. Keep the solid slice distinct from the curve on its surface.

Try the questions, then compare your explanations with the key.

4. Go — triangle area from base and perpendicular height

In your MVP packetSection 5.1 · Go 16–19 · printed pp. 5–6

Use \(A=\frac12bh\). The height is the perpendicular distance to the line containing the chosen base; its foot can lie outside the triangle. Take one small grid spacing as one length unit. Try Go, then check.

Go 18 — Original problem and worked solution

Original problem: find the area of \(\triangle EFG\) in the packet figure.

Count \(EG=5\) grid units. The perpendicular altitude \(FH=10\) units lands outside the triangle, but is still the distance to the base line.

\[A=\frac12(5)(10)=25\]

Answer: 25 square grid units.
