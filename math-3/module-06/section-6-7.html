Try the packet task on your own first

Attempt the task before reading the worked examples. Use the explanations to check your reasoning or get help when stuck.

In your MVP packetMain 1–4 · pages 37–40

Scale a circle without changing its angles

Concentric circles share a center. Points on the same ray have the same rotation angle, even on circles of different radii. Multiply the radius by a scale factor and both coordinates and the corresponding arc length multiply by that same factor.

Sixteen equally spaced stakes divide one full turn into \(360^\circ/16=22.5^\circ\), or \(\frac{2\pi}{16}=\frac{\pi}{8}\) radians.

Worked example — Main 2

Original problem: Find the arc between consecutive stakes on the radius-50 circle.

\[s=\frac{22.5}{360}(2\pi\cdot50)=\frac1{16}(100\pi)=50\left(\frac\pi8\right)=\frac{25\pi}{4}\approx19.63\text{ meters}.\]

The degree proportion, fraction of the circumference and radian calculation all measure the same arc. This is not the straight chord between the two stakes.

Use one quadrant, then reflect

For an angle θ in Quadrant I, the point is \((r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)\). Reflecting across the y-axis negates x; across the x-axis negates y. Reflecting across both negates both. Arc lengths from the rightward starting ray keep increasing counterclockwise even when coordinates change signs.

Javier’s radius-10 coordinates on page 40 are rounded. Multiply unrounded values whenever possible, then round the result. For a radius R, the first quadrant is:

3. Set — Coordinates, radius and arc length

In your MVP packetSet 6–11 · pages 41–42

Work independently first. Use the answers below to check your work after you have tried each problem, or to help if you are stuck.

Rectangular coordinates (x,y) give two perpendicular displacements. The pair (r,θ) gives a radius and angle; the pair (r,s) gives a radius and distance around the circle. In this first-quadrant exercise, convert using:

\[r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2},\quad \theta=\tan^{-1}(y/x),\quad s=r\theta\text{ with }\theta\text{ in radians}.\]

For a degree angle, use \(s=(\theta/360^\circ)(2\pi r)\). Keep extra digits until the final rounding. From (r,s), first find the radian angle \(s/r\), then compute the coordinates with your calculator in radian mode.
