Why the unit circle makes the definitions simpler

The unit circle is the circle of radius 1 centered at the origin. Its equation is \(x^2+y^2=1\). Earlier definitions now simplify:

\[\sin\theta=\frac y1=y,\qquad \cos\theta=\frac x1=x.\]

So the point at angle θ is \((\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\): horizontal coordinate first, vertical coordinate second. Also, \(s=r\theta=1\theta=\theta\). On a unit circle, the counterclockwise arc length from (1,0) and the angle in radians have the same numerical value. This equality uses radians; it is not true for the degree measure.

Worked example — Main 1, π/4

Original problem: Find sine, cosine and the decimal value of \(\frac{\pi}{4}\). A 45° right triangle on the unit circle has equal legs and hypotenuse 1. If each leg is a, \(a^2+a^2=1\), so \(a=\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\).

\[(x,y)=\left(\frac{\sqrt2}{2},\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\right),\quad \sin\frac\pi4=\cos\frac\pi4=\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\approx0.707.\]
\[s=\frac\pi4\approx0.785.\]

The angle/arc value 0.785 is different from either coordinate 0.707. A radian is an angle unit, not a sine value.

A complex fraction has a fraction inside its numerator or denominator. Divide by multiplying by the reciprocal. To rationalize a denominator, multiply both numerator and denominator by a radical that removes the square root below the fraction bar.

\[\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\cdot\frac{\sqrt2}{\sqrt2}=\frac{\sqrt2}{2}.\]
