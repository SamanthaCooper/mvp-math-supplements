Define sine using a coordinate

An angle is in standard position when its vertex is at the origin and its initial ray points right along the positive x-axis. A positive angle turns counterclockwise; a negative angle turns clockwise. The ray after the turn is the terminal ray.

Choose any circle centered at the origin with radius \(r>0\). Let \((x,y)\) be where the terminal ray meets the circle. Define:

\[\sin\theta=\frac yr.\]

In Quadrant I this is the familiar opposite/hypotenuse ratio. Elsewhere, y may be negative or zero. The coordinate definition still works, even where no right triangle exists. Changing the circle’s radius does not change the ratio: similar triangles scale y and r by the same amount.

Worked example — Main 9

Original problem: Use the point (−3,4) to find the radius, sine and a rotation angle.

\[r=\sqrt{(-3)^2+4^2}=5,\qquad \sin\theta=\frac45.\]

The point lies in Quadrant II. Its acute reference angle is \(\sin^{-1}(\frac{4}{5})\approx53.13^\circ\), but that is not the full rotation angle:

\[\theta=180^\circ-53.13^\circ\approx126.87^\circ.\]

A calculator in degree mode confirms \(\sin126.87^\circ\approx0.8\). Use the coordinate signs to choose the quadrant; inverse sine alone cannot locate the point.

Signs, repeated values and limits on the output

Sine is positive above the x-axis (Quadrants I and II), negative below it (III and IV), and zero on it. The axis-angle values are:

Angle0°90°180°270°360°
Sine010−10

Angles such as 30° and 150° have the same sine because their points have the same vertical coordinate. Adding or subtracting full 360° turns returns to the same point, so the sine repeats. Every real angle has a sine value, but its output always stays between −1 and 1, inclusive, because \(-r\le y\le r\).

Main 10 check: for (−1,−3), \(r=\sqrt{10}\), \(\sin\theta=-3/\sqrt{10}\), and a counterclockwise angle is \(180^\circ+\tan^{-1}(3)\approx251.57^\circ\). Sketch not shown: draw its terminal ray in Quadrant III.
