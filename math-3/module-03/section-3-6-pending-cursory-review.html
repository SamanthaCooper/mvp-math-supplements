What makes a function polynomial?

A polynomial function is a finite sum of terms with real coefficients and nonnegative integer powers of the input:

\[P(x)=a_nx^n+\cdots+a_2x^2+a_1x+a_0.\]

The allowed powers are \(0,1,2,3,\ldots\). A coefficient can be negative, fractional, irrational, or zero. The variable cannot appear in an exponent, denominator, or fractional power in polynomial form. Simplify expressions before applying the definition, while keeping any original domain restrictions.

The degree of a nonzero polynomial is its highest power with a nonzero coefficient. A nonzero constant has degree 0. The zero polynomial is included in the set of polynomials, but it has no highest nonzero term and is not assigned an ordinary degree here. For example, \(0x^4+3x^2-1\) has degree 2.

Closure is an “every pair” claim

A set is closed under an operation if applying that operation to any two members always produces another member of the same set. Whole numbers are \(0,1,2,\ldots\); integers include their negatives as well.

Examples illustrate a true claim, but a general reason proves it. A single counterexample disproves a claim that says “always.” For division, use a nonzero divisor; division by zero is undefined.

Worked solution — Main 3

Original problem: Determine whether odd numbers are closed under multiplication and explain.

Answer: Yes. Write any two odd integers as \(2a+1\) and \(2b+1\). Then

\[(2a+1)(2b+1)=2(2ab+a+b)+1,\]

which is odd because the quantity in parentheses is an integer. Examples: \(3\cdot5=15\) and \((-3)\cdot7=-21\).

How operations affect degree

Adding or subtracting combines matching powers; it cannot create a higher power. Matching leading terms may cancel, lowering the degree. Multiplying adds the degrees of nonzero polynomials: their leading coefficients multiply to a nonzero number. In these degree claims, linear means degree 1, with nonzero x-coefficient; quadratic means degree 2 and cubic means degree 3.

This explains why polynomial addition, subtraction, and multiplication stay polynomial. Long division may leave a fraction involving the variable. Review 3.3, 3.4, and 3.5 for the operations (all three are drafts pending human review).

4. The set of whole numbers is closed under addition.TrueAdding two nonnegative integers gives a nonnegative integer. \(2+3=5\); \(0+7=7\).
5. The sum of a quadratic function and a linear function is a cubic function.False\(x^2+x\) has degree 2, not 3.

7. The set of polynomials is closed under addition.TrueCombining like terms leaves allowed powers and real coefficients. \((x+1)+(x-1)=2x\); \((x^2+2)+(-x^2+3)=5\).
8. The set of whole numbers is closed under subtraction.False\(2-5=-3\) is not a whole number.
9. The set of integers is closed under subtraction.TrueThe opposite of an integer and the sum of integers are integers. \(2-5=-3\); \((-4)-(-7)=3\).
10. A quadratic function subtracted from a cubic function is a cubic function.TrueA quadratic has no cubic term to cancel the nonzero cubic term. \(x^3-x^2\); \((2x^3+1)-(3x^2-2)=2x^3-3x^2+3\).
11. A linear function subtracted from a linear function is a polynomial function.TrueThe difference has the form \((a-c)x+(b-d)\), possibly constant or zero. \((2x+3)-(x+1)=x+2\); \((x+5)-(x+2)=3\).
12. A cubic function subtracted from a cubic function is a cubic function.False\((x^3+x)-x^3=x\) has degree 1. Cancellation matters.
13. The set of polynomial functions is closed under subtraction.TrueChanging signs and combining like terms preserves polynomial form. \((x^2+1)-(x+2)=x^2-x-1\); \(x^3-x^3=0\).
14. The product of two linear functions is a quadratic function.TrueThe nonzero leading coefficients produce a nonzero \(x^2\) term. \(x(x+1)=x^2+x\); \((2x-1)(x+3)=2x^2+5x-3\).
15. The set of integers is closed under multiplication.TrueInteger multiplication produces an integer, including negative and zero results. \((-3)4=-12\); \((-2)(-5)=10\).
16. The set of polynomials is closed under multiplication.TrueMultiply coefficients and add nonnegative integer exponents; combining terms stays polynomial. \(x(x+1)=x^2+x\); \((x^2+1)(x-1)=x^3-x^2+x-1\).
17. The set of integers is closed under division.False\(1\div2=\frac12\) is not an integer, although both inputs are integers.
18. A cubic function divided by a linear function is a quadratic function.False\(\frac{x^3+1}{x}=x^2+\frac1x\) is not a quadratic function. The polynomial quotient alone has degree 2; the full division result also has a remainder fraction.
19. The set of polynomial functions is closed under division.False\(\frac1x\) divides two polynomials, 1 and x, but is not polynomial.

For exact divisions such as \(x^3\div x=x^2\), the expression simplifies to a polynomial rule for \(x\ne0\), but the original fraction still excludes zero. A single failed division is enough to show that polynomials are not closed under division.

Main 20 — Sample claims

Your answers can differ. Sample claim 1: A nonzero polynomial multiplied by a nonzero constant keeps its degree. For example, \(3(x^2+1)=3x^2+3\) and \(-2(x^3-x)=-2x^3+2x\); scaling does not remove the leading term.

Sample claim 2: The product of a nonzero quadratic and a nonzero cubic is degree 5. For example, \(x^2x^3=x^5\) and \((x^2+1)(x^3+1)=x^5+x^3+x^2+1\); the leading powers add.

Answers: zeros −1 and 3; mark \((-1,0)\) and \((3,0)\). The factor 5 is never zero.

3. Set — Always, sometimes, or never

In your MVP packetSection 3.6 · Set 7–16 · printed pp. 42–43

“Sometimes” needs both a successful example and a failed example. “Always” needs a general reason. A closure statement is about an entire set, so successful individual divisions do not make that statement sometimes true. Use the degree rules and try each claim before checking.

Wording — Set 10, printed p. 42

Interpret “is an integer” as “is a constant function with an integer value.” A function itself is not a number. This is different from asking whether it has integer outputs at selected inputs.

4. Go — Conjugate pairs

In your MVP packetSection 3.6 · Go 17–24 · printed p. 43

In a surd conjugate pair, keep the rational part and reverse the radical part: \(a+b\sqrt d\) pairs with \(a-b\sqrt d\). For a complex conjugate, keep the real part and reverse the imaginary part: \(a+bi\) pairs with \(a-bi\), where \(i^2=-1\). For pure terms, write a zero real or rational part first. For earlier review of number sets and complex arithmetic, see Math II 3.10.

Original examples — keep the first part
\[\frac12+3\sqrt2\quad\longleftrightarrow\quad\frac12-3\sqrt2\]
\[0-7i\quad\longleftrightarrow\quad0+7i.\]

Do not negate the entire expression. The complex conjugate of a real number is itself; the radical exercises use the surd convention instead.

Conjugate products connect to a difference of squares:

\[(a+b\sqrt d)(a-b\sqrt d)=a^2-b^2d\]
\[(a+bi)(a-bi)=a^2+b^2.\]
Qualification — Go explanation, printed p. 43

The packet says irrational or imaginary quadratic solutions always come in conjugate pairs. Nonreal complex roots pair for real coefficients; irrational surd roots pair for rational coefficients. Arbitrary real coefficients do not force an irrational root’s surd conjugate to occur. For example, \((x-\sqrt2)(x-1)\) has roots \(\sqrt2\) and 1, not \(-\sqrt2\).

The answer key follows the intended surd convention for 17, 20 and 23, and the complex convention for the others.
