Count complex roots with multiplicity

A root or zero of a polynomial \(P\) is a number \(r\) for which \(P(r)=0\). Roots can be real or nonreal complex numbers. A real root \(r\) gives the x-intercept \((r,0)\); a nonreal root is not an input on a real-coordinate graph.

Fundamental Theorem of Algebra — root-counting form

A degree-\(n\) polynomial, with \(n\ge1\), has exactly \(n\) complex roots counted with multiplicity.

Multiplicity is how many times the same root occurs in the factorization. If \((x-r)^m\) divides a polynomial and there is no additional factor \(x-r\), the root \(r\) has multiplicity \(m\). In \((x-2)^2\), the root 2 occurs twice: there is one distinct root, counted two times.

Read the theorem precisely — opening statement, printed p. 44; Set reminder, p. 49

The packet’s brief “degree n has n roots” statement counts complex roots, with repeated roots counted according to their multiplicity. It does not promise n distinct x-intercepts.

Review the quadratic tools

Factoring and the zero-product property work when factors are accessible. Completing the square or the quadratic formula gives exact roots when a graph only supplies estimates. For \(ax^2+bx+c=0\), with \(a\ne0\):

\[x=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}.\]

The discriminant \(b^2-4ac\) determines the type of roots: positive gives two distinct real roots, zero gives one real root of multiplicity 2, and negative gives two nonreal complex roots. Recall \(i^2=-1\), so \(\sqrt{-d}=i\sqrt d\) for positive \(d\). See Math II 3.10 for number sets and nonreal quadratic roots.

Worked solution — Main 1(b)

Original problem: Find the roots of \(g(x)=x^2-2x-7\) and identify their type.

Worked solution: Set the output equal to zero, then use \(a=1,b=-2,c=-7\):

\[x=\frac{2\pm\sqrt{(-2)^2-4(1)(-7)}}2\]
\[=\frac{2\pm\sqrt{32}}2=1\pm2\sqrt2.\]

Answers: two real irrational roots, approximately −1.828 and 3.828. The graph locates them approximately; the formula gives their exact values.

Main 2 answer: All four have two roots when nonreal roots and multiplicity are included. Main 3 sample response: I used factoring, completing the square, or the quadratic formula, and checked against the graph where the roots were real.

A root gives a factor

The Factor Theorem connects the two: \(r\) is a root exactly when \(x-r\) is a factor. This is the zero-remainder case of the Remainder Theorem in 3.5 (draft pending human review). Notice the sign: root −3 gives factor \(x+3\), while root 2 gives \(x-2\).

Main 4 checks: Multiply the factors to recover each printed polynomial. For 4(b), the conjugate product is \((x-1)^2-8=x^2-2x-7\). For 4(d), it is \((x-2)^2-i^2=x^2-4x+5\). Plots not shown. For 4(a–c), graph the printed polynomial and its factorization together to check overlap. A real graphing tool may not accept the complex factors in 4(d); expanding their product verifies the equality.

Main 5 answer: Roots are numbers; intercepts are points. Every real root gives an x-intercept, including a point where the graph touches the axis without crossing. Nonreal roots do not give real-coordinate intercepts.

Main 6 answer: A root \(r\) corresponds to the factor \(x-r\). Repeated roots give repeated factors.

Main 7–9 — Three distinct real roots

In your MVP packetSection 3.7 · Main 7–9 · printed p. 46
Worked solution — Main 7

Original problem: Use the supplied graph to find the roots of \(g(x)=x^3-3x^2-10x\), then verify each root in the equation.

Answers: −2, 0 and 5. Substitution checks:

\[g(-2)=-8-12+20=0\]
\[g(0)=0\]
\[g(5)=125-75-50=0.\]

Main 8 answer: Factor out x, then factor the quadratic:

\[g(x)=x(x^2-3x-10)=x(x-5)(x+2).\]

Expand \((x-5)(x+2)=x^2-3x-10\), then multiply by x to recover the original rule. Main 9 answer: Three distinct roots, each of multiplicity 1, total 3, matching the cubic degree.

Repeated roots and the shape near an intercept

In your MVP packetSection 3.7 · Main 10–13 · printed p. 47

Main 10 answers: The supplied graph has real roots −4 and 1. Main 11 factorization and check:

\[x^3+7x^2+8x-16=(x+4)^2(x-1).\]
\[(x^2+8x+16)(x-1)=x^3+7x^2+8x-16.\]

Main 12 answer: The root −4 has multiplicity 2 and the root 1 has multiplicity 1. Their total is 3, as required.

Near a real root, a factor of even multiplicity keeps the same sign on either side, so the graph touches the axis and turns back. A factor of odd multiplicity changes sign, so the graph crosses. The remaining factors are nonzero near that root and do not change this conclusion.

Main 13 answer: \(h(x)=x^3=(x-0)^3\) has one distinct root, 0, of multiplicity 3. The curve crosses the axis and flattens there. Crossing does not mean the multiplicity must be 1.

Main 14–16 — Roots that do not appear as intercepts

In your MVP packetSection 3.7 · Main 14–18 · printed p. 48

Main 14 answers: For \(p(x)=(x+3)(x^2+4)\), solve each factor equation:

\[x+3=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad x=-3\]
\[x^2+4=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad x=\pm2i.\]

Only −3 appears on the real graph. For every real x, \(x^2+4\) is positive, so it cannot supply a real intercept.

Main 15 verification:

\[p(-3)=0\cdot13=0\]
\[p(2i)=(3+2i)((2i)^2+4)=(3+2i)(-4+4)=0\]
\[p(-2i)=(3-2i)((-2i)^2+4)=(3-2i)(-4+4)=0.\]

Main 16 answer: The three distinct complex roots −3, 2i, −2i each have multiplicity 1. Their total is 3.

Main 17–18 — Move between roots and factors

Main 17 answer: Write a factor \(x-r\) for each root, repeat it according to its multiplicity, and multiply by the leading coefficient:

\[P(x)=a(x-r_1)(x-r_2)\cdots(x-r_n).\]

List each root according to its multiplicity; for a double root, include its factor twice. The number \(a\) is the polynomial’s leading coefficient. The roots alone determine a family of constant multiples. Use the original polynomial or another known output to find \(a\); in the Main examples above, it is 1.

Main 18 answer: Set each factor equal to zero and solve. Include nonreal solutions of quadratic factors and record repetitions. A nonzero constant factor changes the graph’s scale, but adds no roots.

3. Set — Root counts, factors and multiplicity

In your MVP packetSection 3.7 · Set 5–17 · printed pp. 49–51

For 5–10, the theorem predicts the total complex root count from the degree. For 11–16, read candidate real roots from the supplied graphs, build factors, and multiply to check. Include a constant multiplier if the leading coefficient is not 1. A graph suggests the roots; expansion confirms their values and multiplicities.

Worked solution — Set 16

Original problem: Check the root prediction and write \(g(x)=x^6-5x^4+4x^2\) in factored form using its supplied graph.

Worked solution: The graph suggests −2, −1, 0, 1 and 2. Zero is a touch point. Algebra confirms:

\[g(x)=x^2(x^4-5x^2+4)\]
\[=x^2(x^2-1)(x^2-4)\]
\[=x^2(x-1)(x+1)(x-2)(x+2).\]

Answer: Four simple roots and a double root at zero total 6. The five distinct intercepts do not contradict the degree-6 root count.

4. Go — Use the quadratic formula

In your MVP packetSection 3.7 · Go 18–23 · printed p. 51

The packet specifically requests the quadratic formula. Identify a, b and c—including c=0 in 20 and b=0 in 21—then substitute, simplify the radical, and give both signs. Study the formula and Main 1(b), then try these equations.

Worked solution — Go 23

Original problem: Find the zeros of \(f(x)=x^2+2x+3\) using the quadratic formula.

\[x=\frac{-2\pm\sqrt{2^2-4(1)(3)}}{2(1)}\]
\[=\frac{-2\pm\sqrt{-8}}2=\frac{-2\pm2i\sqrt2}2\]
\[=-1\pm i\sqrt2.\]

Answers: two nonreal complex zeros. A real graph has no x-intercepts for this function.
