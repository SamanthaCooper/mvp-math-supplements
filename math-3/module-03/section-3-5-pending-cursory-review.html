Factoring reverses multiplication

A factor is an expression multiplied by another to produce the original polynomial. In an area model, the sides give the factors and the cells give the distributed products. For Main 1, split the middle term as \(2x+5x\):

Polynomial long division

In \(P(x)\div D(x)\), \(P\) is the dividend, \(D\) is the divisor, and the result has a quotient \(Q\) and remainder \(R\). Long division repeatedly removes the leading term of what remains.

  1. Write both polynomials in descending powers. Insert a zero coefficient for any missing power.
  2. Divide the current leading term by the divisor’s leading term. Write this next term in the quotient.
  3. Multiply the entire divisor by that term.
  4. Subtract the entire product, changing every sign. Bring down remaining terms.
  5. Repeat until the remainder is zero or its degree is smaller than the divisor’s degree.

The steps parallel \(1452\div12=121\). The supplied polynomial example gives

\[x^3+4x^2+5x+2=(x+2)(x^2+2x+1).\]
Packet correction — Main explanation, printed p. 29

The final paragraph says that 121 divided by 12 leaves no remainder and that 12 is a factor of 121. Replace 121 with 1452 in those two claims. The calculation shown is \(1452\div12=121\); in contrast, \(121\div12=10\) remainder 1.

Stopping rule — Main 3(e), printed p. 30

For polynomials, “smaller” means smaller degree. When dividing by \(x-1\), stop at a constant remainder or zero; comparing expression values is not the stopping test.

Worked solution — Main 3(a–e)

Original problem: Divide \(x^3-3x^2-13x+15\) by \(x-1\), following the printed long-division steps.

(a) Put \(x-1\) outside the division bracket and the dividend inside. Align matching powers. (b) The first quotient term is \(x^3\div x=x^2\). (c) Multiply: \(x^2(x-1)=x^3-x^2\). (d) Subtract:

\[(x^3-3x^2-13x+15)-(x^3-x^2)\]
\[=-2x^2-13x+15.\]

(e) Continue the same cycle:

Quotient termProduct to subtractExpression after subtraction
\(-2x\)\(-2x^2+2x\)\(-15x+15\)
\(-15\)\(-15x+15\)0

Answer: quotient \(x^2-2x-15\), remainder 0. Therefore \(x-1\) is a factor:

\[(x-1)(x^2-2x-15)=x^3-3x^2-13x+15.\]

Answer: quotient \(x^{2}+2x-2\), remainder \(15\). The divisor is not a factor.

\[(2x+3)(x^{2}+2x-2)+(15)=2x^{3}+7x^{2}+2x+9\]
\[(2x+3)\left(x^{2}+2x-2+\frac{15}{2x+3}\right)=2x^{3}+7x^{2}+2x+9\]

The second statement applies where \(2x+3\ne0\).

Two ways to write a remainder

\[P=DQ+R\]
\[P=D\left(Q+\frac{R}{D}\right),\qquad D\ne0.\]

The first form is a polynomial identity for every input. The fraction form requires the divisor to be nonzero. If \(R=0\), the divisor is a factor and the packet asks for only the first multiplication statement.

A remainder of 15 in Main 4 is not the fraction \(\frac{15}{2x+3}\). The division result is the quotient plus that fraction.

The Polynomial Remainder Theorem

When dividing \(P(x)\) by \(x-c\), the remainder is \(P(c)\). To see why, write \(P(x)=(x-c)Q(x)+R\) and substitute \(x=c\); the product disappears, leaving \(P(c)=R\). Consequently, \(x-c\) is a factor exactly when \(P(c)=0\).

For Main 3, \(P(1)=1-3-13+15=0\), confirming the factor. For Main 5(b), \(P(2)=8-16+4+5=1\), confirming its remainder below. Evaluation checks the remainder; use long division when the quotient is required.

Main 5(b)

Original problem: Divide \((x^{3}-4x^{2}+2x+5)\div(x-2)\).

Answer: quotient \(x^{2}-2x-2\), remainder \(1\). The divisor is not a factor.

\[(x-2)(x^{2}-2x-2)+(1)=x^{3}-4x^{2}+2x+5\]
\[(x-2)\left(x^{2}-2x-2+\frac{1}{x-2}\right)=x^{3}-4x^{2}+2x+5\]

Main 5(c)

Original problem: Divide \((6x^{3}-11x^{2}-4x+5)\div(2x-1)\).

Answer: quotient \(3x^{2}-4x-4\), remainder \(1\). The divisor is not a factor.

\[(2x-1)(3x^{2}-4x-4)+(1)=6x^{3}-11x^{2}-4x+5\]
\[(2x-1)\left(3x^{2}-4x-4+\frac{1}{2x-1}\right)=6x^{3}-11x^{2}-4x+5\]

The second statement applies where \(2x-1\ne0\).

Next quotient termMultiply by divisorSubtract; remaining expression

Answer: quotient \(x^{2}-24x+22\), remainder \(75x-40\). The divisor is not a factor.

\[(x^{2}+x+2)(x^{2}-24x+22)+(75x-40)=x^{4}-23x^{3}+49x+4\]
\[(x^{2}+x+2)\left(x^{2}-24x+22+\frac{75x-40}{x^{2}+x+2}\right)=x^{4}-23x^{3}+49x+4\]

The second statement applies where \(x^{2}+x+2\ne0\).

Main 5(d) uses \(0x^2\) as a placeholder. The remainder \(75x-40\) has degree 1, smaller than the degree-2 divisor, so division is complete.

4. Go — Describe a variety of functions

In your MVP packetSection 3.5 · Go 16–21 · printed pp. 35–36

The domain is the set of allowed inputs; the range is the set of resulting outputs. Read increasing/decreasing behavior from left to right. An x-intercept has output zero; a y-intercept requires input zero to be allowed. An absolute maximum/minimum is the highest/lowest output over the whole domain. End behavior describes outputs as inputs approach a domain’s far ends.

Review Math II 2.1 for transformations and Math I 2.1 for sequence inputs. Use the printed graphs for 20–21.

Worked solution — Go 18

Original problem: Graph \(f(x)=\sqrt{x-3}+1\) and identify its features.

Worked solution: Shift the square-root parent right 3 and up 1. The radicand requires \(x-3\ge0\), so the domain is \([3,\infty)\). Outputs begin at 1 and grow, so the range is \([1,\infty)\). Plot \((3,1),(4,2),(7,3),(12,4)\); start with a filled endpoint, then draw a curve that increases and becomes less steep. Plot not shown.

16: Absolute minimum −9 at \(x=0\); no maximum. As \(x\to\pm\infty\), \(f(x)\to\infty\). Plot the vertex \((0,-9)\), intercepts \((\pm3,0)\), and \((\pm4,7)\); draw an upward parabola.

17: The printed recurrence is \(f(n-1)=f(n)+3\), with \(f(1)=4\). Rewrite it in forward form:

\[f(1)=4,\qquad f(n+1)=f(n)-3,\quad n\ge1.\]
\[f(n)=7-3n,\quad n=1,2,3,\ldots.\]

Plot separate dots \((1,4),(2,1),(3,-2),(4,-5)\). The maximum is 4 at \(n=1\); there is no minimum, and \(f(n)\to-\infty\) as \(n\to\infty\). The zero of the continuous line, \(n=\frac73\), is not a sequence input.

Domain convention — Go 17, printed p. 35

The packet does not state the index domain. The answer uses the usual sequence starting at \(n=1\). If your class extends the recurrence to all integer indices, the domain is \(\mathbb Z\), the range is \(\{7-3n:n\in\mathbb Z\}\), the y-intercept is \((0,7)\), and there is no maximum or minimum. The x-intercept is still absent.

18: Minimum 1 at \(x=3\); no maximum. As \(x\to\infty\), \(f(x)\to\infty\); as \(x\to3^+\), \(f(x)\to1\). There is no leftward infinite end of this domain.

19: \(f(x)=\log_2x-1\). No maximum or minimum. Vertical asymptote \(x=0\); as \(x\to0^+\), \(f(x)\to-\infty\); as \(x\to\infty\), \(f(x)\to\infty\). Plot \((\frac12,-2),(1,-1),(2,0),(4,1)\), drawing an increasing logarithmic curve only for positive inputs.

20: Domain \(\mathbb R\); range \([-5,\infty)\). Increasing on \((-2,0)\) and \((3,\infty)\); decreasing on \((-\infty,-2)\) and \((0,3)\). The y-intercept is \((0,4)\). Visible x-intercepts are \((-2,0)\) and \((2,0)\). Extending the straight right branch through the grid points \((3,-5)\) and \((9,-2)\) gives a further intercept \((13,0)\), outside the displayed window. Graph coordinates are read approximately.

The absolute minimum is −5 at \(x=3\); no absolute maximum. There is also a local minimum at \((-2,0)\) and a local maximum at \((0,4)\). Both ends rise toward infinity.

21: The supplied graph shows separate dots, with no arrows indicating additional terms. Its domain is \(\{-1,0,1,2,3,4,5,6\}\). Reading the halving pattern gives outputs \(16,8,4,2,1,\frac12,\frac14,\frac18\), so those eight values form the range. The last small outputs are inferred from that pattern because the graph’s scale cannot resolve them precisely. Outputs decrease across the listed inputs; none increase. The y-intercept is \((0,8)\); there is no x-intercept. Maximum 16 at −1; minimum \(\frac18\) at 6 under the halving interpretation. An infinite-end limit is not part of the finite graph as drawn.
