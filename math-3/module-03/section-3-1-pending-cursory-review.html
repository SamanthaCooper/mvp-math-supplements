Daily amounts and accumulated totals are different functions

The input \(n\) is the day number. A daily amount counts push-ups on that day alone. An accumulated total counts all push-ups from day 1 through day \(n\). For example, 5 push-ups on day 2 brings last year’s total to \(3+5=8\).

A recursive rule gives a starting value and tells you how to get the next value from the current one. An explicit rule lets you calculate a value directly from its input. Both describe the same pattern.

Worked solution — Main 1: last year’s daily amount

Original problem: Write recursive and explicit equations for the daily push-ups, and connect them to the packet’s bar graph and table.

Answers: The daily amounts are \(3,5,7,9,11,\ldots\). Each new bar is two blocks taller, and each new table value is 2 greater.

Recursive:

\[f(1)=3,\qquad f(n+1)=f(n)+2\quad(1\le n<31).\]

Explicit:

\[f(n)=3+2(n-1)=2n+1.\]

Starting at 3, there are \(n-1\) increases of 2 before day \(n\). This is a linear rule: its change per day is constant.

Worked solution — Main 2: last year’s accumulated total

Original problem: Write recursive and explicit equations for the accumulated total by day \(n\).

Answers: Add the next day’s amount to the current total.

Recursive:

\[g(1)=3,\qquad g(n+1)=g(n)+[2(n+1)+1]\quad(1\le n<31).\]

Explicit:

\[g(n)=n(n+2)=n^2+2n.\]

To see the explicit rule, write the \(n\) daily amounts in increasing order, then underneath in reverse order. Every pair adds to \(3+(2n+1)=2n+4\). There are \(n\) pairs, and the two rows contain twice the total:

\[2g(n)=n(2n+4),\qquad g(n)=n(n+2).\]

For day 4, \(g(4)=4(6)=24\), agreeing with \(3+5+7+9\). The total is quadratic even though the daily amount is linear.

This year: yesterday’s kind of total becomes today’s daily amount

In your MVP packetMain 3–4 · printed p. 2
Packet wording — Main Part II, printed p. 2

Use the table to interpret the plan: this year’s amount on day \(n\) equals last year’s total through day \(n\), so \(m(n)=g(n)\). It does not mean repeating last year’s full-month total every day.

Worked solution — Main 3–4

Original problem 3: Find this year’s day-four amount, explain it, and give a recursive rule for this year’s accumulated total.

Answers: \(m(4)=g(4)=24\) push-ups on day 4. This year’s daily rule is \(m(n)=n^2+2n\). Its accumulated total is \(T(n)\):

\[T(1)=3,\qquad T(n+1)=T(n)+[(n+1)^2+2(n+1)]\quad(1\le n<31).\]

Original problem 4: Find this year’s total through day 4.

Answer: \(T(4)=3+8+15+24=50\) push-ups. The 24 on day 4 is only the newest contribution to that total.

Successive differences reveal the degree

In your MVP packetMain 5–7 · printed p. 3

A first difference is the next output minus the current output. A second difference is the difference between consecutive first differences; continue the same way for third differences. Always check that the inputs are equally spaced.

For one-day steps, a first difference is also the average change in push-ups per day over that step. In this lesson, “rate of change” refers to these step-by-step changes.

A working definition of polynomial

A polynomial is a sum of constant multiples of whole-number powers of the variable, such as \(2n+1\), \(n^2+2n\), or \(n^3+3n^2+n\). The highest power with a nonzero coefficient is its degree. Degree 1 is linear, degree 2 is quadratic, and degree 3 is cubic. A constant nonzero polynomial has degree 0.

At equally spaced inputs, a degree-\(d\) polynomial has constant nonzero \(d\)th differences. Thus linear → constant first differences; quadratic → constant second differences; cubic → constant third differences.

Worked solution — Main 5–6: make and test a conjecture

Original problem 5: Conjecture the type of this year’s total without finding its explicit equation.

Answer: Cubic. Its first differences are quadratic daily amounts; their differences are linear, and one further set of differences is constant.

Original problem 6: Compare its rate of change with those in Main 1 and 2.

Answer: \(f\) changes by the constant 2; \(g\) changes by a linear daily amount; \(T\) changes by a quadratic daily amount.

Worked solution — Main 7: why the pattern continues

Original problem: Test and justify the conjecture, then generalize to polynomial functions.

Worked solution: The daily amount is \(m(n)=n^2+2n\). Its next-day change is

\[m(n+1)-m(n)=(n+1)^2+2(n+1)-(n^2+2n)=2n+3.\]

The next difference of \(2n+3\) is always 2. Therefore the third differences of \(T\) stay equal to 2, rather than merely matching in the first few rows.

One explicit cubic rule is

\[T(n)=\frac{n(n+1)(2n+7)}6=\frac13n^3+\frac32n^2+\frac76n.\]

You can verify it without a new summation formula: it starts at \(T(1)=3\), and

\[T(n+1)-T(n)=(n+1)^2+2(n+1)=m(n+1).\]

A rule with the correct starting value and the correct added amount at every step gives the same accumulated total for every day.

Generalization: Taking a one-step difference of a polynomial of positive degree lowers its degree by 1: the highest powers cancel when you subtract the old value from the next one. Repeating eventually gives a nonzero constant difference. Accumulating the values of a nonzero degree-\(d\) polynomial reverses this process, producing a polynomial total of degree \(d+1\). Scott’s sequence of degrees is \(1\to2\to3\).

This means accumulating successive values, as in \(3+5+\cdots+(2n+1)\). It is different from adding two function rules at the same input.

2. Ready — Compare expressions

In your MVP packetReady 1–9 · printed p. 4

Adding the same quantity or multiplying by the same positive number preserves an inequality. When \(0<x<1\), multiplying a positive quantity by \(x\) makes it smaller: \(x^2<x\). Taking the positive square root moves such a number upward: \(\sqrt{x}>x\). For \(x>1\), the order is \(\sqrt{x}<x<x^2<x^3\).

For example, if \(x=\frac14\), then \(x^2=\frac1{16}\) and \(\sqrt{x}=\frac12\). Use the interval specified in the packet, then try the problems before checking.

3. Set — Classify functions from tables

In your MVP packetSet 10–16 · printed p. 4

Use successive differences for polynomial patterns. A basic exponential pattern instead multiplies by the same positive factor for each equal input step. A vertical shift can hide that ratio in the outputs; subtracting the shift reveals it, or check whether first differences have a constant ratio.

Original example — an exponential with a vertical shift

Suppose outputs at consecutive inputs are \(8,11,17,29\). The first differences \(3,6,12\) double. Subtracting 5 from the outputs gives \(3,6,12,24\), which also double. A model starting at input 1 is \(y=3\cdot2^{x-1}+5\).

Use the simple continuing patterns indicated by the packet’s tables. Give a classification and supporting evidence; an explicit formula is optional. Try each table before opening the key.

4. Go — Long division and factors

In your MVP packetGo 17–28 · printed p. 5

In whole-number division, the dividend is the number being divided, and the divisor is the number you divide by. The whole-number quotient and remainder satisfy

\[\text{dividend}=(\text{divisor})(\text{quotient})+\text{remainder}.\]

The remainder must be at least 0 and smaller than the positive divisor. A whole number is a factor of another when division leaves remainder 0.

Original worked example — \(1{,}247\div23\)
  1. 23 goes into 124 five times: \(5(23)=115\). Subtract to get 9.
  2. Bring down the 7 to get 97. 23 goes into 97 four times: \(4(23)=92\). Subtract to get 5.

The quotient is 54 remainder 5. Check: \(23(54)+5=1{,}247\). Since the remainder is not zero, 23 is not a factor of 1,247.

Packet values — Go 21 and 23, printed p. 5

Problem 21 divides 14,857 by 22, while problem 23 asks whether 22 is a factor of 14,587. They are different numbers. Use each printed value; both leave nonzero remainders, but the quotients and remainders differ.

Do the divisions without a calculator as requested, then check both your quotient and remainder. The key also gives multiplication checks for each factor question.

Teacher/parent review of the source packet

Go 21 and 23, printed p. 5: the dividend changes from 14,857 to 14,587. This breaks the apparent division/factor pairing. Both “not a factor” conclusions are correct, but the supporting arithmetic differs. If the intent is a paired check, make the two dividends agree; the packet does not establish which value was intended.

Main Part II, printed p. 2: the prose says “for the month” where the table indicates a running total through the corresponding day. Suggested wording: “On day \(n\) this year, Scott does as many push-ups as he had done in total through day \(n\) last year.” The lesson follows that table.

The lesson uses finite differences over equal input steps for “rate of change.” It does not require an instantaneous rate or calculus. No other substantial mathematical issue was identified in Section 3.1.
