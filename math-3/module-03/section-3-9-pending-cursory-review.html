Part I — The leading term controls the far ends

End behavior describes what outputs do as inputs move far to the left or right. The notation \(x\to\infty\) means x grows without bound; infinity is not an input to substitute. In a nonconstant polynomial, the leading term has the highest power with a nonzero coefficient. Its power and coefficient determine end behavior.

For example,

\[3x^4-2x+5=x^4\left(3-\frac2{x^3}+\frac5{x^4}\right).\]

For large positive or negative x, the fractions approach zero, so the factor in parentheses approaches 3. Thus the ends behave like \(3x^4\). Lower terms can change the middle of a graph substantially without changing its far ends.

Part I 1 sample comparison: Linear, quadratic and cubic functions are degree 1, 2 and 3 polynomials, with constant first, second and third differences on equal input steps. Use \(l(x)=x^7\) for the supplied seventh-power example. The center of the organizer labels it p, but p is already \(x^3+x^2-4\) in the function list.

Part I 4 answer: For sufficiently large positive x, the greatest-to-least order is

\[f>l>q>r>h>m>p>g>k>n.\]

Here f means \(f(x)\), and similarly for the other labels. All ten outputs are eventually positive, so place them in Quadrant I. Part I 5: Compare leading powers and coefficients. The growth exponential \(2^x\) eventually exceeds every fixed polynomial; \((\frac12)^x\) approaches zero.

Part I 6 answer: For sufficiently large negative x,

\[n>q>m>g>f>k>p>r>h>l.\]

The first five are positive (Quadrant II); the last five are negative (Quadrant III). Positive even-power leading terms rise on the left. Positive odd-power terms fall. Compare r and h carefully: \(r(x)-h(x)=4x^2-1\), which is positive far from zero.

Part I 7: For \(b>1\), \(b^x\to0\) on the left and grows without bound on the right. For \(0<b<1\), the directions reverse. Plots not shown for the requested technology tests. Sample tests: graph \(x^4-2x\), \(-x^3+1\), \(3^x\), and \((\frac13)^x\), then compare their far ends with these rules. Part I 8: Negating only the leading coefficient reverses both far-end directions.

The order statements concern sufficiently distant finite inputs. Several outputs may all tend to infinity, but that does not make their finite values equal.

Domain correction — Part II 6, printed p. 61

The real function \(\log_2x\) is defined only for \(x>0\), so the printed request at \(x\to-\infty\) has no real-domain answer. Replace that left-end request with \(x\to0^+\); then \(\log_2x\to-\infty\).

Part II 9 answer: Quadratic ends point the same way, so end behavior alone does not determine whether there are two distinct real roots, one double real root, or a nonreal pair. A real cubic has opposite far-end signs and its continuous graph must meet the x-axis at least once. It has three complex roots counted with multiplicity: either three real roots, or one real and a conjugate nonreal pair. Repeated real roots may reduce the number of distinct intercepts.

Part III — Even and odd describe symmetry

In your MVP packetSection 3.9 · Main Part III 1–9 · printed pp. 62–65

These names concern a function’s symmetry, not just the parity of its degree. The domain must include −x whenever it includes x.

Even: \(f(-x)=f(x)\) for every input. The graph mirrors across the y-axis: \((x,y)\) pairs with \((-x,y)\).

Odd: \(f(-x)=-f(x)\) for every input. A half-turn about the origin leaves the graph unchanged: \((x,y)\) pairs with \((-x,-y)\).

Procedure: Substitute −x for every x, simplify, then compare with both \(f(x)\) and \(-f(x)\). If neither identity holds, the function is neither. A single matching pair of outputs illustrates symmetry but cannot prove the identity for all inputs.

Original example — an even degree need not make an even function

Let \(F(x)=x^2+x\). Then \(F(-x)=x^2-x\), which is neither \(F(x)\) nor \(-F(x)=-x^2-x\). Thus F has even degree but is neither even nor odd. Conversely, \(G(x)=x^3+3x\) gives \(G(-x)=-x^3-3x=-G(x)\), so G is odd.

A polynomial is even exactly when its nonzero terms have even powers (including a constant). The substitution test follows because even powers keep their sign while odd powers reverse it.
