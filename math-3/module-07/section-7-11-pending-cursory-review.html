In your MVP packetMain 1–12 · pages 70–76

A complex number can be described by length and angle

In \(z=a+bi\), \(i^2=-1\), and the corresponding point is \((a,b)\). Its modulus is its distance from the origin, \(r=|z|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\). An argument is an angle \(\theta\) from the positive real axis to that point. Arguments differing by a full turn describe the same nonzero number.

\[z=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta).\]

The real part is \(r\cos\theta\) and the imaginary coefficient is \(r\sin\theta\). For Main 1–2, \(2+5i\) has \(r=\sqrt{29}\) and \(\theta=\arctan(5/2)\approx68.2^\circ\). At zero, the modulus is 0 and no unique direction is defined.

Multiplication stretches and rotates

Multiplying two polar forms multiplies their moduli and adds their angles. To see why, expand the binomials, use \(i^2=-1\), then group the real and imaginary parts:

\[z_1z_2=r_1r_2[(\cos\theta_1\cos\theta_2-\sin\theta_1\sin\theta_2)+i(\sin\theta_1\cos\theta_2+\cos\theta_1\sin\theta_2)].\]

The addition identities combine those parts into cosine and sine of \(\theta_1+\theta_2\). Division reverses that process: divide the moduli and subtract the angles, with a nonzero divisor.

Worked example — multiplication and division

Original problem (additional example): Multiply \(\sqrt2(\cos45^\circ+i\sin45^\circ)=1+i\) by \(2(\cos90^\circ+i\sin90^\circ)=2i\).

Worked solution: The product has modulus \(2\sqrt2\) and angle 135°:

\[2\sqrt2(\cos135^\circ+i\sin135^\circ)=-2+2i.\]

Direct multiplication gives \((1+i)(2i)=2i+2i^2=-2+2i\), confirming the result. Dividing the product by \(2i\) divides its modulus by 2 and subtracts 90°, recovering \(1+i\).

Powers: multiply the angle by the exponent

Repeated multiplication gives De Moivre’s theorem, for positive integers \(n\):

\[[r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)]^n=r^n(\cos(n\theta)+i\sin(n\theta)).\]

For example, cubing \(1+\sqrt3i\) gives modulus \(2^3=8\) and angle \(3(60^\circ)=180^\circ\), so the result is −8. Each additional multiplication doubles the distance from the origin and rotates another 60°. This explains the exponential growth in Main 9–10.

Roots: include every full-turn choice before dividing

To solve \(w^n=z\) for nonzero \(z=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)\), the modulus of \(w\) is \(r^{1/n}\), and its angle must satisfy \(n\phi=\theta+2k\pi\). Thus the distinct roots are

\[w_k=r^{1/n}\left[\cos\left(\frac{\theta+2k\pi}{n}\right)+i\sin\left(\frac{\theta+2k\pi}{n}\right)\right],\quad k=0,1,\ldots,n-1.\]

In degrees, replace \(2k\pi\) by \(360^\circ k\). The roots are equally spaced by \(2\pi/n\); for zero, the only distinct root is zero.

Main “Idea #3,” page 76 — incorrect root angles

The packet prints \(k\theta/n\) as the angle in its root formula. The correct angle is \((\theta+360^\circ k)/n\) in degrees, or \((\theta+2\pi k)/n\) in radians. You must add whole turns before dividing by \(n\). Multiplying \(\theta\) by \(k\) can produce numbers whose nth powers are not the original number. For example, the cube roots of a number at 90° must have angles 30°,150°,270°; angles 30°,60°,90° do not work.

Worked example — Main 11–12: cube roots of 64

Original problem: Find all complex solutions to \(w^3=64\).

Worked solution: Use \(r=64,\theta=0,n=3\). The modulus is 4, and the angles are 0°,120°,240°. The roots are

\[4,\qquad -2+2\sqrt3i,\qquad -2-2\sqrt3i.\]

Cubing each triples its angle to a full-turn multiple and cubes its modulus to 64. The symbol \(\sqrt[3]{64}\) conventionally denotes the real value 4; asking for all solutions of \(w^3=64\) includes the two nonreal roots.
