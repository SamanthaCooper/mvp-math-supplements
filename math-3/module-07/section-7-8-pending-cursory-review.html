Why an inverse trig function selects just one angle

Sine repeats, so many angles have the same sine. To make its inverse a function, restrict sine to an interval where each output occurs once. The inverse returns the angle in that chosen interval, called the principal value.

InverseInput domainOutput angles
\(\arcsin x\)\(-1\le x\le1\)\(-\pi/2\le y\le\pi/2\)
\(\arccos x\)\(-1\le x\le1\)\(0\le y\le\pi\)
\(\arctan x\)All real numbers\(-\pi/2<y<\pi/2\)

The notation \(\sin^{-1}x\) also means \(\arcsin x\); it does not mean \(1/\sin x\). For tangent the endpoint angles are excluded because tangent is undefined there.

Worked example — Main 3: one inverse value, many equation solutions

Original problem: Given \(\arcsin(0.75)\approx0.848\), find other angles whose sine is 0.75.

Worked solution: Put \(\alpha=\arcsin(0.75)\). The other angle in the first turn is \(\pi-\alpha\approx2.294\). Add or subtract full turns to get more:

\[\theta=\alpha+2k\pi\quad\text{or}\quad\theta=\pi-\alpha+2k\pi.\]

Examples include about −5.435, −3.990, 2.294 and 7.131 radians. Keep full calculator precision until the final rounding. The inverse function itself still has just one output: \(\alpha\).

Main 8 asks for \(\arcsin(-1/2)\). Its answer is \(-\pi/6\), inside the principal range, even though \(7\pi/6\) and \(11\pi/6\) also solve the corresponding sine equation.

Do two clues locate a point?

An angle and a positive distance from the origin determine one point. An angle and a quadrant alone can leave many points along a ray. Conflicting clues can leave none. The classroom card activity depends on the actual pair drawn; record the clues and test whether they select one, several, or no locations. The card decks are not reproduced in the student pages, so there is no single checking key for all games.
